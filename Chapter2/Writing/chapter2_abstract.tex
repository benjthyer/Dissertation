U-Th/He geochronology of goethite (FeOOH) is a powerful method to
quantitatively date weathering and erosion processes on Earth's surface;
however, precise interpretation of goethite U-Th/He dates is challenging
because uncertainty persists regarding the mineralogical and environmental
conditions under which radiogenic helium is retained and how it may be lost over
time. Standard vacuum step heating experiments have been successfully
used on a variety of mineral phases to characterize helium migration,
but the results of such experiments on goethite predominantly record 
dehydroxylation-induced helium loss, raising questions about their relevance to
helium retention and loss in nature \citep{farley2024}. Observations
that thermal decomposition of goethite is greatly suppressed in the presence
that thermal decomposition of goethite is greatly suppressed in the presence
of high water pressure led us to develop a new method to conduct helium
step heat experiments under water-saturated conditions up to 300°C within 
standard experimental setups. Using this method, we report
results from the first helium step heat experiment of proton-irradiated
goethite under hydrothermal conditions. Irradiated goethite sample
Win-06-01b of the Flinders Ranges, South Australia and distilled
water were encapsulated in a pure silica glass ampoule and were
subjected to multiple prograde and retrograde thermal cycles over a
temperature range of 170°C to 250°C under water vapor saturated
conditions with step durations ranging from 2 hours to over a week. The results show apparent helium diffusivities approximately
seven log units lower than those observed in vacuum, converging to 
a reproducible linear array on an Arrhenius plot with an activation energy of \ChTwoEa
± \ChTwoEaErr kJ/mol and a closure temperature of \ChTwoTc ± \ChTwoTcErr °C at a cooling rate of 10°C/Ma.
These results are consistent with a volume-diffusion helium loss
mechanism and show high helium retentivity for natural goethite 
that extrapolates to near-quantitative helium retention at
surface conditions over billion-year timescales. Reanalysis of published vacuum step heats of the same sample
shows that they record first-order conversion to hematite, whereas the hydrothermal release
records diffusion. Thermodynamic and
nucleation-kinetic modeling calibrated on vacuum step heat experiments on the same sample
explains this difference and shows that conducting step heats with this method is expected to
slow dehydroxylation of Win-06-01b by many orders of magnitude, effectively suppressing dehydroxylation on 
experimental timescales. This analysis applies only to Win-06-01b, and can only be extended to other 
goethite samples for which kinetic data or vacuum step heat data are available due to the diversity and complexity 
of goethite morphologies and grain sizes. The method
presented in this work can be systematically applied to experimentally
investigate effects of crystallite size, chemical composition, and
radiation damage to refine these results and to assess the applicability
of existing vacuum step heat data to helium retention and loss in
goethite in nature.
