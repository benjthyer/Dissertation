\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Goethite (FeOOH) crystallization is datable only by U-Th/He
geochronology \citep{shustervasconcelos2005,farley2024}. Goethite is a
powerful chronometer for dating surface processes such as weathering and
ferruginization directly: laterite and duricrust formation and
recrystallization \citep{monteiro2014,allard2018,ansart2022}, and the
ferruginization of organic matter and paleosols \citep{heim2006,yappshuster2011,vasconcelos2013,danisik2013,hofmann2017}.

Interpreting a goethite U-Th/He date requires knowing the conditions
under which the mineral retains radiogenic helium. As with any
radiochronometer, meaningful geological interpretation of a measured age
depends on knowing when the system is open to loss or gain of parent or
daughter nuclides. For goethite, step heats of proton-irradiated,
crystalline samples are used to reveal the degree of partial helium loss
by the 4He/3He method \citep{shustervasconcelos2005}, and suggest that
helium loss fractions in nature are always non-zero but modest, ranging
from 2 to 30\% \citep{shustervasconcelos2005,heim2006,hofmann2017,deng2017}.
Although one study attempts to explain the variable loss principally as a
result of variable radiation damage \citep{bassal2022}, the effects of
temperature, crystallite size, and chemical composition have not been
determined experimentally and remain unresolved, because goethite
decomposes to hematite (Fe\textsubscript{2}O\textsubscript{3}) in the step
heat experiments used to characterize it \citep{farley2024}.

Laboratory experiments of helium migration under controlled conditions
have proven extremely useful in understanding the mechanisms and rates
of helium migration in geological materials and have produced sensible
results that are predominantly explainable by thermally activated volume
diffusion \citep{reinersfarley1999,farley2000,farley2007,cherniakwatson2011,farley2018}, albeit variably complicated by
the effects of radiation damage and annealing \citep{shuster2006,guenthner2013}. This approach has also been leveraged for
goethite; however, some complications arise when applying this approach
to goethite. Above surface temperatures, goethite decomposes to hematite
and water, often along crystallite elongation directions \citep{goss1987}.
\citet{farley2024} measured evolved water by capacitance manometry during
vacuum step heats and found helium and water release to be tightly
correlated even below 200\textdegree C, indicating that decomposition,
rather than helium migration through an unchanging crystal, governs the
release. Vacuum step-heat results for goethite therefore need not describe
helium behavior in nature.

Still, existing helium step heat measurements of goethite in vacuum
likely remain informative to an extent, as goethite dehydration kinetics
are highly influenced by some of the same factors that could be
responsible for variable helium retention in natural goethite, like
variable grain size \citep{navrotsky2008,guobarnard2011,goss1987}, crystallinity \citep{schwertmann1985} or substitution of
aluminum or other cations within the goethite structure \citep{majzlan2003,bassal2022}. For example, \citet{shustervasconcelos2005} initially
conducted vacuum step-heating experiments on two vitreous,
polycrystalline goethite samples, in which uniformly distributed 3He was
introduced by proton irradiation for comparison with radiogenic 4He that
was partially lost in nature. The results of \citet{shustervasconcelos2005} could
be well-fit with a model of two distinct diffusion domains or migration
mechanisms with significant differences in helium retention under
surface conditions, e.g. crystallite interiors and intergranular space.
Although these results may have been impacted by dehydration in vacuum,
certain aspects may remain applicable to helium loss in goethite in
nature if helium loss in nature and the laboratory advance through the
samples with similar geometries. One important point is that if
laboratory experiments degas domains in order of their helium
retentivity in nature, even if the rates of loss are affected by
goethite dehydration, then a \textsuperscript{4}He/\textsuperscript{3}He
experiment in vacuum can still be used to measure helium loss \citep{shustervasconcelos2005,vasconcelos2013}.

An additional complexity is that natural goethite almost always occurs
as aggregates of minute crystallites with cross-sections smaller than 1
\textmu m in nature. Neglecting to account for multiple diffusion domains can
lead to inaccurate determinations of activation energy
(E\textsubscript{a}) and frequency factor
(D\textsubscript{o}/a\textsuperscript{2}) for helium diffusion \citep{farley2018}. Detailed step-heating with temperature cycling, as has been done
for hematite \citep{farleyflowers2012,farley2018}, is needed to
characterize domain size and activation energy for polycrystalline
samples.

Molecular simulations indicate that helium diffusion in perfectly
crystalline goethite should be highly anisotropic, essentially reducing
to 1-dimensional diffusion along the b-axis (typically the long axis of
the polycrystalline aggregate) that would amount to near-instantaneous
helium escape from goethite \citep{bassal2022}, which is clearly
contradicted by observed (U--Th)/He dates as old as \textasciitilde275 Ma
\citep{waltenberg2012} and dates that are concordant with
\textsuperscript{40}Ar/\textsuperscript{39}Ar ages of other phases
\citep{vasconcelos2013}. However, the models presented by \citet{bassal2022} suggest that crystallographic defects caused by radiation
damage could block these channels, thereby reducing He loss. While these
simulations are informative, they alone are insufficient for drawing
conclusions about the mineralogical and environmental conditions needed
for accurately dating the crystallization of goethite with the
(U--Th)/He method and require experimental verification.

Goethite dehydration is suppressed at high water pressure \citep{langmuir1971,navrotsky2008}, so a step
heat conducted under hydrothermal conditions may interrogate helium loss
rather than decomposition kinetics. Until now, no studies have reported
helium step-heating results for goethite under hydrothermal (high
pH\textsubscript{2}O) conditions, which could help stabilize goethite or
slow its decomposition to illuminate the processes relevant to helium loss
in natural settings. If
experimental conditions can be controlled to slow the decomposition of
goethite such that the rate of natural helium loss in goethite greatly
exceeds the rate of goethite decomposition into hematite and water, it
would open up an experimental approach to systematically assess how
factors like crystallite size, temperature, radiation damage, and
chemical composition could affect helium mobility and retentivity in
nature.

Early trials of the method were encouraging (\S\ref{sem-study-of-thermally-treated-polycrystalline-goethite}).
SEM imagery taken of thermally treated natural goethites in vacuum and
open air showed the development of contraction cracks on the outside and
throughout the interior of goethite grains heat treated in vacuum and in
open air (Figs.~\ref{fig:ch2-sem-control-vacuum},~\ref{fig:ch2-sem-vacuum-interior}), while goethite heat-treated when
encapsulated with water or gypsum did not form cracks (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-sem-buffered}).
Goethite heat treated in open air turned red, whereas goethite heat
treated in vacuum remained black.

We test this premise with the first helium step heat experiment on goethite under
water-saturated conditions, and compare it with published vacuum step heats of the same sample
(\S\ref{sec:gt_vacuum}--\ref{sec:gt_hydro}). A thermodynamic and kinetic analysis of goethite
dehydroxylation then explains why water has this effect
(\S\ref{sec:gt_thermo_methods}--\ref{sec:gt_nucleation}).

\section{Experimental Methods}\label{ch2-experimental-methods}

This study builds upon previous helium step heat studies of the dense
colloform goethite sample Win-06-01b (occasionally referred to as
Win0601b) collected within float at the Flinders Ranges in South
Australia \citep{waltenberg2012,monteiro2017}. It yields one of the
oldest known goethite U-Th/He ages: 180-135 Ma \citep{waltenberg2012}.
% TODO(BJT): the thermal-history modeling uses a He age range of 130--180 Ma
% (\S\ref{sec:gt_mdd_methods}); reconcile with the 180-135 Ma quoted here.
To
produce a homogeneous distribution of 3He, Win-06-01b was irradiated
with 220 MeV protons for 24 hours at the Francis H. Burr Proton Therapy
Center, Massachusetts General Hospital, following standard procedures
\citep{shuster2005} and subsequently was subjected to helium step
heat measurements in vacuum by \citet{monteiro2017} and \citet{farley2024}.
In their step heat experiment of Win-06-01b, \citet{farley2024}
additionally measured evolved water using capacitance manometry to show
that helium and water release were tightly correlated for goethite step
heats in vacuum. Although they used subtly different heating schedules,
the results of \citet{farley2024} clearly agree well with \citet{monteiro2017}.

In this study, an aliquot of the same irradiated Win-06-01b goethite
used by \citet{monteiro2017} and \citet{farley2024} was encapsulated with
MilliQ water within a silica glass tube. Silica glass was chosen over
other laboratory glasses such as borosilicate because it is the most
permeable glass type to helium \citep{norton1953,altemose1961}. To
prepare the silica glass tube, 10 cm long segments of pure silica glass
tubes with an outer diameter of 4 mm and an inner diameter of 3 mm were
submerged and sonicated within acetone, ethanol, then MilliQ water baths
in sequence, and then were dried overnight in a 100°C oven. The glass
tubes were hand-pulled into thinner pipettes using a natural gas and
oxygen glass-welding torch and the pipette ends were fused closed. After
the glass had cooled, 1.18 mg of irradiated Win-06-01b goethite blades
less than 2 mm in length were carefully transferred to the bottom of the
tube in the presence of an ionizing fan. Less than a microliter of
distilled water was transferred to the sealed pipette using a thin glass
capillary, submerging the goethite, after which the tube was gently
tapped to liberate the bubbles and ensure that the water and goethite
were efficiently packed at the bottom of the pipette. To seal the
pipette, the contents of the ampoule were flash-frozen in liquid
nitrogen and transferred to a saltwater-ice bath at a temperature of
-10°C. While the pipette end of the tube remained frozen in the
saltwater-ice bath, the top was interfaced with a vacuum line and the
tube was pumped to a pressure of \textless0.1 torr. The advantage of
this approach was that water would remain solid within the tube, only
slowly sublimating to vacuum while atmospheric gases would be readily
pumped away. While it was desirable to remove atmospheric gases to
reduce experimental complexity by removing potentially reactive gases
like oxygen, the primary motivation for pumping the tube to rough vacuum
was to aid the sealing process. When the tube contents were under
vacuum, fused glass would deform inwards and self-seal, greatly
improving the ease and quality of the seal while requiring far less heat
input from the torch. After an acceptably low pressure was attained, the
tube was closed to the vacuum line and the saltwater-ice bath was
quickly replaced with a liquid nitrogen bath, ensuring that no ice was
melted. The liquid nitrogen bath was preferable during sealing as it
more effectively protected the goethite and ice from the high
temperatures of the glass-welding torch. A fine-flamed welding torch was
used to seal the top of the ampoule, which dropped into the liquid
nitrogen bath and was retrieved by passing the liquid nitrogen through a
sieve. Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-ampoule-post} shows the sealed ampoule after the completion of the
step heats with all of the original water remaining, a testament to the
quality of the ampoule's seal, which had sustained a massive pressure
gradient of over 11 orders of magnitude across the ampoule walls, with
an inner vapor pressure of about 3$\times$10\textsuperscript{4} torr
(\textasciitilde40 bars: \citet{haar1984}) against the
\textasciitilde10\textsuperscript{-7} torr ultra-high vacuum of the lamp
cell while the ampoule was held at 250°C for over a week.

The sealed ampoule, now referred to as HT-Win-06-01b, was placed in a
copper foil packet and mounted to a K-type thermocouple within an
ultra-high vacuum projector lamp heating cell described in more detail
by \citet{farley1999}. Before the experiment began, the ampoule was
loaded in the lamp cell, pumped to high vacuum, and was held at 70°C for
4 days to degas atmospheric helium entrained within the ampoule.

HT-Win-06-01b was subjected to multiple prograde-retrograde heating
cycles to investigate possible MDD behavior. Step durations varied from
2 hours to about a week; the total experiment was conducted over about
40 days and included experimental steps ranging from 170-250°C. We
elected not to heat the sample hotter because high temperatures
generated very high vapor pressures. In early trials, we found that
similar ampoules most often withstood the \textasciitilde86 bar vapor
pressures associated with liquid water saturation at 300°C \citep{haar1984}, but they would explode when further heated to temperatures beyond
315°C.

Experimental temperature records show \textasciitilde1°C amplitude
oscillations around the target temperature. While we cannot exclude the
possibility that sustained temperature gradients were present throughout
the sample due to the use of the comparatively large \textasciitilde1 mm
thick ampoule, we are confident that the conclusions of this work are
robust. The ampoule was in direct contact with the thermocouple junction
and was in front of the thermocouple junction with respect to the projector
lamp heating source, suggesting that if there were a temperature
discrepancy, the sample would have been hotter than the recorded
temperature, which would not affect the conclusions of this work.
Additionally, during early trials of this method for helium step heats,
silica-glass encapsulated Durango apatite had been subjected to step
heats at Caltech and gave corrected 4He yields indistinguishable from those of
unencapsulated Durango apatite, suggesting that the temperature recorded
by the thermocouple for our experimental setup is sufficiently accurate.

Helium isotope measurements of evolved gases from hydrothermal step
heats of glass-encapsulated irradiated HT-Win-06-01b goethite were
acquired at Caltech on a Thermo Fisher
Helix-MC-Plus\textsuperscript{10K} mass spectrometer in peak hopping
mode from January 31\textsuperscript{st}, 2024 to March
13\textsuperscript{th}, 2024. Helium was purified by exposing evolved
gases to a charcoal trap held in liquid nitrogen, a titanium sponge
getter, then a SAES NP10 getter before freely expanding into the
Helix-MC-Plus\textsuperscript{10K}. 3He was measured with a
pulse-counting electron multiplier, while 4He was detected on a Faraday
detector. The majority of the analysis time was spent measuring 3He.
Every few hours, the background was mass-scanned to set the 3He and 4He
measure points; each was taken to be a constant mass offset from the
background HD\textsuperscript{+} peak. These mass offsets are precisely
measured when tuning the Helix-MC-Plus\textsuperscript{10K} for helium
isotope analysis. Months of helium measurements with the
Helix-MC-Plus\textsuperscript{10K} at Caltech have shown this approach to
be reliable, reproducible, and accurate for helium isotope analysis.
Additionally, an electron multiplier monitored atomic mass 3.5 to detect
4He overspray, which was never significant during the experiment.
Measurements of high 3He MM gas standard were analyzed every 8 hours
(totaling 116 measurements) to track instrumental drift of 3He and 4He
sensitivities and to quantify internal reproducibility.

A cold blank was measured at the beginning of the experiment, showing a
negligible 3He blank and a 4He blank signal
corresponding to about 1.25 pccSTP, which was similar to 4He values
attained during low temperature permeation step measurements. Permeation
steps were thus assumed to measure shifting 4He blanks during the
experiment, and the entire blank correction was added to all 4He
uncertainties.

For the final step, the HT-Win-06-01b ampoule was broken and dried
overnight in a 50°C oven, then completely degassed by heating to
\textasciitilde1100 °C in a resistance furnace. The final step
measurement was conducted on a Thermo Fisher Helix SFT mass spectrometer. A
hot furnace blank subjected to the same volume splits as the final
HT-Win-06-01b measurement gave a 3He blank of 6.8 fccSTP and a 4He blank
of 0.31 nccSTP. This blank correction was less than 1\% of the measured
4He and negligible for 3He. A re-extract of HT-Win-06-01b immediately
following the measurement yielded above-blank 3He and 4He signals, which
were added to the total measurement, although both constituted less than
1\% of the final step yield. Sensitivity was calculated by measuring a
3He-enriched air standard with known 3He and 4He contents immediately
before the measurement.

Helium released by the sample reaches the mass spectrometer only after permeating the ampoule
wall, so each measured step yield lags the true release. Step yields were corrected for this
permeation lag with the bounding method of Chapter~\ref{ch:apparatus} (\S\ref{bounding-method}),
which gives lower-bound, central and upper-bound values for every step
(Tables~\ref{tab:ch2-distilled-3he} and~\ref{tab:ch2-distilled-4he}). The correction and the
ampoule permeabilities measured during the experiment are described in the Supplementary
Information (\S\ref{sec:ch2-si-permeation}), together with the diffusivity inversions, the
isotope-ratio uncertainties and the kinetic analysis of the published vacuum step heats
(\S\ref{ch2-analytical-methods}).

\subsection{Kinetic and Diffusive Reductions of Step-Heat Data}\label{sec:gt_kin_methods}

We reanalyzed the published vacuum step heats of irradiated Win-06-01b by \citet{farley2024} (hereafter \emph{KAF}) and
\citet{monteiro2017} (hereafter \emph{HDM}), together with the water-saturated step heat of this study, by reducing every step
both as a first-order conversion rate constant and as an apparent diffusivity.
For the kinetic reduction,
\textsuperscript{3}He is assumed to be immediately released from the goethite lattice upon conversion to hematite, except for a fraction $\phi$ 
that is inherited by the hematite product. The fraction of helium released in each step is then $F_i = (1-\phi)\,\alpha_i$, where $\alpha_i$ 
is the fraction of goethite converted in step $i$.

We describe the kinetics of goethite dehydroxylation with a first-order rate law,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\alpha}{dt} = k(T)\,(1-\alpha),
  \qquad
  k(T) = A\exp\!\left(-\frac{E_a}{RT}\right),
  \label{eq:gt_F1}
\end{equation}

For a schedule of isothermal steps of duration $\Delta t_j$ at $T_j$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:gt_F1}
integrates to
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_i = 1 - \exp\!\left[-A\sum_{j\le i},\Delta t_j \exp\!\left(-\frac{E_a}{RT_j}\right)\right],
  \label{eq:gt_F1_cum}
\end{equation}

Inverting Eq.~\eqref{eq:gt_F1_cum} step by step gives an apparent rate constant for every
step that is independent of any fitted parameter except $\phi$:
\begin{equation}
  k_i = -\frac{1}{\Delta t_i}\ln\frac{1-\alpha_i}{1-\alpha_{i-1}},
  \qquad \alpha_i = \frac{F_i}{1-\phi}.
  \label{eq:gt_stepk}
\end{equation}

For comparison, the diffusion parameter of each step is obtained with the same
\citet{fechtigkalbitzer1966} inversion for a sphere that is applied to the hydrothermal
experiment (\S\ref{helium-in-goethite-diffusivity-inversions}).

Additionally, in both experiments release stalls at $F\approx0.9$. The fitted $\phi=\ChTwoKinPhi$ is the same for KAF
and HDM. In HDM, the remaining $\sim$\ChTwoKinPhiPct\% is released slowly between 340 and 500~$^\circ$C and on
fusion. We interpret this fraction as $^3$He inherited by the hematite product and subsequently
released by the much slower diffusion out of hematite. The conversion and the plateau correspond to the ``Stage 2'' and
``Stage 3'' release of \citet{farley2024}, whose transition near $F\approx0.9$ is independent of
heating schedule (and tracked by a distinct shift in \textsuperscript{4}He/\textsuperscript{3}He: Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-ratio-spectra}).
The kinetic inversions (Eq.~\eqref{eq:gt_stepk}) for these latest steps are highly sensitive to the choice of $\phi$ and are excluded from the fits of the kinetic parameters.
The KAF steps run with the trap isolated are also excluded from the Arrhenius regression.

\subsection{Multiple Diffusion Domain and Thermal History Modeling}\label{sec:gt_mdd_methods}

Together, the $^3$He release and the bulk-normalized $^4$He/$^3$He spectrum constrain the thermal history, if the activation energy 
for diffusion is known \citep{farleyflowers2012}.
These data from HT-Win-06-01b are interpreted in two stages. First, the diffusion domain structure of the sample is inverted
from the $^3$He release alone. Second, natural thermal histories are fitted to the
$^4$He/$^3$He spectrum with that domain structure held fixed, so that every thermal history
must work within the bounds set by the $^3$He data. 
Derivations and numerical details are given in the SI (\S\ref{sec:ch2-si-mdd}).

\paragraph{Diffusion domain structure.} The sample is represented by $N$ non-interacting
spherical domains that share the activation energy and frequency factor of the Stage~1
regression (\S\ref{sec:gt_ht_results}). Domain $j$ holds a fraction $f_j$ of the uniformly
distributed, proton-induced $^3$He ($\sum_j f_j=1$) and has radius $r_j$ relative to the
reference radius $r_0$ implied by the Stage~1 regression, so that
\begin{equation}
  \left(\frac{D}{a^2}\right)_j = \left(\frac{r_j}{r_0}\right)^{-2}\frac{D_0}{a_0^2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{E_a}{RT}\right).
  \label{eq:gt_mdd_domain}
\end{equation}
A model with $N$ domains has $2N-1$ free parameters. During the heating schedule, the
reference domain accumulates the non-dimensional time
$\tau_{0,i}=\sum_{m\le i}(D_0/a_0^2)\exp(-E_a/RT_m)\,\Delta t_m$ by the end of step $i$, and
domain $j$ reaches $\tau_{0,i}(r_j/r_0)^{-2}$. With $F(\tau)$ the cumulative fraction released
by an initially uniform sphere \citep{crank1979}, the model cumulative and fractional
$^3$He yields are
\begin{equation}
  \Sigma F_i=\sum_{j=1}^{N}f_j\,F\!\left(\tau_{0,i}\,(r_j/r_0)^{-2}\right),\qquad
  \Delta F_i=\Sigma F_i-\Sigma F_{i-1}.
  \label{eq:gt_mdd_mix}
\end{equation}
Following \citet{farleyflowers2012}, the fractional and cumulative yields are weighted
equally, and $\{f_j,r_j\}$ minimize
\begin{equation}
  S=\sum_i\left(\Delta F_i^{\mathrm{mod}}-\Delta F_i^{\mathrm{obs}}\right)^2
   +\sum_i\left(\Sigma F_i^{\mathrm{mod}}-\Sigma F_i^{\mathrm{obs}}\right)^2,
  \label{eq:gt_mdd_S}
\end{equation}
summed over the 28 steps with more than 0.1~pccSTP of $^3$He. The gas released in the six
low-signal steps still counts toward the cumulative yields of later steps, and their
durations toward the heating schedule. Domains are added one at a time, each search
starting from the $(N-1)$-domain solution, until an added domain no longer changes the fit
or the domain structure. The Arrhenius and delta plots are not fitted: they follow from
applying the \citet{fechtigkalbitzer1966} inversion to the model cumulative release, and
their misfit is reported as a reduced $\chi^2$ over the same 28 steps.


\paragraph{Thermal histories.}
Any thermal history predicts a $^4$He/$^3$He spectrum that is obtained following the procedure
of \citet{shuster2004}. A summary of this procedure is given in the SI (\S\ref{sec:4he-forward}). Thus, we evaluate 
how well each thermal history reproduces the measured $^4$He/$^3$He spectrum.
We compare three types of thermal histories, each with two free parameters. 
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Steady cooling:} the temperature falls linearly from $T_0$ at
  $t_\mathrm{form}$ to 25\,\textdegree C today. The goethite forms at an elevated
  temperature, for example from warm fluids at depth, and cools monotonically. Free
  parameters: $t_\mathrm{form}$ and $T_0$.
  \item \textbf{Burial and exhumation:} the path passes through $(t_\mathrm{form},T_s)$,
  $(t_\mathrm{peak},T_\mathrm{max})$, $(50~\mathrm{Ma},T_s)$ and $(0,T_s)$ with
  $T_s=20$\,\textdegree C. For this model, we assume that the goethite forms at the surface, 
  is buried and heated to a peak temperature, and is exhumed by the Paleogene, roughly following 
  the thermal history \citep{gibsonstuwe2000,mitchell1998} interpreted for the region from apatite fission track thermochronometry. We test
  the two free parameters, $t_\mathrm{peak}$ and $T_\mathrm{max}$, for fixed crystallization ages of 180, 195 and
  210~Ma.
  \item \textbf{Isothermal hold:} $T=T_\mathrm{iso}$ from $t_\mathrm{form}$ to the
  present. Free parameters: $t_\mathrm{form}$ and $T_\mathrm{iso}$.
\end{itemize}
Each path is scored against
\begin{equation}
  \chi^2=\sum_i\left(\frac{R_i^{\mathrm{mod}}-R_i^{\mathrm{M}}}{\sigma_i}\right)^2,
  \qquad\sigma_i=\max\!\left[\tfrac12\left(R_i^{\mathrm{UB}}-R_i^{\mathrm{LB}}\right),\,0.02\right],
  \label{eq:gt_mdd_chi2}
\end{equation}
over the \MDDNfit{} steps that have lag-correction bounds and more than 0.1~pccSTP of
$^3$He. Where M, LB and UB are the central, lower-bound and upper-bound
bulk-normalized ratios of the bounding method (Table~\ref{tab:ch2-distilled-4he}).
This analysis fits 24 independent data points; subtracting the two fit parameters gives $\nu=22$ degrees of freedom, which are used to evaluate the reduced $\chi^2$.
$\chi^2$ is evaluated for these free parameters along regularly spaced grids to produce a likelihood heatmap (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-mdd-heatmaps}). 
The measured He age (130--180~Ma; \citealt{waltenberg2012}) is treated as a hard constraint. The best fit of each family is
the lowest-$\chi^2$ path with a predicted age in that range, and parameter ranges are those
of the age-consistent paths within the same $\Delta\chi^2$ of that best fit.

\subsection{SEM Study of Thermally Treated Goethite}\label{sem-study-of-thermally-treated-polycrystalline-goethite}\label{sec:gt_pigment_methods}

In early trials of the ``glass tube method'' for helium step heat
experiments with controlled microenvironments presented here,
sub-samples of goethite morphologically similar to Win-06-01b from
Igarapé Bahia \citep{shustervasconcelos2005,monteiro2017} were heated in
vacuum using the projector lamp heating apparatus of \citet{farley1999}
and in sealed glass ampoules with gypsum or water additionally added to
provide moisture to slow/prevent dehydration of goethite. All samples
were held at 300°C for 4-11 hours, and the glass ampoules were broken
and the goethites were allowed to air dry before all samples were placed
on carbon tape and carbon coated. These samples were imaged at Caltech

$\sim$1~\textmu m long, was treated in the same way but only with water, without vacuum or
open-air runs: at least five larger silica glass ampoules, each loaded with pigment and an excess
of water and fused shut at ambient pressure, were held at 200--250~$^\circ$C for about a day in an
oven whose voltage was adjusted by hand. These runs were a rapid, qualitative test; details of
individual runs (temperature histories, durations, water-to-solid ratios) were not recorded, and
reaction was assessed by color (yellow goethite, red hematite) before imaging.

The diffusivities determined from the step heat 3He and 4He
yields for HT-Win-06-01b are always significantly lower than the
diffusivities determined from step heat experiments on the same sample
in vacuum, and follow evolutions that are distinct from those determined from
the vacuum experiments. The Arrhenius plot of the 3He diffusivities
initially follows a declining concave-up trend until \textasciitilde3.5\%
cumulative 3He yield, then slowly decreases in sub-parallel arrays
during prograde and retrograde heating cycles from 3.5-7\% cumulative
3He yields (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-arrhenius-zoom}, Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-delta-zoom}). The Arrhenius plot of the 4He diffusivities
differs in shape: it is concave-down to about 5\% cumulative 3He yield,
after which it follows a roughly linear array about one log unit below
the 3He array (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-arrhenius-zoom}). The 3He diffusivities settled into a
linear array which was suitable for linear regression after
\textasciitilde7\% of the cumulative 3He yield was lost (Fig.~\ref{fig:gt_arrhenius}b). All
remaining diffusivity measurements of the experiment were included in
the linear regression, so that diffusivities from 3He loss fractions of
about 7-15\% were included. The linear regression yields an
R\textsuperscript{2} of \ChTwoRtwo, an activation energy of \ChTwoEa ± \ChTwoEaErr
kJ/mol, and a closure temperature of \ChTwoTc ± \ChTwoTcErr °C assuming a cooling
rate of 10°C/Ma and considering only the uncertainty in the regression
intercept (D\textsubscript{0}/a\textsuperscript{2}).

An unweighted least-squares fit of $\ln k_i$ against $1/T_i$ for the \ChTwoKinLineN{} steps in the fitting window
gives
\begin{equation}
  E_a = \ChTwoKinLineEa \pm \ChTwoKinLineEaErr~\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}},

\end{equation}
KAF alone gives $\ChTwoKinKAFEa\pm\ChTwoKinKAFEaErr$ and HDM alone $\ChTwoKinHDMEa\pm\ChTwoKinHDMEaErr$~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$), in agreement with the
cumulative fit. The result does not depend on the fitting window: fitting all steps instead gives
$E_a = \ChTwoKinAllFOneEa$~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$ and the same ranking of models (\S\ref{sec:gt_supp_mech}).

The kinetic rate parameters fitted to KAF alone predict the independent HDM schedule with an rms misfit in
$F$ of \ChTwoKinFOneRmsHDM{} over the fitting window. A single-domain spherical diffusion model fits both vacuum step 
heats worse, even when given the same hematite-inherited fraction ($\phi = \ChTwoKinPhi$) as the first-order fit (Table~\ref{tab:gt_mech_fits}). 

KAF steps 1--4 and HDM steps 2--7
(below $\sim$180~$^\circ$C) release $^3$He 0.5--3 orders of magnitude faster than the extrapolated 
conversion rate (Fig.~\ref{fig:gt_arrhenius}). This component amounts
to less than 2\% of the $^3$He.

In two KAF steps the trap was isolated and evolved water accumulated in the line. At
230~$^\circ$C the step rate constant fell to \ChTwoKinTrapSuppLow{} times the mean of its neighbors at the same
temperature, and at 240~$^\circ$C to \ChTwoKinTrapSuppHigh{} times. The rate recovered fully when the trap was
restored. Water and $^3$He were suppressed together. We return to these two steps in \S\ref{sec:gt_nucleation}, where
they calibrate the nucleation model.

\subsection{Isothermal Discrimination Test at 250~$^\circ$C}\label{sec:gt_hold_results}

Sequences of isothermal steps can be particularly powerful in discriminating first and second order processes.
Diffusion slows quickly as the diffusion front moves inward, depleting the grain
rims of helium, whereas the rate of first-order conversion slows less for similar yields. We harnessed the principle to conduct a sensitive test
by performing a series of 4 consecutive, long isothermal steps at 250~$^\circ$C at the end of the water-saturated experiment: three consecutive 5-hour steps and one 170.8-hour step. Using the first three
5~h steps at 250~$^\circ$C, we invert for both kinetic rate constants and apparent diffusivities, then use each model to project the yield for the 170.8~h step.

Both the first-order and diffusion models yield reproducible results for the three 5-hour calibration steps (Fig.~\ref{fig:gt_hold}), but their 
projections sharply diverge for the long step. Diffusion projects a final fractional cumulative yield of $F = 0.149$ against 
an observed $F = 0.146$, a much stronger agreement than the first-order model prediction of $F = 0.173$, which overpredicts
the helium yield for that step by 46\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:gt_hold}). The data's uncertainty is too small to affect this result. In the interest of thoroughness, 
we also tested the contracting-geometry and Avrami models of \S\ref{sec:gt_supp_mech}, which fit the vacuum data less well. 
Calibrated the same way, they project cumulative fractional yields within 0.174--0.212, which diverge even more strongly from the data.

\section{Discussion}\label{ch2-discussion}

\subsection{Dehydroxylation Kinetics in Vacuum Step Heats of Win-06-01b}\label{sec:gt_vacuum}

\citet{farley2024} showed that evolved helium in vacuum step heats of Win-06-01b (and many other goethite samples) is controlled 
by the conversion of goethite to hematite. Here, we present a reanalysis of the vacuum step heat data of Win-06-01b 
from KAF and HDM to show that the helium release of Win-06-01b is better described 
by a model of first-order reaction kinetics for goethite dehydroxylation than by a second order diffusion model.

We postulate that the early $^3$He released faster than the extrapolated conversion rate (\S\ref{sec:gt_kin_results}) is diffusively lost from the least
retentive domains; it corresponds to ``Stage 1'' helium release of \citet{farley2024}.
The kinetic reduction yields simpler, more interpretable Arrhenius plots for the vacuum experiments than the diffusion inversion does. The kinetic inversion collapses the
prograde steps at the beginning of the vacuum experiments onto a single line that the diffusion reduction instead shows as an irreversible 
increase in diffusivity (Fig.~\ref{fig:gt_arrhenius}). This initial helium release is most easily read as 
a diffusion-related loss from a broad distribution of small, poorly retentive domains in the goethite. 
The Arrhenius plot of the kinetic reduction makes it clear that helium release from the initial steps is in 
excess of the rate of dehydroxylation, which is not obvious from the Arrhenius plot produced with the diffusion reduction. 
This shift in the dominance from diffusive loss to dehydroxylation-related loss offers a clear explanation for the change in behavior between ``Stage 1'' and ``Stage 2''
in vacuum step heat experiments described by \citet{farley2024}.

Thermogravimetric studies of goethite dehydroxylation report somewhat higher apparent activation
energies, $154\pm15$ to $169\pm8$~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$, for a reaction controlled by a two-dimensional
phase boundary, with a different mechanism at early stages and low temperature \citep{goss1987}. This may simply
be due to a difference in samples: goethites from different sources have different particle sizes, morphologies, 
and aluminum contents, all of which affect the kinetics of dehydroxylation \citep{langmuir1971,yapp1983,tardy1985,majzlannavrotsky2003}.

\subsection{Helium Release under Water-Saturated Conditions Is Diffusive}\label{sec:gt_hydro}

The goal of this work was to attempt to slow the goethite dehydroxylation process
below the rate of thermal helium loss in unchanging goethite.
The thermal schedule included several prograde-retrograde heating cycles
which together would have completely dehydroxylated goethite in vacuum, using the vacuum kinetics of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:gt_line}. Even so, only 14.7\% of the $^3$He was released in the water-saturated experiment;
we argue below that the helium loss in the water-saturated experiment is controlled by diffusion rather than by conversion.

Fig.~\ref{fig:gt_arrhenius} compares the kinetic and diffusion inversions of the step heat data.
These plots show the opposite trend for the vacuum and hydrothermal experiments: the hydrothermal experiment collapses into a simpler, coherent
linear array with the diffusivity reduction, whereas the kinetic inversion of the same data records a continuously declining $k$ for any given temperature as 
the experiment proceeds, forming a zig-zag pattern. This is the expected behavior for a second-order (diffusion-controlled) process for which helium loss 
slows more strongly than a first-order process as the experiment proceeds. Fig.~\ref{fig:gt_arrhenius} shows that helium loss 
is first-order for vacuum experiments and second order for the water-saturated experiment. Section~\ref{sec:gt_nucleation} shows 
that these observations require the conversion rate at water saturation to be at least $10^{2.5}$ times slower than in vacuum, and that a
nucleation control on the dehydroxylation kinetics explains suppression far greater than that. 

While it is difficult to prove that decomposition or recrystallization is not
playing a role in the results presented in this work, the results presented here suggest that 
a second-order process (likely volume diffusion) is the dominant mechanism of helium loss for Win-06-01b goethite in water-saturated conditions.

\subsubsection{Consistency with MDD Systematics}\label{consistency-with-mdd-systematics}

All existing helium step heat data on goethites are from naturally occurring
polycrystalline aggregates with unclear and potentially widely variable
grain sizes \citep{monteiro2017,farley2024}, which likely could
follow MDD systematics \citep{farley2018}. In MDD systems, apparent bulk diffusivities of a homogeneously distributed
diffusant at a single temperature will decrease as an experiment
proceeds, or for a system where the activation energy of diffusion is known, the temperature dependence can be removed by referring to the data
in relation to a temperature-dependent reference line, as in delta or r/r0 plots (where
\(\Delta = \ ln\left( \frac{D}{a^{2}} \right)_{measured} - ln\left( \frac{D}{a^{2}} \right)_{reference}\)
) \citep{lovera1991,farley2018}. For the activation energy of \ChTwoEa{}~kJ/mol
retrieved by regression in this work, the homogeneously distributed 3He
step heat data follow the expected MDD systematics and monotonically
decrease within a delta plot (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-delta-zoom}), which is consistent with
volume diffusion in an MDD system. 

The strong plateau in the delta plot of the water-saturated goethite experiment and the statistically robust regression
over a wide range of fractional cumulative 3He yield (from 7-15\% $\Sigma F_{3\mathrm{He}}$
with an R\textsuperscript{2} of \ChTwoRtwo) suggest that the frequency
factor obtained by the regression may be dominated by a single
diffusion domain for $F_c \ge 7\%$ rather than a mix of diffusion domains as is most
often observed in the apparent diffusivities for polycrystalline
hematite \citep{farley2018}.

A multiple diffusion domain inversion of the $^3$He release (\S\ref{sec:gt_mdd_methods})
tests this claim (Table~\ref{tab:ch2-mdd-domains}; Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-mdd-domains}).
A single domain cannot fit the release (Arrhenius $\chi^2_\nu=27$), and two domains capture
the high early diffusivities but leave a poor Arrhenius fit ($\chi^2_\nu=3.3$). Three
domains reduce the yield misfit by more than half ($\chi^2_\nu=1.08$). With four, the
smallest domain splits in two and the fit improves slightly further ($\chi^2_\nu=1.00$).
Every domain added beyond four coincides in size with the dominant domain and leaves the
fit unchanged. We therefore adopt four domains as a complete description of the $^3$He data:
\begin{itemize}
  \item 0.57\% of the $^3$He at $r/r_0=6.8\times10^{-4}$;
  \item 0.66\% at $r/r_0=2.9\times10^{-3}$;
  \item 1.5\% at $r/r_0=0.031$;
  \item 97.2\% at $r/r_0=1.12$.
\end{itemize}
The three small domains, holding 2.8\% of the $^3$He in total, are fully degassed during the
experiment and account for the high early diffusivities and the gradual approach of the
delta plot to the Stage~1 line (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-mdd-arrh-delta}). The dominant domain releases
12\% of its $^3$He during the experiment and diffuses at 0.80 times the Stage~1 rate. The
Stage~1 line is therefore a slightly fast description of the dominant domain, because the
bulk-inverted diffusivities on which it was regressed still carry a small contribution from
the fast domains. The four-domain model reproduces the Arrhenius array, including its
retrograde segments, and the delta plot, although neither is fitted directly. Fits for
$N=1$--5 are shown in the SI (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-si-mdd-3he-fits}).

\subsection{Thermodynamic Model of Goethite Dehydroxylation}\label{sec:gt_thermo_methods}\label{sec:gt_thermo}

The preceding sections establish from helium step heat data alone that the vacuum step heats of
Win-06-01b record first order conversion to hematite, whereas the water-saturated step heat records second order diffusion
without detectable dehydroxylation. Here we use the thermodynamics of goethite dehydroxylation to
explain why water has this effect and to estimate how much solid-state
conversion could have occurred in the hydrothermal experiment (\S\ref{sec:gt_nucleation}).

Goethite breaks down to hematite by the dehydroxylation reaction
\begin{equation}
  2\,\alpha\text{-FeOOH} \;\rightleftharpoons\; \alpha\text{-Fe}_2\text{O}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O}_{(\text{fluid})},
  \label{eq:gt_rxn}
\end{equation}
whose Gibbs energy change, written per mole of hematite formed, is
\begin{equation}
  \Delta G_r(T,P) = G_{\mathrm{hem}}(T,P) + G_{\mathrm{w}}(T,P) - 2\,G_{\mathrm{goe}}(T,P) + \Delta G_{\mathrm{surf}} .
  \label{eq:gt_dGr}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta G_r$ is the Gibbs energy change of the reaction and $G_{\mathrm{hem}}$, $G_{\mathrm{w}}$, and $G_{\mathrm{goe}}$ are the Gibbs energies of hematite, water, and goethite, respectively. 
$\Delta G_{\mathrm{surf}}$ is the surface energy contribution, which must be considered to reproduce observed phase stability \citep{navrotsky2008}. 
Goethite is stable where $\Delta G_r>0$ and hematite plus water where $\Delta G_r<0$. The values and equations used to evaluate each of these terms are given in the supplement: \S\ref{sec:eqm-thermo}.

% --- Added 2026-09-25 (goethite stability integration; see CHANGES_JOURNAL.md) ---

This model can be used to calculate the stability field for
goethite with respect to dehydroxylation to hematite and water in $\log P_{\mathrm{H_2O}}$--$T$ space (Fig.~\ref{fig:gt_PT}). This 
phase diagram communicates several important features of the goethite--hematite--water system that are relevant to the experiments considered here:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Goethite is not stable at the conditions of any of the experiments considered here, in water or in vacuum, in spite of the evidence that it did not react in the water-saturated experiment. Thus, we hypothesize that the reaction is kinetically inhibited for goethite under these conditions.
  \item The stability of goethite is strongly dependent on the partial pressure of water below and up to water saturation. Above the saturation pressure, water is liquid and contributes far less to the reaction volume than it does as a gas, becoming a secondary control on goethite's stability.
  \item The stability of goethite is strongly dependent on grain size, with smaller grains being more stable than larger grains. At water saturation, bulk goethite is stable below 18~$^\circ$C, 100~nm goethite below 28~$^\circ$C, and 10~nm goethite below 108~$^\circ$C. 
\end{enumerate}

Stability alone does not determine what happens in a step heat; the rate of conversion controls
whether this equilibrium state is reached or if the reaction proceeds at all,
and that depends on the chemical potential of the reaction: $\Delta\mu$. Table~\ref{tab:gt_dmu} gives the bulk driving

($P_{\mathrm{H_2O}}\approx10^{-8}$~atm) it is 76--106~kJ, about ten times larger. The chemical
potential of water is therefore the key variable separating the two experimental settings.

\subsection{Water Pressure Control on the Hematite-in-Goethite Nucleation Barrier}\label{sec:gt_nucleation}

The hydrothermal data bound how fast goethite could have converted to hematite in water. Under
the vacuum kinetics of Eq.~\eqref{eq:gt_line}, the hydrothermal schedule gives
$\sum_i k_{\mathrm{vac}}(T_i)\,\Delta t_i \approx \ChTwoKinSumKdt$, enough to convert the sample
completely. If water lowers the conversion rate by a factor $R = k_{\mathrm{sat}}/k_{\mathrm{vac}}$,
then even if all of the observed $^3$He release came from conversion
($\alpha = F/(1-\phi) = \ChTwoKinHTFEnd/\ChTwoKinOneMinusPhi = \ChTwoKinHTAlphaObs$), $R$ can be at most
$10^{\ChTwoKinLogRObs}$. The release is second order volume diffusion (\S\ref{sec:gt_hydro}). Limiting 
the dehydroxylation fraction to below 1\% requires $R \le 10^{\ChTwoKinLogRDetect}$. Here we show that classical nucleation
theory, calibrated on the vacuum data alone, predicts an even stronger rate suppression under water-saturated conditions.

\textit{Nucleation barrier.} In classical nucleation theory \citep{gibbs1878,volmer1926,becker1935},
reviewed by \citet{christian2002}, \citet{kashchiev2000} and, for mineral systems,
\citet{deyoreo2003} and \citet{lasaga1998}, a spherical hematite nucleus of radius $r$ has free
energy
\begin{equation}
  \Delta G(r) = -\frac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}\,\frac{\Delta\mu}{V_{\mathrm{hem}}} + 4\pi r^{2}\gamma,
\end{equation}
where $\Delta\mu$ is the driving force per mole of Fe$_2$O$_3$ (Table~\ref{tab:gt_dmu}),
$V_{\mathrm{hem}}$ the molar volume and $\gamma$ the energy of the interface surrounding the
nucleus. Nuclei larger than the critical radius $r^{*} = 2\gamma V_{\mathrm{hem}}/\Delta\mu$ grow,
and the energetic barrier to forming one is
\begin{equation}
  \Delta G^{*} = \frac{16\pi\,\gamma^{3}\,V_{\mathrm{hem}}^{2}}{3\,\Delta\mu^{2}} .
  \label{eq:gt_barrier}
\end{equation}
In the first-order limit of \S\ref{sec:gt_vacuum}, the rate constant is a nucleation rate of the
form derived by \citet{turnbull1949},
\begin{gather}
  k = A\exp\!\left(-\frac{E_a}{RT}\right)\exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta G^{*}}{k_B T}\right), \notag\\
  \frac{\Delta G^{*}}{k_B T} = \frac{C\gamma^{3}}{T\,\Delta\mu^{2}},
  \qquad
  C \equiv \frac{16\pi V_{\mathrm{hem}}^{2}}{3k_B} = 1.112\times10^{15}~\mathrm{m^6\,K\,J^{-1}\,mol^{-2}} ,
  \label{eq:gt_knuc}
\end{gather}
where $A\exp(-E_a/RT)$ describes the atomic mobility and $\exp(-\Delta G^{*}/k_BT)$ is the
equilibrium fraction of sites occupied by critical nuclei. Because the barrier scales as
$\gamma^3/\Delta\mu^2$, the tenfold drop in $\Delta\mu$ between vacuum and water saturation raises 
the energetic barrier $\Delta G^{*}$ a hundredfold (Table~\ref{tab:gt_dmu}).

\textit{Internal nucleation.} Goethite dehydroxylates topotactically: hematite nucleates within
the parent crystal, inherits its oxygen framework and orientation, and the pseudomorph develops
pores and twinned hematite domains \citep{limadefaria1963,watari1979,watari1983,cudennec2005}.
We therefore consider only internal nucleation, in which the new interface of the burgeoning nuclei is between hematite
and goethite. We know of no measured or calculated energy for that interface, so $\gamma$ is
replaced by an effective parameter $\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}$, calibrated from the data.

\textit{Calibration.} When \citet{farley2024} isolated the liquid nitrogen trap from the heated goethite, they raised $P_{\mathrm{H_2O}}$ in the line and lowered
$\Delta\mu$, and the isothermal rate fell by a factor $s$ (\S\ref{sec:gt_vacuum}). With $A$ and $E_a$
unchanged, Eq.~\eqref{eq:gt_knuc} gives
\begin{equation}
  \gamma_{\mathrm{eff}} = \left[\frac{T\ln(1/s)}{C\left(\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{off}}^{-2} - \Delta\mu_{\mathrm{on}}^{-2}\right)}\right]^{1/3},
  \label{eq:gt_gamma_cal}
\end{equation}
with $\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{on}}$ evaluated at $P_{\mathrm{H_2O}} = 10^{-8}$~atm and $\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{off}}$ at an
upper bound on the water pressure with the trap isolated. This pressure estimate of the water released depends on the
volume of the experimental apparatus and the water content emitted by the goethite, which was measured with 
capacitance manometry (\S\ref{sec:gt_supp_barrier}). This gives $\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}} = 0.16$~J\,m$^{-2}$
(range \ChTwoKinGammaLo--\ChTwoKinGammaHi). Under vacuum the corresponding critical nucleus is smaller
than a formula unit ($r^{*} = \ChTwoKinRstarVac$~nm) and the barrier is only \ChTwoKinBarVac~$k_BT$:
conversion in vacuum is effectively barrierless, consistent with the simple Arrhenius behavior of
both vacuum experiments. At this scale classical nucleation theory has no literal meaning, so
$\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is best read as the single parameter of a rate law in which $\ln k$ falls as
$-1/\Delta\mu^{2}$, not as an interface energy. At saturation, $r^{*} \approx 1$~nm and the barrier
is \ChTwoKinBarSat~$k_BT$.

\textit{Translation to water saturation.} For the same sample, only $\Delta\mu$ changes between
vacuum and saturation:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{k_{\mathrm{sat}}}{k_{\mathrm{vac}}} = \exp\!\left[-\frac{C\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}^{3}}{T}\left(\frac{1}{\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{sat}}^{2}} - \frac{1}{\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{vac}}^{2}}\right)\right].
  \label{eq:gt_translate}
\end{equation}
Applied step by step to the hydrothermal schedule, Eq.~\eqref{eq:gt_translate} predicts a total
conversion ($\sum_i \frac{k_{\mathrm{sat}}}{k_{\mathrm{vac}}}(T_i)\,k_{\mathrm{vac}}(T_i)\,\Delta t_i$) 
of $10^{\ChTwoKinLogAlphaLow}$, $10^{\ChTwoKinLogAlphaMid}$ and $10^{\ChTwoKinLogAlphaHigh}$ for
$\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}} = 0.14$, 0.16 and 0.19~J\,m$^{-2}$, and $10^{\ChTwoKinMCCNTLo}$ to
$10^{\ChTwoKinMCCNTHi}$ (95\%) when the uncertainties in the calibration and in the thermodynamic
data are propagated (\S\ref{sec:gt_supp_barrier}). The exact values reflect the assumed
$\Delta\mu^{-2}$ dependence; what matters is that the model predicts
suppression far beyond the $10^{\ChTwoKinLogRDetect}$ that the data require.

\textit{Assumptions.} This is a consistent physical explanation of the data, not a proof. It is not exhaustive. This analysis
assumes (i) that the trap-isolated slowdown acts through $\Delta\mu$, rather than, for example,
through water blocking reactive sites; (ii) that $\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}$, $A$ and $E_a$ are the same in
vacuum and in water; (iii) that the barrier depends on the bulk driving force without significant contribution from surface
terms (this analysis extended to 10~nm grains raises the predicted conversion only to $\sim10^{-23}$;
\S\ref{sec:gt_supp_barrier}); and (iv) that conversion is solid-state. Conversion by dissolution
of goethite and precipitation of hematite from the fluid is not described by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:gt_translate} (see \S\ref{sec:gt_pigment}). The calibrated $\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}$ absorbs contributions from the
sample's microstructure and cannot be transferred to other goethites.

\subsection{Goethite Dehydration and/or Recrystallization During Helium Step Heat Experiments}\label{goethite-dehydration-andor-recrystallization-during-hydrothermal-experiments}

The aim of helium step heat experiments is to characterize the
mechanisms and rates of helium loss in nature to aid in the interpretation
of geochronologic and helium geochemical data. The results of this
experiment are encouraging in that they differ remarkably from
experiments on the same samples in vacuum that are primarily
controlled by dehydration processes \citep{farley2024}, but we cannot exclude that this
experiment may have been affected to some degree by processes aside from
volume diffusion that are unrelated to helium loss in
goethite in nature. We consider two processes outside of volume diffusion that could have contributed to the helium released in the
water-saturated experiment: (i) recrystallization of goethite or (ii) fluid-mediated conversion of goethite to hematite. If either 
of these processes affected the helium release, then the apparent diffusivities reported here would represent upper bounds for 
diffusive helium loss in goethite in nature.

The helium step heat data at water-saturated conditions reported here are better fit with a diffusion model than with a
conversion model (\S\ref{sec:gt_hydro}). The kinetic analysis of \S\ref{sec:gt_vacuum} and the nucleation analysis of
\S\ref{sec:gt_nucleation} indicate that topotactic dehydroxylation has a nucleation barrier that is insurmountable for
experimental conditions and timescales; however, this model has no bearing on fluid-mediated dehydroxylation. 
Equilibrium thermodynamics predict that goethite is unstable with respect to hematite and water at
these conditions \citep{majzlan2003} (\S\ref{sec:gt_thermo}) and therefore dehydroxylation of goethite
in the experiment cannot be outright excluded. 

The process of recrystallization is another potential complication in
interpreting helium step heats of goethite in hydrothermal conditions.
Poorly crystalline synthetic goethite held in hydrothermal conditions is
known to recrystallize into more orderly, crystalline goethite
aggregates, even at temperatures as low as 125°C in as little as 16
hours \citep{schwertmann1985}. In this study, natural goethite was
held in contact with liquid water and water vapor at hydrothermal
conditions as harsh as \textasciitilde40 bars and 250°C for many days. 
These conditions appear to have visibly dissolved and
reprecipitated silica from the ampoule's inner walls (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-ampoule-post}),
qualitatively testifying to the ability of the hydrothermal fluid to
dissolve and recrystallize rock-forming oxides. It is unquestionable
that recrystallization of some goethite could have occurred under these
conditions; however, it is unclear if conditions were favorable to
recrystallize this specimen of goethite, Win-06-01b, which is amongst
the most helium-retentive and crystalline dated goethite samples
\citep{waltenberg2012,farley2024}. Less crystalline goethites in
nature are commonly recrystallized, which appears to result in helium
loss, resetting the U-Th/He age \citep{monteiro2018,monteiro2014,heim2006}. 
Recrystallization could be the dominant process
controlling helium loss in this experiment, but only if it is faster
than diffusive loss, in which case the data presented here would
represent new upper bounds for apparent diffusivities for helium in
Win-06-01b goethite. Recrystallization might also be faster than volume
diffusion of helium in goethite in nature over geologic timescales even if it did not occur in this experiment. 
In that case, recrystallization could be the primary driver of helium loss in nature regardless of the results of this experiment.

\subsubsection{Fluid Mediated Conversion of Synthetic Goethite Pigment}\label{sec:gt_pigment}

We observed the spontaneous conversion of synthetic goethite pigment (\S\ref{sec:gt_pigment_methods}) to hematite when 
heated in ampoules with excess water at experimental temperatures. Roughly equal proportions of
ampoules were completely reacted (red), completely unreacted (yellow), or contained both
reacted and unreacted material in isolated patches. In the mixed ampoules, the red and yellow portions were entirely
isolated from one another, separated by gas bubbles. Unreacted pigment retains its original acicular habit
(Fig.~\ref{fig:gt_pigment}b). The reacted hematite product is instead composed of equant to platy crystals
$\sim$0.5--1~\textmu m across (Fig.~\ref{fig:gt_pigment}a). The change of shape rules out pseudomorphic dehydroxylation
like that observed to occur in goethite heated in vacuum (Figs.~\ref{fig:ch2-sem-buffered},~\ref{fig:ch2-sem-control-vacuum}, and~\ref{fig:ch2-sem-vacuum-interior}), indicating dissolution of
goethite and nucleation and growth of hematite from the fluid, a pathway outside the solid-state
nucleation model developed in \S\ref{sec:gt_nucleation}.

Conversion either went to completion within a fluid-connected volume or did not occur at all, 
even where reacted and unreacted pigment shared the same ampoule and thermal history. We posit 
that the fluid-mediated reaction requires some threshold of dissolved Fe(III) in the fluid to 
nucleate hematite, but because the reaction consumes and produces dissolved Fe(III) in equal proportions, 
it is not self-limiting and proceeds quickly to completion once that threshold is reached. 

We conclude that water-saturated conditions do not by themselves exclude fluid-mediated
conversion of goethite, even where topotactic conversion is suppressed (\S\ref{sec:gt_nucleation}). 
Whether that pathway operates likely depends on the solution chemistry. Still, the helium step heat data for Win-06-01b-HT
show no evidence for the abrupt and extreme helium loss that would be expected to accompany a fluid-mediated reaction. 
The step heat data are instead consistent with a smooth, history-dependent release of helium, as expected for diffusive loss (\S\ref{sec:gt_hydro}).

Grain size and bulk thermodynamics cannot explain why the pigment blades reacted when the natural 
polycrystalline goethite did not. At 1~\textmu m, the surface contribution to $\Delta G_r$ is only
$\sim$0.04~kJ, and the bulk driving force at saturation is the same 6--11~kJ for both materials
(Table~\ref{tab:gt_dmu}). The most likely difference is the fluid itself. Commercial iron-oxide
pigments are commonly produced by precipitation from iron-salt solutions. They can carry
residual soluble salts such as iron and alkali sulfates or nitrates, and they are often surface-treated with 
organometallic compounds \citep{legodi2007,almeidasilva2017,xu2021}. Such contaminants would
change the pH and ionic strength of the water they are heated in, and with them the solubility
of Fe(III). The dissolution--precipitation pathway is limited by how much iron the fluid
carries, so a modest increase in Fe(III) solubility could make a nucleation-limited reaction
viable. The natural Win-06-01b goethite, by contrast, was heated in
distilled water, where near-neutral Fe(III) solubility is very low, potentially offering an explanation for
the divergent outcomes.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{High Helium Retentivity of
Win-06-01b-HT}{ High Helium Retentivity of Win-06-01b-HT}}\label{high-helium-retentivity-of-win-06-01b-ht}

The apparent diffusivities of the water-saturated experiment are approximately seven natural log units lower than
those obtained from the same sample in vacuum by \citet{monteiro2017} and \citet{farley2024} (Fig.~\ref{fig:gt_arrhenius}b,
Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-arrhenius-zoom}, Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-delta-zoom}). This high
helium retentivity is explored through three lenses: closure temperature
of cooling samples \citep{dodson1973}, isothermal diffusion \citep{wolf1998}, and through comparison with step heats of other polycrystalline
samples.

If the slope and intercept on the regression of 3He apparent
diffusivities from this experiment are taken to represent a real
diffusion domain for goethite, then a closure temperature can be
calculated for this domain. Considering uncertainty only in the
intercept (D\textsubscript{0}/a\textsuperscript{2}) term and a cooling rate of 10°C/Ma, the closure
temperature for that domain would be \ChTwoTc ± \ChTwoTcErr °C.
The multiple diffusion domain inversion (\S\ref{consistency-with-mdd-systematics}) indicates
that the dominant domain, which holds 97\% of the $^3$He, diffuses at 0.80 times the rate of
the Stage~1 line. Its closure temperature is correspondingly about 1.6\,\textdegree C higher,
well within the quoted uncertainty; the Stage~1 parameters are used throughout this section.

Goethite is formed near Earth's surface and thus U-Th/He geochronology
is most typically applied to date surface processes \citep{heim2006,yappshuster2011,vasconcelos2013,danisik2013,monteiro2014,hofmann2017,allard2018}. 
For crystals of goethite grown and collected near the surface, 
the concept of a closure temperature is poorly applicable, as
it necessarily assumes a cooling rate \citep{dodson1973}. In such cases, a
better measure of retentivity is an analysis of isothermal retentivity
over time \citep{wolf1998}, which is commonly expressed in terms of
t'/t, where t' is the helium age and t is the isothermal holding time
(most often the `real age'). For an isothermal holding time of 100 Ma at
40°C, a diffusive domain with the parameters obtained from the
regression of 3He apparent diffusivities of Win-06-01b-HT has a t'/t = 0.968, where t'/t = 1 corresponds to perfect retentivity. Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-isothermal-retention}
shows isothermal holding time against helium age for this same domain
for 4 different temperatures. At 25°C, this domain is nearly
quantitatively retentive of helium even at billion-year time scales. The
results presented here qualitatively support that goethite is highly
retentive of helium at surface conditions, even at 100-million-year
timescales.

This study of a single sample has no bearing on potential complexities
related to aluminum content, radiation damage, or crystal defects on bulk diffusion
\citep{bassal2022}, nor does it permit estimation of the diffusivity
at infinite temperature (D0); this would require experiments on controlled grain sizes.

\subsection{Profiling Radiogenic Helium Loss with $\frac{^4He}{^3He}$ Ratios of Win-06-01b-HT}\label{spectra-of-win-06-01b-ht}

Bulk-normalized \textsuperscript{4}He/\textsuperscript{3}He ratios in step heats of irradiated samples
measure the rounding of concentration profiles of radiogenic helium by natural helium loss, if the
loss mechanism in nature and in the laboratory share the same geometry
\citep{shuster2004,farley2024}. Aside from the first
experimental step, bulk-normalized \textsuperscript{4}He/\textsuperscript{3}He ratios for all experiments begin
at vanishingly small values and steeply increase at first, with the rate
of increase slowing as the experiments proceed (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-ratio-spectra}). This
behavior is consistent with complete then partial loss of radiogenic
helium in nature in the least retentive domains and is observed in all
step heat experiments on irradiated goethite \citep{farley2024}. 
These least retentive domains could represent fine goethite grains, or they could represent 
interstitial helium trapped between crystallite grain boundaries.
Goethite aggregates are known to have interstices that are filled by
chemisorbed non-stoichiometric water that represents the earliest water
to be lost in vacuum \citep{naono1987}. Non-stoichiometric hydrogen
is known to persist in goethite even after heating to 140°C \citep{yapppoths1995}. It is therefore reasonable to posit that artificial 3He
observed in less-retentive domains may be trapped by non-stoichiometric
water or hydroxyl groups within interstices of goethite aggregates.
Dehydration of non-stoichiometric interstitial water may be associated
with early fractions of degassing 3He from goethite samples
during step heats, and could potentially represent the LRD (low retention
domain) of \citet{shustervasconcelos2005}, and the 3He offgassed during ``Stage
1'' in vacuum step heats \citep{farley2024}. The high variability of
the portion of 3He hosted in early-degassing ``Stage 1'' fractions
within vacuum step heats is sensible considering observations that
goethite formed at different conditions is known to have highly
variable crystallinity, crystallite ordering, surface area, and
non-stoichiometric water contents \citep{schwertmann1985}. 

The hydrothermal experiment records lower bulk-normalized \textsuperscript{4}He/\textsuperscript{3}He
ratios than the vacuum experiments at any given $\Sigma F_{3\mathrm{He}}$ for the duration
of the experiment. Similarly, the approach to the \textsuperscript{4}He/\textsuperscript{3}He plateau varies
between the three experiments: a high bulk normalized \textsuperscript{4}He/\textsuperscript{3}He plateau is
most decisively represented in the \citet{farley2024} experiment,
whereas the \textsuperscript{4}He/\textsuperscript{3}He ratios in the step heats of \citet{monteiro2017} transition
more gradually before reaching a plateau later in the experiment, while
the increase in \textsuperscript{4}He/\textsuperscript{3}He ratios is slowest for the hydrothermal experiment
(Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-ratio-spectra}). This suggests that the geometry of helium loss
differs between the hydrothermal and vacuum experiments, and could be explained by the relative
dominance of dehydration processes or their mode of spatial propagation as a
function of water pressure, where the smoothest \textsuperscript{4}He/\textsuperscript{3}He spectra are
generated by higher water pressures. We interpret this experimental control on the
\textsuperscript{4}He/\textsuperscript{3}He spectra to reflect the relative importance of goethite dehydration and helium
diffusion through crystalline goethite in governing the geometry of helium loss from
the sample.

If the geometry of helium loss due to dehydration and due to diffusion
differ, then the \textsuperscript{4}He/\textsuperscript{3}He spectra of goethite measured in vacuum may not
straightforwardly encode the thermal history of the sample in the way that such
data faithfully encode this history in phases such as hematite or apatite
\citep{farleymckeon2015,shuster2004}. More \textsuperscript{4}He/\textsuperscript{3}He spectra of goethite under hydrothermal
conditions are needed to investigate this hypothesis further. 

These ratios also bear on the natural thermal history of Win-06-01b. Throughout the range of
$\Sigma F_{3\mathrm{He}}$ that defines the Stage~1 reference line (7--15\%), step \textsuperscript{4}He/\textsuperscript{3}He
ratios remain at 0.67--0.82 of the bulk value (Table~\ref{tab:ch2-distilled-4he}).
Radiogenic \textsuperscript{4}He is depleted relative to the uniformly distributed \textsuperscript{3}He in this domain, 
so even this retentive domain lost helium in nature. A domain with the
Stage~1 parameters is nearly quantitatively retentive at Earth-surface temperatures
(\S\ref{high-helium-retentivity-of-win-06-01b-ht}), so this loss is difficult to attribute to thermally activated
diffusion at surface conditions. Either Win-06-01b experienced temperatures well above surface conditions 
(perhaps it grew in a hydrothermal setting or experienced burial heating), or non-thermal processes such as fluid-mediated
recrystallization caused partial helium loss in nature. Section~\ref{sec:gt_thermal_history}
tests whether thermally activated diffusion alone, within the domain structure inferred from
the $^3$He release, can reproduce the measured spectrum. 

\subsection{Thermal History of Win-06-01b}\label{sec:gt_thermal_history}

We begin the exercise by limiting our exploration of potential thermal histories to 
geologically reasonable and simple paths. With the four-domain structure of
\S\ref{consistency-with-mdd-systematics} held fixed, we fitted steady-cooling,
burial-and-exhumation, and isothermal thermal histories to the $^4$He/$^3$He spectrum of HT-Win-06-01b
(\S\ref{sec:gt_mdd_methods}). Previous studies constrain our exploration of the burial-and-exhumation paths. 
\citet{gibsonstuwe2000,mitchell1998} interpret regional cooling 
from roughly 50--70\,\textdegree C after the mid-Cretaceous from their apatite
fission-track studies. \citet{mitchell1998} additionally interpret, from limited 1.5~km thick sediment accumulation 
in Triassic intramontane basins, that parts of the ranges were near the surface in the early
Mesozoic.  Table~\ref{tab:ch2-mdd-thermal} gives the best fit of each
family, Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-mdd-spectrum-compare} compares the best steady-cooling and burial
predictions with the spectrum, and Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-mdd-heatmaps} shows their $\chi^2$ maps on a
common color scale.

\paragraph{Steady cooling.} Linear cooling to 25\,\textdegree C fits the spectrum best. The best
age-consistent path starts at 78\,\textdegree C at 150~Ma ($\chi^2_\nu=2.00$), and the

\paragraph{Burial and exhumation.} Burial paths fit the spectrum less well

210~Ma, only hotter and earlier peaks (70--74\,\textdegree C at 105--130~Ma) give an age within
the measured range, and they fit worse ($\chi^2_\nu=6.2$).

\paragraph{Isothermal hold.} The spectrum constrains only the product of diffusivity and
time, so the $\chi^2$ minimum is a ridge from $\sim$47\,\textdegree C at 50~Ma to
$\sim$35\,\textdegree C at 500~Ma, along which $\chi^2_\nu\approx\MDDIsoChi$ throughout. No
isothermal path reproduces the shape of the spectrum: they underpredict the ratios of the
early steps and overpredict those of the late steps. We do not consider this family further.

\paragraph{Where the families differ.} The steady-cooling and burial predictions differ
mainly in the earliest steps ($\Sigma F_{3\mathrm{He}}\lesssim0.035$;
Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-mdd-spectrum-compare}), which sample the outer rim of the dominant domain. On
the burial path the sample sits at 20\,\textdegree C for the last 50~Myr, long enough for
radiogenic $^4$He to re-accumulate in the rim, so the early ratios are overpredicted. Steady
cooling keeps the rim depleted until geologically recent times, with diffusive loss
declining monotonically toward the present. Elsewhere both leave the same residual pattern:
ratios underpredicted between $\Sigma F_{3\mathrm{He}}=0.04$ and 0.06, and overpredicted in
the two longest high-temperature steps.

\paragraph{Consistency with diffusive loss.} These two families of thermal paths encode very different geologic origins. We 
note that any number of arbitrarily complex thermal paths can be evaluated against the data with 
this workflow, but a thorough search for viable thermal paths is beyond the scope 
of this study, and is an ill-posed problem for a single sample. The main purpose of this exercise 
is to demonstrate that the data we present here are consistent with diffusive loss of 
radiogenic helium in nature. We do not claim to have found the unique thermal history of the sample, but 
have demonstrated that plausible thermal histories reproduce our results reasonably well.

\section{Conclusions}\label{conclusions}

Predicting the degree of helium retention and loss to guide
interpretation of U-Th/He dates of natural goethite based on factors
such as grain size, crystallinity, radiation damage, defect density,
impurities, and aluminum substitution remains challenging. Previous
efforts to understand helium loss in natural goethite with step heating
experiments in vacuum have instead queried the kinetics of dehydration
\citep{farley2024}. Because goethite dehydration is suppressed at high water pressure, 
helium step heats under hydrothermal
conditions are more likely to interrogate the processes controlling
radiogenic helium loss in natural goethite. The method
presented in this study leverages the high helium permeability of silica glass to easily
conduct helium step heat experiments under water-saturated conditions,
achieving internal water pressures up to 86 bars at 300°C using standard
ultra-high vacuum experimental setups \citep{farley1999}. Natural crystalline goethite does not develop the
visible dehydration-induced contraction cracks when heated at water-saturated conditions like vacuum-heated goethite does. 

The experiment conducted on irradiated Win-06-01b represents the first helium step heat experiment for goethite at water-saturated conditions. 
Vacuum step heats of the same sample follow first-order conversion to hematite, whereas the hydrothermal release shows the history and time
dependence of diffusion. The hydrothermal experiment gives apparent
diffusivities approximately seven natural log units below those obtained
from the same sample in vacuum. The corrected \textsuperscript{3}He array is linear between
7--15\% cumulative yield, with an activation energy of \ChTwoEa ±
\ChTwoEaErr kJ/mol. Extrapolated to surface conditions, a domain with these
parameters is essentially quantitatively retentive of helium at
billion-year timescales at 25°C. The \textsuperscript{4}He data require that this domain
experienced helium loss in nature. A four-domain diffusion model fitted to the $^3$He release
shows that 97\% of the $^3$He resides in a single domain and can account for this loss by
thermally activated diffusion alone. Within that model, steady cooling from crystallization at
76--80\,\textdegree C at 150--175~Ma to the present surface temperature fits the
$^4$He/$^3$He spectrum best, favoring crystallization from warm, possibly hydrothermal, fluids.
Burial of surface-formed goethite to a Late Cretaceous peak of 58--65\,\textdegree C, consistent
with regional apatite fission-track constraints, remains a viable alternative.

The \textsuperscript{4}He/\textsuperscript{3}He spectrum observed for this sample is distinct from that of the
same sample in vacuum, suggesting that the geometry of helium loss induced
by goethite dehydration is distinct from that of diffusion. More
hydrothermal step heat experiments of goethite are necessary to confirm
this hypothesis.

A thermodynamic and kinetic analysis of goethite dehydroxylation explains why water seems to stabilize goethite up to 250°C
at experimental timescales. The hydrothermal release itself requires solid-state conversion to be at least
$10^{2.5}$ times slower in water than in vacuum. At water saturation, the roughly tenfold smaller
driving force raises the barrier to hematite nucleation enough to suppress solid-state conversion
far below this limit. Fluid-mediated
conversion, which transformed synthetic goethite pigment in similar conditions, is rapid and would be accompanied by abrupt and extreme helium loss, which is not observed in the hydrothermal step heat data.
This divergent outcome may be explained by different solution chemistry between the two experiments, which would affect the 
availability of aqueous Fe(III) that supplies the reaction.
Recrystallization, however, cannot be excluded.
If it contributed, the diffusivities reported here would be upper
bounds and the retentivity a lower bound. Distinguishing these
possibilities, and separating the effects of crystallite size, chemical
composition, and radiation damage, will require further hydrothermal
experiments on irradiated single-crystal goethite of controlled size. The
method described here makes such experiments straightforward.
