\section{Permeation Lag Correction and Ampoule Permeability}\label{sec:ch2-si-permeation}

\paragraph{Permeation Lag Correction}\label{permeation-lag-correction}

Because HT-Win-06-01b was encapsulated in an imperfectly
helium-transmissive silica glass ampoule, permeation lag was an expected complication in these
measurements. To address this, experimental steps at temperatures at or above 170°C were bracketed by low temperature
(\textless120°C) steps where the helium in goethite at high pH2O was
effectively immobile at experimental timescales, following the permeation step method (Chapter~\ref{ch:apparatus}, \S\ref{permeation-step-method}). For 3He, measured
signals at these low temperature permeation steps were entirely
controlled by the process of permeation through silica glass, and
therefore could be used to estimate the amount of helium remaining within the
ampoule between high-temperature experimental steps. However, for 4He,
the measured signals at low temperature permeation steps were most often
indistinguishable from the blank, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:n0perm} (Chapter~\ref{ch:apparatus}) could not be
applied to 4He and the permeation step method could not be carried through for that isotope.

As a result, permeation lag is instead addressed using the bounding method described in Chapter~\ref{ch:apparatus} (\S\ref{bounding-method}), which constrains the ampoule's residual helium from above rather than measuring it.
In this approach, an upper bound constraint for the helium content of the ampoule at the end of each high-temperature step was
calculated using Eq.~\eqref{eq:steadystate} (Chapter~\ref{ch:apparatus}). 3He was reduced this way as well, for consistency with 4He; because 3He can also be reduced by the permeation step method, the two reductions can be compared directly, and that comparison is used below (\S\ref{complete-degas-test}) as an internal test of the bounding method.

Both routes require the ampoule's permeation rate constant $\lambda$, which was measured during the experiment itself rather than assumed from the literature (Chapter~\ref{ch:apparatus}, \S\ref{in-situ-rate-constant}). This in-experiment measurement is not an alternative method of handling permeation lag but a prerequisite of the permeation step method and, here, the empirical basis for choosing a conservative $\lambda$ for the bounding method. It is indispensable in this experiment: the permeability of ampoules used in hydrothermal step heats
cannot reasonably be assumed to match literature values for anhydrous silica glass.

10 series of 3-4 consecutive permeation steps held at a single
temperature between 90-110°C for 120-180 minutes each with 26 minutes of
cooling time before being inlet into the Helix-MC-Plus10k were
identified as suitable for this exercise. The 3He measurements in these
series were fit to Eq.~\eqref{eq:incyield} using a non-linear least
squares optimization (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-permeation-fits}). This is accomplished with
the Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm implemented in
the curve\_fit function in the SciPy package of Python
\citep{virtanen2020}, weighted by each measurement's own uncertainty.
One additional series that otherwise qualified was not well fit by this
model and was not used further for clarity, though its fit value of
$\lambda$ was within uncertainty of the other series' fit values for
$\lambda$.

An initial guess of $\lambda$ $\approx$ 6x10\textsuperscript{-6}
sec\textsuperscript{-1} and \(n_{0} \approx \frac{m_{i}}{1 - e^{- \lambda t_{i}}}\)
(evaluated at the first measurement of a series) pccSTP was passed to
the optimizer for each series. Uncertainties from the model fits were
calculated using the covariance matrices generated for each of the model
fits. Although the initial helium amount \(n_{0}\) was a free parameter
in the optimizations, the rate constant of permeation ($\lambda$) was
the only parameter of interest for this exercise. The fitted rate constants span
\ChTwoPermLamMin\ to \ChTwoPermLamMax\ s\textsuperscript{-1}, with 2$\sigma$ uncertainties of
0.08 to 0.46 log units and no trend resolvable against step duration:

All measured apparent 3He permeabilities were equal to or in excess of the literature values for anhydrous silica glass (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-ampoule-permeability}),
similar to observations discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:apparatus} (\S\ref{resolving-the-paradoxically-high-apparent-helium-permeability-in-water-filled-tubes}). 4He permeability itself could not be independently measured in this
experiment due to blank-level signals during the low temperature permeation steps and is assumed to similarly exceed the literature values for anhydrous silica glass.
To evaluate the uncertainty due to the permeation lag, we use the literature values as a conservative (lower bound) estimate of the true permeability; a $\lambda$ that underestimates the ampoule's true rate constant yields a conservative (higher) upper bound on the residual helium (Chapter~\ref{ch:apparatus}, \S\ref{bounding-method}).
$\lambda t$ (where $\lambda = GRTK$) is estimated here using the 4He
permeability \emph{K} reported by \citet{swets1961}, while the 3He permeability
is taken to be the product of the 4He permeability and the relative 3He/4He permeability reported by \citet{jones1953}. The geometric constant \emph{G} is calculated using the measured inner and
outer diameter of the ampoule (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-ampoule-dims}).

The first three experimental steps had values of $\lambda t$ lower than 4, and therefore
the upper bound of the helium content of these steps could not be determined. These measurements are omitted
from further analysis. Later experimental steps with values of $\lambda t$ lower than 4 could be determined
using Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-8} from the supplement to Chapter~\ref{ch:apparatus} because upper bounds on previous steps' residual helium
content could be evaluated.

\paragraph{Measurement and Numerical Simulation of Apparent Permeability of Experimental Ampoule}\label{internal-measurement-of-ampoule-apparent-permeability}

The apparent permeability values used in the numerical simulation (\S\ref{finite-difference-diffusion-model-of-cooling-tube}) are taken from the literature \citep{swets1961,jones1953} and explicitly apply to
an ampoule without water. However, the purpose of these simulations was simply
to quantify the excess apparent permeabilities that would be observed for ampoules
with a cooling history similar to that of the hydrothermal experiments. Because
the predicted offset is universally small and a weak function of temperature or
permeability, these simulations demonstrate that the much larger experimentally observed permeability
is not an artifact of the cooling history of the hydrothermal experiments, reaffirming the conclusion
that the hydrothermal experiments are indeed more permeable than the vacuum experiments.

The difference in log permeability between the actual and apparent
permeabilities of the anhydrous simulation were significantly smaller
than nearly all the 2$\sigma$ uncertainties of the apparent permeabilities
determined above for the hydrothermal Win-06-01b experiment, which
ranged from 0.08 to 0.46 log units. Thus, no trends with step duration could be identified in the
effective permeability values. 

Therefore, the literature values underestimate the helium
permeabilities of the ampoule used in this experiment, which is permissible because these values are used
to calculate an upper bound constraint of the helium content of the ampoule, and lower permeabilities produce more
conservative (higher) upper bound constraints.

A linear regression of ln($K$) against inverse temperature (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-ampoule-permeability}) fit to these isothermal
3He permeation measurements over 90-110°C yields an R\textsuperscript{2} of \ChTwoPermRtwo\ and an activation energy of
\ChTwoPermEakJ$\pm$\ChTwoPermEaErrkJ\ kJ/mol (2 s.e.), significantly higher than expected for pure silica glass with or without water
\citep{swets1961,jones1953,masaryk1968,shelby1972}, but similar to glasses with a high abundance of network-modifying cations such as
Na, K, Pb, or Li \citep{norton1953,altemose1961}. Because 4He signals during these isothermal steps were blank-level,
only the ampoule's 3He permeability could be measured directly this way, using the fitting procedure described above.

Every 3He permeability measurement for HT-Win-06-01b equals or exceeds the anhydrous literature value, which
runs counter to expectations for a hydrothermal experiment: water and hydroxyl groups dissolved in silica glass are
reported to \emph{decrease} helium permeation at all temperatures \citep{tsuru2011,shelby1972,masaryk1968}, under the
common paradigm that species such as (OH)\textsuperscript{-} occupy interstitial space within the amorphous glassy
network \citep{norton1953,shackelford1999}. The offset is nonetheless real: it cannot be accounted for by the ampoule's
extended permeation during cooling before measurement (\S\ref{finite-difference-diffusion-model-of-cooling-tube}), and a
similarly elevated permeability was independently observed at 100°C in a separate silica glass ampoule at Caltech
partially filled with MilliQ water and equilibrated with \textasciitilde0.1 torr of tank helium, as discussed in
Chapter~\ref{ch:apparatus} (\S\ref{resolving-the-paradoxically-high-apparent-helium-permeability-in-water-filled-tubes}).

The likely explanation is geometric. Helium is only sparingly soluble in water and diffuses through it rapidly,
roughly $7\times10^{-5}$ cm\textsuperscript{2}/s at room temperature \citep{verhallen1984} against about
$2\times10^{-8}$ cm\textsuperscript{2}/s in silica glass \citep{swets1961}. On the timescale of permeation through
the glass wall, transport through the water is effectively instantaneous, so the gas phase stays in equilibrium with
the glass over the entire inner surface of the ampoule, including where liquid water contacts it. Helium partitions
strongly into the gas phase over both water and glass: helium concentrations in the glass and water are about 3\% and
1\%, respectively, of the gas-phase concentration, using solubilities from \citet{sander2015} and \citet{swets1961}.
Displacing gas-phase volume with water therefore raises the helium partial pressure without changing the surface area
available for permeation, and because the rate of permeation is proportional to the gas-phase partial pressure
\citep{norton1953,altemose1961,swets1961,masaryk1968}, the ampoule's rate constant scales with the inverse volume
fraction of the gas phase (Chapter~\ref{ch:apparatus}, Eq.~\eqref{eq:keff}), generalized here to any condensed payload:
$\lambda_{ampoule} = \lambda_{empty}\left(V_{condensed} + V_{gas}\right)/V_{gas}$. This observed complexity emphasizes
the need to measure the ampoule's rate constant in-experiment as described in Chapter~\ref{ch:apparatus}
(\S\ref{in-situ-rate-constant}).

\paragraph{Consistency of the Bounding Method with Direct Permeation-Step Quantification: The Complete Degas Test}\label{complete-degas-test}

All Win-06-01b step heat data presented in this chapter are reduced using the bounding method (Eq.~\eqref{eq:steadystate}), because 4He permeation-step yields were near blank level and could not support the permeation step method directly. As an internal check on this choice, the ampoule's 3He inventory was also reconstructed directly from the permeation steps themselves, using the in-experiment rate constants of Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-ampoule-permeability} (\S\ref{sec:ch2-si-detailed-methods}). Reducing 3He by the bounding method gives Stage 1 activation energies between 157.1 and 160.6\,kJ/mol and closure temperatures between 97.5 and 101.2\textdegree C across its lower-bound, central, and upper-bound reductions, a spread comparable to the regression uncertainty itself. Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-nt-vs-ntss} compares the two: $n_t$, the ampoule's residual helium quantified directly from a permeation step, against $n_{t,SS}$ (equivalent to $n_{t,\mathrm{UB}}$ used elsewhere in this chapter), the upper bound on that same residual calculated independently from the preceding high-temperature step. For every ordinary permeation step in the experiment, $n_t \leq n_{t,SS}$, exactly as expected if the steady-state approximation genuinely bounds the ampoule's residual helium from above. This agreement is the justification for reducing the 4He dataset by the bounding method alone, since no analogous direct check of 4He is possible.

Beyond the ampoule's residual helium content itself, the two methods' derived apparent diffusivities agree just as closely (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-diffusivity-comparison}, \S\ref{sec:ch2-si-detailed-methods}): $\ln(D/a^2)$ from the permeation step method tracks the bounding method's central value along the 1:1 line within uncertainty across the full range of the experiment. Fitting the permeation step method's own diffusivities with the same Stage 1 procedure used for the bounding method in the main text (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-permstep-arrhenius-zoom}, \S\ref{sec:ch2-si-detailed-methods}) yields an activation energy of \ChTwoPermStepEa$\pm$\ChTwoPermStepEaErr\ kJ/mol (R\textsuperscript{2}=\ChTwoPermStepRtwo, n=\ChTwoPermStepN) and a closure temperature of \ChTwoPermStepTc$\pm$\ChTwoPermStepTcErr\textdegree C, indistinguishable from the bounding method's own \ChTwoEa$\pm$\ChTwoEaErr\ kJ/mol and \ChTwoTc$\pm$\ChTwoTcErr\textdegree C (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-arrhenius-zoom}).

The two ``complete degas'' permeation steps depart from this pattern. For the longer of the two (7200 minutes; predicted $n_t/n_0 \approx 1\times10^{-8}$ under simple first-order decay), the directly measured 3He inventory exceeds $n_{t,SS}$ by a factor of 1.5. For the shorter (1580 minutes; predicted $n_t/n_0 \approx 0.02$), the excess is smaller, 25\% over $n_{t,SS}$. A measured inventory in excess of $n_{t,SS}$ requires that the source term ($\Omega$: \S\ref{bounding-method}) increased during the high temperature experimental step, or the goethite sample was not effectively closed to helium loss during the low temperature permeation step (\S\ref{permeation-step-method}), or both. The measured diffusivitiy is constant at a given temperature for these high temperature measurements at the end of the experiment (Figs.~\ref{fig:ch2-arrhenius-zoom},~\ref{fig:ch2-delta-zoom}), so there is no evidence the source term ($\Omega$) is increasing during the high temperature measurements.

The size of the two excesses scale with duration of their respective permeation step. The 7200-minute step is associated with the larger (1.5$\times$) excess whereas the 1580-minute step is associated with the smaller (25\%) excess. An artifact intrinsic to the the first order process of helium permeation through the ampoule itself, such as an underestimated rate constant, has no reason to strongly scale with the step duration, since both of these long steps would be expected empty the vast majority of the helium inventory of the ampoule. That the excess does scale this way points to open system behavior in the sample. The preferred explanation is short-circuit or fast pathway diffusion \citep{lee1992} of residual helium held within the interstitial space of the goethite crystallite aggregate (\S\ref{consistency-with-mdd-systematics}), recharged during the preceding high-temperature step, released slowly and with weak temperature dependence during the following extended low-temperature hold. This requires communication between a lattice or grain-boundary source and an interstitial reservoir, a departure from the independent, non-communicating domains of a strict MDD interpretation, but it is consistent with the interstitial framework already invoked to explain the early, low-retention fraction of 3He release (\S\ref{consistency-with-mdd-systematics}). Unlike dehydration, it requires no change in the intrinsic retentivity of the crystalline lattice recorded by the bulk of the experiment.

\section{Analytical Methods}\label{ch2-analytical-methods}

\paragraph{Finite Difference Diffusion Model of Cooling Tube}\label{finite-difference-diffusion-model-of-cooling-tube}

One potential concern about the results of the internally measured permeabilities is that some additional helium was permeated 
during the cooling period after each permeation step. In order to evaluate the significance of 
this effect, a finite difference model was built in Python to simulate diffusion, influx, and
outflux of helium across a hollow glass cylinder containing an
arbitrary amount of helium gas within a helium-free chamber using the procedure described in Chapter~\ref{ch:apparatus} (\S\ref{numerical-diffusive-permeation-model-for-arbitrary-thermally-history-and-diffusive-disequilibrium}). The glass
cylinder was given the same dimensions of the ampoule used in the
Win-06-01b hydrothermal experiment, and was subjected to the same
heating and cooling schedule as the hydrothermal experiment's permeation
steps. The tube was charged with 100 arbitrary units of helium atoms at
the end of each experimental step and a steady state concentration
profile was assumed for the new arbitrary charge at T = 24°. Permeability
values for 3He were assumed as the product of the 4He permeability
values given by \citet{swets1961} and the 3He/4He relative
permeabilities given by \citet{jones1953}.

To simulate cooling, a cooling rate had to be assumed. To most
accurately simulate the cooling of the experimental ampoule, Watlow temperature records
of the experimental ampoule during cooling steps were used to calculate the cooling rate dT/dt for discrete time intervals.
A quadratic function was fit to these data points to model the cooling rate as a function of temperature with a single root at 
T = 24°C such that the temperature would stabilize at T = 24°C by design.
\[
\frac{dT}{dt} = -\left(9.23081\times10^{-6}\right)(T-24)^{2} - \left(7.74304\times10^{-4}\right)(T-24)
\]
(dT/dt in \textdegree C/s, T in \textdegree C; \(R^{2} = 0.9633\)).

\paragraph{Helium-in-Goethite Diffusivity Inversions}\label{helium-in-goethite-diffusivity-inversions}


Apparent values of D/a\textsuperscript{2} for 3He and 4He were
calculated from times and cumulative measured yields using the equations of \citet{fechtigkalbitzer1966}. 
Lower and upper bounds for diffusivity were calculated 
using the upper bound for the helium contents of the 
ampoules before and after each step, evaluated using \eqref{eq:steadystate}. 
Diffusivity for step $i$ is a function of the cumulative fractional yield for step i 
$\Sigma ^{i}F$ and the cumulative fractional yield of the previous step $\Sigma ^{i-1}F$. The diffusivity
is maximized when the difference between the fractional yields is largest, and minimized when the difference is smallest. 
This difference is maximized for the extreme case where the previous step ended with no permeation lag, and the current step ended 
with the maximum amount of permeation lag $\left(_{SS}^{i}n_t\right)$, whereas the opposite is true for the minimum diffusivity. 


\begin{equation}
\label{eq:diffusivity-bounds}
\begin{gathered}
{}^{UB}D_\omega = D\!\left(\Sigma^{i-1}F_m ,~ \Sigma^{i}F_m + {}_{SS}^{i}n_t\right) \\
{}^{LB}D_\omega = D\!\left(\Sigma^{i-1}F_m + {}_{SS}^{i-1}n_t ,~ \Sigma^{i}F_m\right)
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}

For the 3He diffusivities, a linear regression was fit to the steps with cumulative 3He fractional
yield $\geq$7\% (\ChTwoN\ steps, Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-arrhenius-zoom}),
yielding an R\textsuperscript{2} statistic of \ChTwoRtwo. The fit's slope and intercept are used as a reference line to
calculate ``Delta'' values for the diffusivities of 3He and 4He (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-delta-zoom}). No uncertainty on
the total amounts of 3He and 4He is propagated into the diffusivities
because the experiment operated at low cumulative yields where this
error affects all diffusivities approximately homogeneously, so it is only
relevant for the y-intercept of the regression and the corresponding
calculated closure temperature. The uncertainty from the
intercept and slope parameters of the linear regression is significantly
larger than the uncertainty related to the total yield, so it is
neglected.

\paragraph{Helium Isotope Ratio Uncertainty for Step Heats}\label{helium-isotope-ratio-uncertainty}

The upper and lower bounds of the $\frac{^4He}{^3He}$ isotope ratios for each heating step are taken as the maximum and minimum  $\frac{^4He}{^3He}$ 
isotope ratio calculated out of the 4 hypothetical end-member permeation scenarios, which would have been experienced by both helium isotope systems:

\begin{enumerate}
\item $n_t = 0,\ n_0 = 0$
\item $n_t = n_t^{\mathrm{UB}},\ n_0 = 0$
\item $n_t = n_t^{\mathrm{UB}},\ n_0 = n_0^{\mathrm{UB}}$
\item $n_t = 0,\ n_0 = n_0^{\mathrm{UB}}$
\end{enumerate}

Intuitively, higher differential permeability between the two helium isotopes ($\delta = \frac{K_{3He}}{K_{4He}}$ here) will result in greater isotope fractionation between the measured $\left(\frac{m_{4}}{m_{3}}\right)$ and true $\left(\frac{\omega_{4}}{\omega_{3}}\right)$.
Furthermore, lower absolute permeabilities will result in stronger permeation lag, additionally producing higher isotope fractionation in the measured value. The 4He permeability is thus conservatively taken to be identical to \citet{swets1961}, an anhydrous
literature value which this experiment's own isothermal 3He permeation measurements show to underestimate the ampoule's true permeability (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch2-ampoule-permeability}); 4He permeability itself could not be independently measured in this experiment. For the purposes of evaluating the measured isotope ratios, the relative 3He/4He permeability ($\delta$) is taken to be 1.29, the 2$\sigma$ upper bound of the value measured independently for a water-filled ampoule in Chapter~\ref{ch:apparatus} (\S\ref{results}):
1.10 $\pm$ 0.19 (2$\sigma$). 1.29 is in great excess of realistic values of the relative 3He/4He permeability, which is expected to be between 1.06-1.11 for the temperature range relevant to this experiment \citep{jones1953}. These
conservative assumptions taken together guarantee that the calculated upper and lower bounds for the isotope ratios accurately constrain the true value. The upper bounds of the
ampoule contents are calculated using the steady-state approximation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:steadystate} (Chapter~\ref{ch:apparatus}): $n_{ss} = \frac{m}{\lambda t - 1}$. For the four extreme cases listed above, the following equations apply:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \scalebox{1.5}{$\frac{\omega_{4}}{\omega_{3}} = \frac{m_{4}}{m_{3}}$}
\item \scalebox{1.5}{$\frac{\omega_{4}}{\omega_{3}} = \frac{m_{4}+\frac{m_{4}}{\lambda t - 1}}{m_{3}+\frac{m_{3}}{\delta \lambda t - 1}}$}
\item \scalebox{1.5}{$\frac{\omega_{4}}{\omega_{3}} = \frac{m_{4}+\frac{m_{4}}{\lambda t - 1} - \frac{m_4^{i-1}}{\left(\lambda t\right)^{i-1} - 1}}{m_{3}+\frac{m_{3}}{\delta \lambda t - 1} - \frac{m_3^{i-1}}{\delta\left(\lambda t\right)^{i-1} - 1}}$}
\item \scalebox{1.5}{$\frac{\omega_{4}}{\omega_{3}} = \frac{m_{4} - \frac{m_4^{i-1}}{\left(\lambda t\right)^{i-1} - 1}}{m_{3} - \frac{m_3^{i-1}}{\delta\left(\lambda t\right)^{i-1} - 1}}$}
\end{enumerate}

Where $m_{x}^{i-1}$ and $\left(\lambda t \right)^{i-1}$ refer to the measured helium content and $\lambda t$ of the previous step, respectively. $\lambda$ here applies explicitly to 4He, whereas $\delta \lambda$ applies to 3He. The upper and lower bounds of the isotope ratios are then taken to be the maximum and minimum of these four extreme cases.

\section{Equilibrium Thermodynamics of Goethite Dehydroxylation}
\label{sec:eqm-thermo}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

\textit{Gibbs energies of the solids.} The Gibbs energy of a phase at $(T,P)$ follows from
integrating $dG = -S\,dT + V\,dP$ from the reference state $(T_r,P_r) = (298.15~\mathrm{K}, 1~\mathrm{bar})$,
first in temperature at $P_r$ and then in pressure at $T$. At $P_r$,
$H(T) = H(T_r) + \int_{T_r}^{T}C_p\,dT$ and $S(T) = S(T_r) + \int_{T_r}^{T}(C_p/T)\,dT$, so
$G(T,P_r) = H(T) - T S(T)$. Adding $\int_{P_r}^{P}V\,dP$, and treating the solids as incompressible
with no thermal expansion, gives
\begin{equation}
  G_i(T,P) = \Delta_f H_i^{0} + \int_{T_r}^{T} C_{p,i}\,dT
  \;-\; T\left[S_i^{0} + \int_{T_r}^{T}\frac{C_{p,i}}{T}\,dT\right]
  \;+\; V_i\,(P-P_r),
  \label{eq:gt_Gsolid}
\end{equation}
where we have set $H(T_r) = \Delta_f H_i^{0}$, the enthalpy of formation from the elements, and
$S(T_r) = S_i^{0}$, the third-law entropy. This is the ``apparent'' Gibbs energy of formation
\citep{berman1988}; see also \citet{helgeson1978} and \citet{anderson2005}. It differs from
the true Gibbs energy of formation,
$\Delta_f G_i = \Delta_f H_i - T(S_i - \sum_e n_{e,i} S_e)$, by the term $T\sum_e n_{e,i}S_e$,
where $n_{e,i}$ is the number of atoms of element $e$ in phase $i$ and $S_e$ the entropy of the
element in its reference state. For any balanced reaction $\sum_i \nu_i n_{e,i} = 0$ for every element,
so these terms cancel in $\Delta G_r$. The incompressible approximation affects $\Delta G_r$
through $\Delta V_s (P-P_r)$ with $\Delta V_s = V_{\mathrm{hem}} - 2V_{\mathrm{goe}} = -11.5$~cm$^3$,
i.e.\ at most a few kJ below 1~GPa; compressibility and thermal expansion change $V_i$ by
$\lesssim1\%$ over the conditions considered and are neglected.

Goethite heat capacity uses the Berman--Brown form \citep{berman1985} refined by
\citet{majzlan2003},
\begin{equation}
  C_p = k_0 + k_1 T^{-1/2} + k_2 T^{-2} + k_3 T^{-3},
\end{equation}
which integrates to
\begin{align}
  \int_{T_r}^{T} C_p\,dT &= k_0 (T-T_r) + 2k_1\left(T^{1/2}-T_r^{1/2}\right) \notag\\
    &\quad - k_2\left(T^{-1}-T_r^{-1}\right) - \tfrac{1}{2}k_3\left(T^{-2}-T_r^{-2}\right), \\
  \int_{T_r}^{T} \frac{C_p}{T}\,dT &= k_0 \ln\frac{T}{T_r} - 2k_1\left(T^{-1/2}-T_r^{-1/2}\right) \notag\\
    &\quad - \tfrac{1}{2}k_2\left(T^{-2}-T_r^{-2}\right) - \tfrac{1}{3}k_3\left(T^{-3}-T_r^{-3}\right).
\end{align}
Hematite uses a Maier--Kelley form, $C_p = a + bT + cT^{-2}$, with
\begin{align}
  \int_{T_r}^{T} C_p\,dT &= a(T-T_r) + \tfrac{1}{2}b\left(T^2-T_r^2\right) - c\left(T^{-1}-T_r^{-1}\right), \\
  \int_{T_r}^{T} \frac{C_p}{T}\,dT &= a\ln\frac{T}{T_r} + b(T-T_r) - \tfrac{1}{2}c\left(T^{-2}-T_r^{-2}\right).
\end{align}
% TODO: cite the source of the hematite Maier-Kelley coefficients (Kelley-type fit,
% cf. Robie & Hemingway 1995, USGS Bull. 2131); Majzlan et al. used Berman (1988). Differences are < 1 J/K/mol at 298 K.

\textit{Water.} Water is evaluated with the IAPWS-95 equation of state \citep{wagner2002},
implemented in CoolProp \citep{bell2014}, which is valid for liquid, vapor and supercritical
water to 1~GPa. In the same apparent convention,
\begin{equation}
  G_{\mathrm{w}}(T,P) = \Delta_f H_{\mathrm{w}}^{0} + \left[h(T,P) - h(T_r,P_r)\right]
  - T\left[S_{\mathrm{w}}^{0} + s(T,P) - s(T_r,P_r)\right],
  \label{eq:gt_Gw}
\end{equation}
where $h$ and $s$ are the IAPWS-95 molar enthalpy and entropy and the reference state is liquid
water at $T_r,P_r$. Table~\ref{tab:gt_thermo_data} lists all
input data. We use the enthalpies and entropies of \citet{majzlan2003} refined by mathematical
programming against the phase-equilibrium brackets of \citet{voigt1981}.

As a check, at 298.15~K and 1~bar with liquid water, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:gt_dGr}--\eqref{eq:gt_Gw}
give $\Delta G_r = -0.26$~kJ: hematite plus liquid water is favored by only 0.13~kJ per mole of
FeOOH. This is well inside the $\pm1.4$~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$ uncertainty that \citet{majzlan2003}
assign to the goethite--hematite free-energy difference; the two assemblages are essentially
balanced at ambient conditions. We carry this uncertainty ($\pm2.8$~kJ per reaction as written)
as bounding curves on the phase diagram.

\textit{Surface energy.} A crystal of finite size carries an excess free energy at its
surface in addition to the free energy of its bulk \citep{gibbs1878}. For a population of
particles of fixed size, this excess is $\gamma A$, where $\gamma$ is the surface energy
(J\,m$^{-2}$) and $A$ the total surface area. Both the bulk and surface terms scale with the
amount of material at fixed particle size, so the surface contribution can be written per mole,
$G = G_{\mathrm{bulk}} + \gamma A_m$, with $A_m$ the surface area per mole. $A_m$ grows as $1/d$,
so the term is negligible for coarse crystals and significant for nanoparticles
\citep{navrotsky2008}. For the reaction as written (one mole of hematite from two moles of
goethite),
\begin{equation}
  \Delta G_{\mathrm{surf}} = \gamma_{\mathrm{hem}} A_{m,\mathrm{hem}} - 2\,\gamma_{\mathrm{goe}} A_{m,\mathrm{goe}},
  \label{eq:gt_dGsurf}
\end{equation}
where $A_{m,\mathrm{hem}}$ is per mole of Fe$_2$O$_3$ and $A_{m,\mathrm{goe}}$ per mole of FeOOH.

A size must be chosen for each phase. We assume pseudomorphic dehydroxylation, in which hematite
inherits the external envelope of the parent goethite, so that both phases share the same
external surface area per mole of Fe. This is also the basis of the stability diagrams of
\citet{navrotsky2008}. For spherical goethite grains of diameter $d$ this shared area is
\begin{equation}
  A_{\mathrm{Fe}} = \frac{6\,V_{\mathrm{goe}}}{d}\quad\text{per mole of Fe}.
\end{equation}
Goethite has one Fe per formula unit, so $A_{m,\mathrm{goe}} = A_{\mathrm{Fe}}$. Hematite has two,
so $A_{m,\mathrm{hem}} = 2A_{\mathrm{Fe}}$. Substituting into Eq.~\eqref{eq:gt_dGsurf},
\begin{equation}
  \Delta G_{\mathrm{surf}} = \gamma_{\mathrm{hem}}\,(2A_{\mathrm{Fe}}) - 2\,\gamma_{\mathrm{goe}}\,A_{\mathrm{Fe}}
  = 2A_{\mathrm{Fe}}\left(\gamma_{\mathrm{hem}} - \gamma_{\mathrm{goe}}\right).
  \label{eq:gt_equalarea}
\end{equation}
The factor of two counts the two moles of Fe that pass through the reaction. It multiplies both
terms because both phases carry the same area per Fe. For $d=10$~nm,
$A_{\mathrm{Fe}} = 6\times20.881\times10^{-6}/10^{-8} = 1.253\times10^{4}$~m$^2$\,mol$^{-1}$, and
with hydrated surface energies
$\Delta G_{\mathrm{surf}} = 2\times1.253\times10^{4}\times(0.75-0.60) = +3.76$~kJ.
The corresponding values are $+0.38$~kJ at 100~nm and $+0.04$~kJ at 1~\textmu m. A positive
$\Delta G_{\mathrm{surf}}$ stabilizes goethite. The result depends on the choice of basis: at
equal diameter the same hydrated surface energies destabilize fine goethite
(\S\ref{sec:gt_supp_basis}).

We use hydrated surface energies. They are referenced to liquid water, so they apply wherever the
surfaces are in equilibrium with water at or near saturation, which includes all of the
hydrothermal experiments. Anhydrous surface energies apply only to surfaces that have lost their
chemisorbed water, i.e.\ under vacuum.

\textit{Phase diagram.} The phase diagram (Fig.~\ref{fig:gt_PT}) is computed by solving
$\Delta G_r(T,P) = 0$ (Eqs.~\eqref{eq:gt_dGr}--\eqref{eq:gt_equalarea}) numerically for
$P = P_{\mathrm{H_2O}}$ at each temperature, using Brent's method on $\log_{10}P$ between
$10^{-12}$ and $10^{4}$~bar, with the solids at the fluid pressure. Where no root exists below the
melting curve of ice, the curve is truncated.

\textit{Driving force and the role of water.} The quantity that matters for kinetics is the
driving force for hematite formation, $\Delta\mu \equiv -\Delta G_r/N_A$ (per mole of Fe$_2$O$_3$).
Water controls $\Delta\mu$ through its own chemical potential, because it is a product of
reaction~\eqref{eq:gt_rxn}. $G_{\mathrm{w}}$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:gt_dGr} is the chemical potential of
H$_2$O at the prevailing water fugacity. It depends on the partial pressure of water, not on the
total pressure. An inert gas, or any other mechanical load, acts only through the solid-volume
term $\Delta V_s(P-P_r)$, which contributes $-1.15$~J\,bar$^{-1}$ and slightly favors the denser
hematite. At 40~bar that is 46~J, three orders of magnitude smaller than the effect of 40~bar of
water. The water-buffered step-heating technique therefore stabilizes goethite primarily by
reducing the driving force of the reaction. Surface hydration is a second-order correction that matters
only for nanometer grains (Eq.~\eqref{eq:gt_equalarea}).


\section{Kinetic Analysis of Vacuum Step Heats}\label{sec:gt_kinetics_methods}

Two published vacuum step heats of irradiated Win-06-01b are reanalyzed here. The experiment of
\citet{farley2024} (hereafter KAF) used 1~h steps from 70 to 330~$^\circ$C, including isothermal
repeats at 210, 230 and 240~$^\circ$C. Its extraction line was open to a liquid-nitrogen-cooled
charcoal trap, so evolved water did not accumulate, and the water released in each step was
measured by capacitance manometry. The experiment of \citet{monteiro2017} (hereafter HDM) used
a different schedule: prograde to 238~$^\circ$C, retrograde to 180~$^\circ$C, then prograde
again, with 0.5~h steps above 238~$^\circ$C, to 498~$^\circ$C. Water could accumulate in the
HDM line. For each experiment, the cumulative fraction of $^3$He released after step $i$ is
\begin{equation}
  F_i = \frac{\sum_{j\le i} {}^3\mathrm{He}_j}{\sum_{\mathrm{steps}} {}^3\mathrm{He} + {}^3\mathrm{He}_{\mathrm{fusion}}} .
\end{equation}

We describe conversion by a first-order rate law,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\alpha}{dt} = k(T)\,(1-\alpha),
  \qquad
  k(T) = A\exp\!\left(-\frac{E_a}{RT}\right),
  \label{eq:gt_F1}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is the fraction of goethite converted. For a powder of many grains, first-order
kinetics is the limit in which each grain converts rapidly once a hematite nucleus forms, so
that the conversion rate is set by the nucleation rate per untransformed grain
\citep{mampel1940}. $k$ is then a per-grain nucleation rate. We assume $^3$He is released when
its host grain converts, except for a fraction $\phi$ that is inherited by the hematite
product:
\begin{equation}
  F = (1-\phi)\,\alpha .
  \label{eq:gt_Fphi}
\end{equation}
For a schedule of isothermal steps of duration $\Delta t_j$ at $T_j$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:gt_F1}
integrates to
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_i = 1 - \exp\!\left[-A\sum_{j\le i} m_j\,\Delta t_j \exp\!\left(-\frac{E_a}{RT_j}\right)\right],
  \label{eq:gt_F1_cum}
\end{equation}
where $m_j = s$ for the two KAF steps run with the trap isolated (\S\ref{sec:gt_vacuum}) and $m_j=1$
otherwise. Inverting Eq.~\eqref{eq:gt_F1} step by step gives an apparent rate constant for every
step that is independent of any fitted parameter except $\phi$:
\begin{equation}
  k_i = -\frac{1}{\Delta t_i}\ln\frac{1-\alpha_i}{1-\alpha_{i-1}},
  \qquad \alpha_i = \frac{F_i}{1-\phi}.
  \label{eq:gt_stepk}
\end{equation}
For comparison, the diffusion parameter of each step is obtained with the same
\citet{fechtigkalbitzer1966} inversion for a sphere that is applied to the hydrothermal
experiment (\S\ref{helium-in-goethite-diffusivity-inversions}),
\begin{equation}
  \frac{Dt}{a^2} =
  \begin{cases}
    \dfrac{1}{\pi^2}\left[2\pi - \dfrac{\pi^2}{3}F - 2\pi\sqrt{1-\dfrac{\pi F}{3}}\,\right], & F \le 0.85,\\[2ex]
    -\dfrac{1}{\pi^2}\ln\!\left[\dfrac{\pi^2}{6}(1-F)\right], & F > 0.85,
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:gt_FK}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation*}
  \left(\frac{D}{a^2}\right)_i = \frac{(Dt/a^2)_i - (Dt/a^2)_{i-1}}{\Delta t_i}.
\end{equation*}
For the hydrothermal experiment, $F_i$ and $D/a^2$ are always taken from the bounding-method
reduction (Table~\ref{tab:ch2-distilled-3he}): the cumulative release at the end of step $i$ is
bounded by the measured cumulative $^3$He and by that value plus $n_{t,\mathrm{UB}}$, and the
central value is their midpoint. As elsewhere in this chapter, steps without defined bounds and
steps with less than 0.1~pccSTP of measured $^3$He are excluded.

All steps are plotted, but fits use only KAF steps \ChTwoKinWinKAF{}
(\ChTwoKinWinKAFT~$^\circ$C) and HDM steps \ChTwoKinWinHDM{} (\ChTwoKinWinHDMT~$^\circ$C). KAF steps 1--4 and HDM steps 2--7
(below $\sim$180~$^\circ$C) release a small ($<2\%$), separate low-temperature component
(\S\ref{sec:gt_vacuum}). Later steps sit on the post-conversion plateau, where
$1-\alpha\to0$ and Eq.~\eqref{eq:gt_stepk} becomes controlled by the choice of $\phi$. The KAF
steps run with the trap isolated are excluded from the Arrhenius regression.

schedule this integrates to
\begin{equation}
  g(\alpha) \equiv \int_0^{\alpha}\frac{d\alpha'}{h(\alpha')} = x,
  \qquad

\section{Sensitivity of the size effect to the surface-area basis}
\label{sec:gt_supp_basis}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The sign of the surface stabilization depends on how the surface areas of the two phases are
related. At equal area per mole of Fe (Eq.~\eqref{eq:gt_equalarea}), goethite is stabilized
whenever $\gamma_{\mathrm{hem}}>\gamma_{\mathrm{goe}}$. If both phases are instead spheres of the
same diameter $d$, then $A_i = 6V_i/d$ and
\begin{equation}
  \Delta G_{\mathrm{surf}} = \frac{6}{d}\left(\gamma_{\mathrm{hem}}V_{\mathrm{hem}} - 2\gamma_{\mathrm{goe}}V_{\mathrm{goe}}\right),
\end{equation}
which stabilizes goethite only if
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{hem}}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{goe}}} > \frac{2V_{\mathrm{goe}}}{V_{\mathrm{hem}}} = \frac{2\times20.881}{30.274} = 1.379 .
\end{equation}
Hematite is denser and carries less surface per Fe at equal size. For hydrated surfaces,
$\gamma_{\mathrm{hem}}/\gamma_{\mathrm{goe}} = 0.75/0.60 = 1.25 < 1.379$, so fine goethite is
destabilized: at 10~nm,
$\Delta G_{\mathrm{surf}} = (6/10^{-8})(0.75\times30.274 - 2\times0.60\times20.881)\times10^{-6} = -1.41$~kJ.
For anhydrous surfaces, $1.90/0.91 = 2.09 > 1.379$ and $\Delta G_{\mathrm{surf}} = +11.7$~kJ at
10~nm. Table~\ref{tab:gt_basis} compares the two bases. The equal-area basis corresponds to
pseudomorphic conversion and underlies the main-text calculations. The equal-diameter basis is
more appropriate where hematite grows as separate crystals, as in fluid-mediated conversion
(\S\ref{sec:gt_pigment}). There the surface term slightly favors hematite, but it is
negligible at micrometer sizes.

\section{Nucleation barrier ratio and the calibration of $\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}$}
\label{sec:gt_supp_barrier}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

From Eq.~\eqref{eq:gt_barrier}, the ratio of barriers at water saturation and under vacuum at
the same temperature is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\Delta G^{*}_{\mathrm{sat}}}{\Delta G^{*}_{\mathrm{vac}}}
  = \left(\frac{\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{vac}}}{\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{sat}}}\right)^{2}
    \left(\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{sat}}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{vac}}}\right)^{3}.
\end{equation}
The prefactors, the temperature and the molar volume cancel. The first factor follows from the
thermodynamic data alone (Table~\ref{tab:gt_dmu}; 99 at 250~$^\circ$C for bulk goethite). The
second is 1 if nucleation occurs at the same internal interface in both settings. It is
$(0.75/1.90)^3 = 1/16$ in the extreme case that the relevant interface is a free hematite
surface, hydrated at saturation and anhydrous under vacuum. Figure~\ref{fig:gt_barrier_ratio}
shows the ratio between these bounds for bulk and 10~nm goethite. Even at the lower bound, the
saturated barrier is about six times the vacuum barrier at 250~$^\circ$C. Because the barrier
appears in an exponent, that is still a difference of many orders of magnitude in rate.

\section{Sensitivity to the water-inhibition rate law}
\label{sec:gt_supp_ratelaws}

The prediction of \S\ref{sec:gt_nucleation} extrapolates from the trap-isolated KAF steps, at a few
pascals of water, to water saturation, about six orders of magnitude higher in $P_{\mathrm{H_2O}}$. This
section tests how much of the result is set by the assumed rate law and propagates the calibration
uncertainties. 

\paragraph{Rate laws.} Each law gives the ratio $R(P) = k(P)/k(P_{\mathrm{on}})$ of the conversion rate at
water pressure $P$ to the rate in the trapped line, $P_{\mathrm{on}} = 10^{-8}$~atm, and has one free
parameter, fixed by requiring $R(P_{\mathrm{off}}) = s$ at a trap-isolated step:
\begin{align}
  \text{nucleation:}\quad & \ln R = -\frac{C\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}^{3}}{T}\left(\frac{1}{\Delta\mu(P)^{2}}-\frac{1}{\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{on}}^{2}}\right), \\
  \text{site blocking:}\quad & R = \frac{1+KP_{\mathrm{on}}}{1+KP}, \qquad K = \frac{1/s-1}{P_{\mathrm{off}}-P_{\mathrm{on}}/s}, \\
  \text{power law:}\quad & R = \left(\frac{P}{P_{\mathrm{on}}}\right)^{-n}, \qquad n = \frac{\ln(1/s)}{\ln(P_{\mathrm{off}}/P_{\mathrm{on}})} .
\end{align}
The Langmuir constant $K$ is held constant between the calibration temperatures (230 and
240~$^\circ$C) and the hydrothermal steps (170--250~$^\circ$C), because the data give no temperature
dependence; adsorption normally weakens with temperature, so this probably overstates site
blocking below 230~$^\circ$C. The power-law exponent depends on the assumed $P_{\mathrm{on}}$; a higher
background pressure in the trapped line gives a larger $n$ and more suppression. A rate linear in
the driving force, $k\propto1-\exp(-\Delta\mu/RT)$, has no free parameter: because $\Delta\mu\gg RT$
both with and without the trap, it predicts $s = \ChTwoKinLinearS$ and cannot reproduce the observed
slowdown. Each calibrated law is applied at $P = P_{\mathrm{sat}}(T_i)$ to every step of the hydrothermal
schedule, with $k_{\mathrm{vac}}(T_i)$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:gt_line}, and the conversion is
$\alpha = 1-\exp[-\sum_i k_{\mathrm{vac}}(T_i)R_i\,\Delta t_i]$.

\paragraph{Empirical bounds.} Over the hydrothermal schedule,
$\sum_i k_{\mathrm{vac}}(T_i)\Delta t_i = \ChTwoKinSumKdt$. For a suppression $R$ that is the same at every
step, the conversion is $1-\exp(-R\sum_i k_{\mathrm{vac}}\Delta t_i)$. If all of the observed $^3$He
release came from conversion, $\alpha = \ChTwoKinHTAlphaObs$ and $R = 10^{\ChTwoKinLogRObs}$; any
larger $R$ would release more $^3$He than was observed. Keeping conversion below 1\% requires
$R\le10^{\ChTwoKinLogRDetect}$. These bounds use only the vacuum kinetics and the hydrothermal
release, not a rate law.

\paragraph{Uncertainty propagation.} The Monte Carlo draws (\ChTwoKinNMC) sample: the trap-isolated
step (230 or 240~$^\circ$C, equally likely); $s$, log-uniformly between the extremes of six
reductions of that step (first-order and contracting-sphere step rate constants, each against the
mean of its two trapped neighbors and against each neighbor alone), \ChTwoKinSLowRange{} at
230~$^\circ$C and \ChTwoKinSHighRange{} at 240~$^\circ$C; the apparatus volume, log-uniformly between 190
and 600~mL; the time-averaged pressure, uniformly between half and all of the end-of-step value;
and, for the nucleation law only, a common offset of $\Delta G_r$, uniformly within
$\pm2.8$~kJ per reaction (the $\pm1.4$~kJ per mole FeOOH of \citealt{majzlan2003}), which shifts
every driving force together. The offset changes $\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}$ by only a few percent
(95\% range \ChTwoKinMCGammaLo--\ChTwoKinMCGammaHi~J\,m$^{-2}$) but enters $\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{sat}}$, which is
only 6--11~kJ, squared, and so contributes most of the spread in the nucleation prediction.
Figure~\ref{fig:gt_ratelaws} and Table~\ref{tab:gt_ratelaws} give the results.
