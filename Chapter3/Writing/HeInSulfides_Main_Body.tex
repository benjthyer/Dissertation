\section{Introduction}\label{sec:ch3-introduction}

Sulfide minerals are nearly ubiquitous in the formation histories of economic ore deposits, 
and indeed are themselves the primary repositories for many base and precious
metals in the crust. The question of when and how they formed is
central to understanding ore-forming processes, crustal fluid
circulation, and the tectonic contexts in which economic deposits
develop. Despite decades of study, the timing of sulfide mineralization
remains contested or poorly resolved across a wide range of deposit
types, from shallow epithermal systems to deep, high temperature
magmatic deposits in mafic intrusions. This uncertainty reflects both
the genuine complexity of ore-forming systems (which are frequently
multistage, hydrothermal, and overprinted) and the limitations of the
geochronological tools available for dating sulfide phases directly.

\subsection{An Alternative and Complement to Re-Os Dates in Sulfide
Geochronology}

The rhenium-osmium isotope system is the most widely applied method for
direct radiometric dating of sulfide minerals, exploiting the relatively
high Re concentrations that pyrite, molybdenite, arsenopyrite, and
pyrrhotite can accommodate. Re-Os geochronology has produced
geologically meaningful ages across a wide range of sulfide-bearing
deposit types, and remains indispensable for directly dating ore-forming
events \citep{chen2015,hnatyshin2026,schoenberg1999,ostendorf2019,selby2002}. Nevertheless, the
Re-Os system is vulnerable to a set of well-documented failure modes,
and a growing number of published ages exhibit scatter, implausible
geologic interpretations, or internal inconsistencies that motivate the
development of independent dating methods \citep{stein2003,suzuki2001,li2025,lyu2020}.

More practically, the highly specialized and costly n-TIMS method for
Re-Os dating restricts its application to a select few labs, limiting
the scale of its application \citep{ackerman2025}. Furthermore, Re-Os dating
of sulfides (other than the rare mineral molybdenite) requires
gram-quantities \citep{qi2010} and is therefore poorly suited to
resolve distinct mineralization episodes in multistage systems.

For multistage systems in particular, the co-application of independent
radiochronometers is often useful to distinguish the timings for
hydrothermal alteration from uplift, cooling, exhumation, and
potentially earlier emplacement of host rocks. For example, \citet{selby2002}
demonstrate that Re-Os molybdenite is more likely to accurately
record the age of sulfide mineralization in the Tintina Gold Belt,
Alaska, relative to the comparatively thermally-sensitive Ar-Ar mica
ages. However, molybdenite is far from a ubiquitous phase in
sulfide-bearing ores, unlike pyrite. A pyrite U-Th/He geochronometer
would expand such opportunities to co-apply radiochronometers that
interrogate processes operating at different times and temperatures to a
much more diverse and expansive array of locales that experienced
complex histories. Similar geochronometers developed for other sulfide
phases such as arsenopyrite, galena, and sphalerite may offer the
opportunity to disentangle multiple episodes of sulfide mineralization
and alteration within an ore body, allowing geologists to better
understand the sequence of events that lead to economic ore formation.

One such example of an opportunity for applying U-Th/He sulfide
geochronology is in the world\textquotesingle s largest known
platinum-group element deposit, the Bushveld Complex of South Africa,
which hosts the Merensky Reef and Platreef PGE deposits in the sulfide-bearing
upper critical zone, for which geochronometers yield a surprising diversity of ages:
zircon U/Pb ages vary from $\approx$2057-2060 Ma, while rutile ages are significantly younger at $\approx$2053 Ma 
\citep{scoates2021}, yet Re-Os systematics show evidence for
multiple sulfide mineralization events within the same horizon
\citep{schoenberg1999}. The Re-Os and Rb-Sr isochron ages they report
are unable to resolve these distinct mineralization events from the
zircon U/Pb emplacement age. Unlike Re-Os dating, the U-Th/He system
produces distinct ages for every dated aliquot, potentially offering the
opportunity to resolve the timings of prolonged volatile activity or
late hydrothermal Re-Os disturbance.

Similarly, at the Norilsk-Talnakh deposits of the Siberian Traps, the
ore-bearing intrusions can be correlated with a specific transition in
flood basalt source and eruptive style \citep{yao2021}, yet only
three of many differentiated intrusions in the region are economic, and
the precise timing and mechanism of sulfide saturation relative to trap
emplacement are not fully understood. In the 1.1 Ga Sonju Lake Intrusion
of Minnesota, USA, stable isotopic evidence suggests that
meso/epithermal fluids reacted with primary magmatic sulfides, raising
questions about when redistribution occurred and how much metal was
moved \citep{park2004}. These are deposits where independent
thermochronometric constraints capable of distinguishing primary
crystallization from later hydrothermal reworking are needed.

\subsection{The Single-Jump Migration Model and its Application to Pyrite}

The Russian school of noble gas geochronology spearheaded by
Shukolyukov and Yakubovich approaches the problem of helium migration
in minerals through a theoretical framework that differs fundamentally
from the volume diffusion model used to describe noble gas migration
dominant in the Western thermochronological literature \citep{dodson1973,
harrison1985,fortiergiletti1989,lovera1991,wolf1996,farley2000,lee1997}. In the Shukolyukov
``single-jump model,'' helium atoms occupy discrete energy minima within
the crystal structure, and migration proceeds through thermally
activated jumps between these sites \citep{shukolyukov2009,yakubovich2010}.

Within this framework, \citet{shukolyukov2012} developed the \textsuperscript{190}Pt-\textsuperscript{4}He
system as a new geochronometer for native platinum minerals,
demonstrating through thermal desorption analysis that radiogenic \He\ in
isoferroplatinum is stable to temperatures approaching the melting point
of platinum, and obtaining \textsuperscript{190}Pt-\textsuperscript{4}He ages from the Galmoenan and Kondyor
massifs consistent with independent Sm-Nd and Rb-Sr estimates. He
storage in native platinum was attributed to accumulation in vesicles
and clusters at dislocation cores and grain boundaries. Western scientists
similarly model helium migration in metals with single-jump mechanisms, although these single jumps are not typically modeled to
instantaneously liberate helium; instead, the modeled single jump allows
the atom to spontaneously travel a distance of some angstroms with some
frequency, introducing a spatial scale from which a diffusivity can be
evaluated \citep{ratzke1992,costhickman1975,wilson1973}.

\citet{yakubovich2015} extend this method to sperrylite (PtAs\textsubscript{2}), a
rare mineral found in magmatic PGE deposits that is isostructural with
pyrite. \textsuperscript{190}Pt-\textsuperscript{4}He ages from sperrylite at the Kondyor and
Norilsk-Oktyabrsky deposits were geologically consistent, and the high
He retentivity demonstrated in this arsenide prophesies similar behavior
in pyrite.

Without providing any sulfur buffer, \citet{yakubovich2019}
interpreted ramped-heating experiments for a variety of silica glass
encapsulated sulfides under the single-jump step-heating method. Pyrite,
in particular, was found to have anomalously high retentivity of helium. 
The first application of U-Th/He pyrite geochronometry to a natural ore
deposit was reported by \citet{yakubovich2019,yakubovich2020} for the Uzelga
Cu-Zn VMS deposit of the South Urals, yielding a consistent weighted
mean age of 382 $\pm$ 12 Ma that is older than regional prehnite-pumpellyite
metamorphism, demonstrating that pyrite can survive a metamorphic
overprint without complete isotopic resetting. While these results
establish the viability of pyrite as a U-Th/He geochronometer, their
analytical approach employs a simple, low-resolution monotonic heating schedule without
grain-size scaling experiments, sulfur buffers, or proton-implanted \textsuperscript{3}He
domain mapping, which leaves the diffusion/migration physics of sulfides
undercharacterized. Unsurprisingly, given the rapid heating rates used in their experiments, 
their results for pyrite generally show abrupt helium loss at temperatures corresponding to the upper stability 
field of pyrite around 700°C \citep{toulminbarton1964}, and therefore are unlikely to contain substantial information about helium loss 
in natural pyrite over geologic timescales. Extending these initial observations into a robust, predictive thermochronometric framework requires the more
rigorous experimental and theoretical treatment that is one of the aims of this work. 

\subsection{Geologic Setting}
\label{sec:ch3-geologic-setting}

Pyrite and arsenopyrite for the geochronologic study were collected from two deposits that strongly 
differ in the timing and conditions of their formation: the Archean Kidd Creek volcanogenic massive sulfide (VMS)
deposit, Ontario, Canada, and the Permo-Triassic orogenic gold system at
Gympie, Queensland, Australia. Pairing a $\sim$2.7 Ga Cu-Zn-Ag VMS sulfide
with a $\sim$235 Ma Au-bearing arsenopyrite provides two independently
well-constrained age brackets against which to test He retentivity and
closure behavior in pyrite across very different formation temperatures,
host lithologies, and post-depositional thermal histories.

\subsubsection{Kidd Creek, Ontario, Canada}

Kidd Creek is among the largest and most profitable VMS deposits in the
world, with past production and reserves exceeding 138.5 Mt at average
grades of 2.4 percent Cu, 6.5 percent Zn, 0.23 percent Pb, and 90 g/t Ag
\citep{hannington1999}. The deposit occurs within the Late Archean
Kidd-Munro assemblage of the Abitibi subprovince, Superior Province, hosted
at the top of a thickened pile of massive rhyolite flows, felsic
volcaniclastics, and coarse epiclastic rocks that attain a maximum
thickness of $\sim$300 m beneath the mine \citep{hannington1999}. The three
main stratiform orebodies (North, Central, and South) formed largely by
subseafloor infilling and replacement of permeable volcaniclastic rocks
within a subsiding, rift-related graben, rather than by high-temperature
venting at the seafloor \citep{hannington1999}. U-Pb zircon geochronology
brackets the ore-forming interval tightly: the oldest footwall rhyolite is
dated at 2716.1 $\pm$ 0.6 Ma, the onset of bimodal komatiite-rhyolite
volcanism at 2717 $\pm$ 1.1 Ma, and the youngest, uppermost quartz porphyry
unit at 2711.5 $\pm$ 1.2 Ma, indicating that mineralization took place
during at least one and possibly several million years of intermittent
volcanic activity \citep{hannington1999}. Oxygen isotope studies of the
host rhyolites show that, while primary igneous zircon retains magmatic
$\delta^{18}$O values (5.4\textperthousand{} $\pm$ 0.3\textperthousand{}), coexisting quartz
phenocrysts are pervasively shifted to much higher and more heterogeneous
values (14.2\textperthousand{} to 16.1\textperthousand{}), recording extensive subsolidus
recrystallization during the same long-lived hydrothermal convection that
deposited the sulfides \citep{king1997}.

The interior of the Canadian shield has been a stable platform for hundreds of millions of years, 
as documented by U-Th/He and fission track dating of apatite \citep{sturrock2024}. 
Kidd Creek has been of particular interest for its famously ancient
groundwaters that sustain a low-biomass but metabolically active community of sulfate-reducing and
alkane-oxidizing microorganisms \citep{lollar2019}. The Kidd Creek deposit is thus an 
ideal natural laboratory for testing the long-term He retentivity of pyrite and arsenopyrite.

\subsubsection{Gympie, Queensland, Australia}\label{sec:ch3-geologic-setting-gympie}

The Gympie arsenopyrite comes from a very different setting: an accreted,
Early Permian bimodal (basalt-dacite) submarine volcanic sequence, the
Gympie Group, interpreted as a fragment of an intra-oceanic island arc that
rifted from the Gondwana margin before docking against the New England
Orogen of eastern Australia \citep{sivellwaterhouse1988}. Drillcore near
the Gympie Eldorado Gold Mine resolves at least nine sequentially emplaced
magmatic suites and a $>$2 km thick volcano-sedimentary succession. This arc terrane is
inferred to have accreted to the Gondwana margin by about 220 Ma (Early to
Middle Triassic), based on cross-cutting granitoid relationships with
adjacent New England Orogen terranes \citep{sivellmcculloch2001}.

Gold-bearing pyrite-quartz mineralization at Gympie occurs in fibrous extensional veins developed within sheared,
carbonaceous black slates. Alteration associated with this vein set has yielded
a K/Ar age of about 235 Ma \citep{holcombe1997}. Refractory dolerite dykes and later
microdiorite-shoshonite dykes cross-cut the mineralized structures and
bracket the timing of the auriferous quartz veining from below and above,
respectively, giving an independent field constraint on the age of
mineralization that is broadly consistent with the K/Ar alteration ages
\citep{sivellmcculloch2001}.

The nominal ``Early Permian'' age assigned to the host rocks of the Gympie deposit, the Highbury Volcanics and
Rammutt Formation, however, rests mainly on sparse invertebrate and plant
fossil biostratigraphy higher in the sequence and on lithologic
correlation, rather than on direct radiometric dates on the volcanic pile
itself. \citet{holcombe1997} note that the
lower Gympie Block volcanics, like the correlative Berserker beds, ``may
contain older volcanic units,'' and fold the entire Gympie succession into
a long-lived ($\sim$320-280 Ma, peaking at $\sim$305 Ma) regional
magmatic episode. These volcanics may very well be related to nearby $\sim$300-310 Ma 
granodiorite magmatism and rapid uplift of the regional epidote-blueschist-facies basement, 
which record $^{40}$Ar/$^{39}$Ar hornblende cooling ages around 297 Ma \citep{little1995,holcombe1997}.

\section{Experimental Methods}\label{sec:ch3-experimental-methods}

\subsection{U-Th/He Geochronology}\label{sec:ch3-experimental-methods-geochronology}

\subsubsection{Sample Preparation}

Intact crystals of arsenopyrite (and pyrite from the Kidd Creek deposit) were extracted from Kidd Creek and Gympie samples using a 
hammer and chisel at Caltech and SELFRAG high-voltage pulse fragmentation at the University of Queensland, respectively. Pyrite is present in the Gympie sample but only in small amounts, and no pyrite dates are reported for it. The majority of the Kidd Creek samples were silver arsenopyrite, but brassy cubes (presumed pyrite) were also analyzed.
A subsample of the Gympie arsenopyrites was leached in 1M HCl for 4 hours to remove any surface contamination and then rinsed in MilliQ water and dried.
Individual crystals were washed in a sequence of acetone, ethanol, and MilliQ water baths, then promptly dried using clean, lint-free wipes.
These samples, along with the hematite MI-43 standard from Gogebic, Michigan \citep{farleymckeon2015}, and the apatite standard from 
Durango, Mexico \citep{farley2000}, were weighed, then encapsulated in silica glass ampoules similarly to the sulfides in \S\ref{sec:ch3-experimental-methods-step-heats}, 
but without any additional buffer materials. An empty tube was also encapsulated to serve as a procedural blank. Photos and masses of the
prepared ampoules were recorded so that the helium and trace metal analyses could be correctly matched.

\subsubsection{Helium Analysis}\label{ch3-geochron-helium-methods}

Encapsulated samples were wrapped in Ni-foil and loaded into a custom-built autosampler with the procedural blank loaded first. The autosampler was wrapped in heating tape, which was used
to warm the samples to 80-100°C for at least 12 hours in ultra-high vacuum before the first step heat to allow for atmospheric helium to escape. Initial 
efforts in method development showed that atmospheric helium contamination was significant (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-durango-age-vs-ra}). This atmospheric helium payload of an ampoule equilibrated with air is on the order of 10s of nccSTP helium per ampoule (Chapter~\ref{ch:apparatus}, \S\ref{experimental-methods}).
It is difficult to mathematically correct for even when 3He can be accurately measured, because helium diffusion and permeation through silica glass fractionates 3He/4He ratios \citep{shelby1971,jones1953}, and the 
ampoules were each geometrically unique and therefore had different permeation rates. Still, we found the best results when we applied a simple correction to the helium data after substantially mitigating this contamination with the warm (80-100°C) degassing step. To correct for the much smaller remaining atmospheric contamination, we subtract 4He proportionally to measured 3He by
the atmospheric 3He/4He isotope ratio: 1.39 $\times$ 10\textsuperscript{-6}. The procedural blanks showed that atmospheric helium contamination was reduced by a factor of 5-10, which was sufficient for these helium-rich samples with 100s of nccSTP helium (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-durango-age-vs-ra}). Future work may develop a more rigorous
approach to experimentally remove atmospheric helium contamination or to mathematically correct for it using the first order framework presented in Chapter~\ref{ch:apparatus} (\S\ref{theoretical-framework-of-permeation-lag}).

After 12 hours, unheated furnace blank measurements were periodically taken until the measured value was similar to such blanks 
taken for batches of samples not encapsulated in silica glass, then samples were individually dropped into a resistance furnace and baked at 1000-1200°C for 15 minutes to degas their helium contents. 
Although pyrite and arsenopyrite are unusually retentive of helium at lower temperatures, these temperatures were far above these phases' stability field, and so they were readily degassed. 
Helium abundances and isotope ratios were measured on a Thermo Fisher Helix SFT mass spectrometer. Gases evolved during the bake were passed through a charcoal filter held at 77~K, then exposed to an SAES-10 getter and a heated 
titanium getter before being cryofocused at 14~K, then released at 34~K. Sensitivities were quantified using a calibrated 3He-enriched air standard several times throughout the analysis.

\subsubsection{Trace Metal Analysis}
The encapsulated, now degassed samples were removed from the furnace and were matched to their pre-bake photos and masses. Around 20\% of samples were typically lost during the helium analysis process,
with ampoules either broken by the autosampler or by thermal stresses during the bakes. Samples bearing residual nickel-foil that had fused to the surface of the glass were scratched clean with a dental pick,
and the remaining glass ampoule was sonicated in a sequence of ethanol then MilliQ water baths. Samples were then placed in an acid-washed Teflon beaker and were broken underwater by an agate pestle, to prevent 
loss of sample material. The pestle was rinsed with MilliQ water between samples to avoid cross-contamination. Excess water was pipetted away from each sample and disposed of, then the remaining water was evaporated on a hot plate.
The vast majority of the samples were then spiked with a U-232-, Th-230-, and vanadium-enriched spike and digested in concentrated HNO\textsubscript{3} in closed Teflon beakers, leaving the glass intact. 
The uranium and thorium concentrations of the glass ampoules were measured to be $\approx$ 50 and 30 ppb by mass respectively, but this contaminant payload was only delivered when the glass was dissolved in HF; 
the uranium and thorium contents of concentrated nitric acid in extended contact with broken glass were below detection limits. 

After this point, the Kidd Creek pyrite/arsenopyrite and Durango Apatite solutions were pipetted and diluted to an ICPMS-ready solution. The Gympie samples, however, required further processing to dissolve fine U-Th bearing rutile inclusions. The Gympie and MI-43 hematite samples, now mostly digested
in concentrated HNO\textsubscript{3}, were dried down and put back up in small volumes of concentrated HCl, then were loaded into Parr vessels and baked at 200°C for 24 hours. 
The Gympie samples were then dried down again and put back up in concentrated HNO\textsubscript{3} before the concentrated acid was transferred and diluted to an ICPMS-ready solution. U and Th contents were quantified with isotope dilution using an Agilent 8800 ICP-MS. 
Other trace elements were measured as ratios with spike vanadium. The U-Th contents of the spikes and the sensitivities of the other quantified trace metals relative to vanadium were continuously monitored by analyzing a standard solution of known composition that was spiked with the same U-Th-V spike as the samples.
The same empty tube procedural blank used for the helium analysis was also processed through the same acid digestion and ICPMS analysis, and the measured U, Th, and other trace metal contents were subtracted from the measured values of the samples. The U-Th contents of the procedural blank were below detection limits, and the other trace metals were typically 1-5\% of the measured values of the samples.

The Gogebic Iron Range hematite standard \citep{farleymckeon2015} was carried through this same Parr-vessel digestion step alongside the Gympie arsenopyrite, serving as an in-run check on how completely this digestion protocol recovers a sample's true U-Th-Fe budget. Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-gogebic-he-vs-eu} plots each Gogebic aliquot's measured \textsuperscript{4}He against its eU, normalized by the sample mass calculated from measured Fe content where available 
and by the pre-analysis balance mass otherwise. Except for one clearly erroneous outlier (with an age in excess of 1.5~Ga),

\textbf{Encapsulation}

Before encapsulating the sulfide samples, glass tubes were cleaned and
pulled into pipettes. 10 cm long segments of pure silica glass tubes
with an outer diameter of 4 mm and an inner diameter of 3 mm were
submerged and sonicated within acetone, ethanol, then MilliQ water baths
in sequence, and then were dried overnight in a 100°C oven. The glass
tubes were hand-pulled into thinner pipettes using a natural gas and
oxygen welding torch and the thin ends were fused closed.

Aliquots of sulfides were weighed and loaded into room temperature glass
tubes along with fused sulfur in the presence of an ionizing fan to
mitigate static. The arsenopyrite ampoule was loaded with crystalline
realgar (arsenic sulfide) instead of fused sulfur, and one pyrite
ampoule was loaded with silica glass pieces above and below the pyrite
aliquot to separate the pyrite from the sulfur liquid during the
experiment. To seal, the top of each pipette was interfaced with a
vacuum line and the tube was pumped to a pressure of \textless60
millitorr, which greatly aided the ease and quality of the final seal.
After attaining rough vacuum, a fine-flamed welding torch was used to
seal the top of each ampoule. To determine the geometric factors for
permeation of the ampoules, the inner and outer diameters of most
ampoules were measured using the measurement function of the Amscope
stereo microscope software, which was calibrated and tested during early
development of this method. LVCOPy230630 was completed without these geometric measurements.
For this experiment, a geometric factor of 3000 cm\textsuperscript{-2}
was arbitrarily assigned to estimate uncertainties of the measurements
due to permeation lag. This assumption is unlikely to affect the interpretation
of the results.

The sealed ampoules were placed in copper foil packets and mounted to a
K-type thermocouple within an ultra-high vacuum projector lamp heating
cell described in detail by \citet{farley1999}. Before the experiment
began, each ampoule was washed with ethanol, air-dried, loaded in the
lamp cell, pumped to high vacuum, and was held at 100°C for at least 30
minutes under dynamic vacuum to desorb ethanol, water, and other
atmospheric species on the ampoule, copper packet, and lamp cell.

\textbf{Helium Mass Spectrometry}

Heating steps and 3He measurements for all samples were automated using
custom LabView routines on a Helix-MC-Plus\textsuperscript{10K}
multi-collector mass spectrometer, or a Pfeiffer Prisma QMS 200
quadrupole mass spectrometer for the Rustenburg Pyrite ampoule. For all
ampoules, a Watlow temperature controller held the ampoule at the
temperature set point for a pre-programmed duration, after which the
ampoule was allowed to cool while the evolved gas was prepared for
measurement for about 25 minutes.

For the irradiated samples, helium was purified by exposing evolved
gases to a charcoal trap held in liquid nitrogen, a titanium sponge
getter, then a SAES NP10 getter before freely expanding into the
Helix-MC-Plus\textsuperscript{10K}. 3He was measured with a
pulse-counting electron multiplier, while 4He was detected on a Faraday
detector. The Helix-MC-Plus\textsuperscript{10K} mass spectrometer was
operating in peak hopping mode and the majority of the analysis time was
spent measuring 3He. Every few hours, the background was mass-scanned to
set the 3He and 4He measure points; each was taken to be a constant mass
offset from the background HD\textsuperscript{+} peak. These mass
offsets are precisely measured when tuning the
Helix-MC-Plus\textsuperscript{10K} for helium isotope analysis. Months
of helium measurements with the Helix-MC-Plus\textsuperscript{10K} at
Caltech have shown this approach to be reliable, reproducible, and
accurate for helium isotope analysis. Additionally, an electron
multiplier monitored atomic mass 3.5 to detect 4He overspray. 4He
signals were near-blank for all experiments of irradiated samples, and
no attempt is made here to interpret those data. 4He overspray likewise
did not affect 3He signals. Measurements of high 3He MM gas standard
were analyzed every 8 hours to track instrumental drift of 3He and 4He
sensitivities and to quantify internal reproducibility. These standards
show 3He was generally internally reproducible to within 1\% (2$\sigma$) for
rolling sets of 5 consecutive standards, except for the latest
measurements on the 112123SalAspIrr Arsenopyrite, during which the ion
source began to fail. Those measurements are excluded from further
analysis, and any helium evolved during those steps is likely to be a
very small proportion of the total helium contents of the sample
(\textless0.2\%) and thus would not significantly affect the calculation
of the total helium content of the sample.

After the step heat experiments, the LVCOPy230630 and GalTS230630 ampoules 
were broken and then completely degassed following the step heat experiments by heating to 1100 \textdegree C in a resistance furnace, then 
\textsuperscript{3}He and \textsuperscript{4}He yields were quantified using a Thermo Fisher Helix SFT. The total \textsuperscript{3}He yields 
for the remainder of the irradiated pyrite samples were inferred assuming the measured \textsuperscript{3}He concentration of LVCOPy230630. 
The chalcopyrite experiment was assumed to be entirely degassed in the step heat experiment without the final fusion step. 
\hl{The total \textsuperscript{4}He content of the Rustenburg pyrite was assumed to be 200 nccSTP, a conservatively low estimate based on the measured \textsuperscript{4}He content of other Rustenburg pyrite aliquots.}


Radiogenic 4He evolved during step heats of the encapsulated Rustenburg
Pyrite was quantified using isotope dilution with a high purity 3He
spike on a Pfeiffer Prisma QMS 200 quadrupole mass spectrometer. A
standard gas with a known amount of 4He was measured before and after
the experiment, to monitor drift in the relative sensitivities of 3He
and 4He and to estimate reproducibility, which was about 6\% (2$\sigma$). After
each step, evolved gas from the ampoule in the lamp heating cell was
allowed to mix with the 3He spike and the mixture was frozen on charcoal
held at 14~K within a cryostat, then helium was selectively released to
the mass spectrometer by raising the temperature to 36~K.

\subsection{Analytical Methods For Step Heat Experiments}\label{sec:ch3-analytical-methods}

Similarly to \citet{yakubovich2019}, these helium step heat experiments were designed according to the 
assumption that the silica glass ampoule was
effectively ``helium transparent'', or in other words that helium
permeation through the silica glass ampoule was nearly instantaneous
with respect to the rate of helium evolution from the encapsulated
sulfides. Thus, permeation steps (as discussed in Chapters~\ref{ch:apparatus} and~\ref{ch:goethite}) were
rarely used. This assumption was acceptable for most of the pyrite and arsenopyrite step heats, and much of the galena
and chalcopyrite step heats. Unfortunately, the assumption was not valid for any of the sphalerite or chalcocite step heats, 
nor for some of the lower temperature steps of the other sulfides, which can only be discussed here qualitatively.
The helium yields for steps at high temperatures are approximately the measured value, and the uncertainty of this estimation due to 
permeation lag is constrained by upper bounds following the uncertainty-bounding 
approach presented in Chapter~\ref{ch:apparatus} (\S\ref{bounding-method}). This approach
requires no permeation steps or knowledge of the helium content within the ampoule from previous
steps, instead calculating the maximum possible helium content remaining within
the ampoule following a given high-temperature measurement, provided the step was long and hot
enough for the helium inventory of the tube to have reached a steady state concentration and
assuming that the rate of helium evolution from the encapsulated mineral
is constant or decreasing as is generally expected for first and second order processes. The steady state assumption is taken to be effectively
valid once the ampoule loses memory of the helium content remaining from
the previous step. According to Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-8} in Chapter~\ref{ch:apparatus}, only 1.8\% of the
difference between the previous step's remaining helium content and the
current step's steady state helium content remains when
\(\lambda t = 4\). In this work, the steady state assumption is only
applied for steps long and hot enough for \(\lambda t \geq 4,\ \)where
\(\lambda = KGRT\), \emph{K} is the temperature-dependent permeation
coefficient for 4He or 3He through silica glass, \emph{G} is the ampoule
geometric factor, \emph{R} is the gas constant, \emph{T} is the
temperature in Kelvin, and \emph{t} is the time in seconds. The 4He
permeation coefficient \emph{K} is taken from \citet{swets1961}; and
these coefficients are multiplied by the permeation coefficient ratio
from \citet{jones1953} to yield the 3He permeation coefficient.

Many heating steps, especially near the beginning of the experiments
where lower temperature steps were used, are not interpreted or plotted
in this work because \(\lambda t < 4\); however, some steps with
\(\lambda t < 4\) are used when Eq.~\eqref{eq:steadystate} is used to place upper bound
constraints on preceding steps, so that Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-8} can in turn be used to
quantify the uncertainty on the yields of steps that have not reached steady
state. 

This method of calculating uncertainties produces an asymmetric upper
and lower uncertainty for each step. These asymmetric uncertainties are propagated 
through the diffusivity inversion of \citet{fechtigkalbitzer1966} with Eq.~\eqref{eq:diffusivity-bounds}
in Chapter~\ref{ch:goethite}. In general, the 2$\sigma$ analytical uncertainties as calculated using the
reproducibility of standards are greatly exceeded by the uncertainties
due to the permeation lag of the ampoule as constrained by the procedure outlined in \S\ref{bounding-method}.

\subsection{Pyrite/Arsenopyrite U-Th/He Ages from Kidd Creek}\label{ch3-results-kiddcreek-ages}

Twelve aliquots of Kidd Creek pyrite/arsenopyrite were dated by U-Th/He step-degassing
(Table~\ref{tab:ch3-main-geochron}) and give atmospherically corrected ages ranging from 465 to 860 Ma
with the exception of one aliquot (011123KCPyST05C) that gives an older measured age of 1596 Ma 
(Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-kiddcreek-age-th-he}). The mean age of the remaining eleven aliquots is

far younger than the $\sim$2716 Ma age of the host rhyolite
\citep{hannington1999} (\S\ref{sec:ch3-discussion}).

\subsection{Arsenopyrite U-Th/He Ages from Gympie}\label{ch3-results-gympie-ages}

Sixteen aliquots of Gympie arsenopyrite were dated by the same method
(Table~\ref{tab:ch3-main-geochron}; Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-gympie-age-vs-eu}).
The first four aliquots analyzed (013023PVGyPy-1 through -4) were prepared
under an early iteration of the protocol that predated both the
tube-blank atmospheric helium degassing step and the Parr-vessel HCl digestion
step (\S\ref{sec:ch3-experimental-methods-geochronology}) later
adopted to fully dissolve fine U-Th-Sm-bearing rutile inclusions. The measured
ages of these aliquots are unreasonable: ranging from 638 to 1008 Ma (mean 834 Ma, n=4), 
though it is unclear to what extent the bias is due to atmospheric contamination, incomplete digestion of rutile, or both.  
The ages of these four early aliquots are therefore
considered unreliable and are excluded from further interpretation.

The remaining twelve Gympie aliquots, prepared after both protocol
refinements were adopted, define a substantially younger and tighter age
population. Eleven of these twelve give atmospherically corrected ages
between 191 and 306 Ma (mean 258 $\pm$ 35 Ma, 1$\sigma$, n=11; these
eleven are plotted in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-gympie-age-vs-eu}). The
twelfth, 0413PyGy-4, is a clear outlier at 623 Ma and is
also anomalous in its measured 4He concentration (68.4 ncc/mg), roughly
three times that of the next most helium-rich aliquot in the
accepted Gympie suite (230628GyPyHClab, 21.65 ncc/mg: supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-gympie-4he-outlier-si}). We interpret
this combination as evidence for helium implantation from a U-Th-Sm-rich
accessory phase concentrated at the sulfide
grain margin and not fully digested during trace-element sample
preparation. Aliquot 0413PyGy-4 is therefore also excluded from further
interpretation.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:ch3-discussion}

\subsection{Effectiveness of the Ampoule Method in Stabilizing Sulfides}\label{ch3-sem-imaging-tube-buffered-sulfides}
The methodology for the heating treatments and SEM imagery of pyrite, arsenopyrite, and marcasite 
surfaces following thermal treatments in different conditions is discussed in \S\ref{sem-imaging-of-buffered-and-decomposed-sulfides}.
The SEM imagery reveals deformed, variably raised, \textasciitilde10 µm
cracked surfaces on pyrite and marcasite that are heated in open
air (open-air marcasite not shown), and for arsenopyrite heated in a closed capsule without a
realgar buffer (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-sulfide-sem-grid}). Pyrite and marcasite samples that
were heated in ultra-high vacuum developed recessive \textasciitilde1 µm
rounded craters (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-sulfide-sem-grid}). Contrarily, images of thermally treated, encapsulated
pyrite and arsenopyrite with added fused sulfur or realgar show no
obvious distinction from the control group: both of which showcase
conchoidal fracture (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-sulfide-sem-grid}). The buffered, encapsulated marcasite
sample shows a raised layer of angular (presumably pyrite)
crystals growing atop its surface (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-sulfide-sem-grid}); evidence of phase transformation is
present in all three heat-treated marcasite populations, demonstrating
that the encapsulation method is not effective in preventing phase
breakdown at elevated temperatures for marcasite. These
experiments are not conclusive evidence that thermal phase
transformation is prevented for pyrite or arsenopyrite (indeed, arsenopyrite's 
equilibrium stoichiometry varies with temperature, suggesting that some reaction 
during step heats would be expected for this phase: \citet{morimotoclark1961,barton1969}). 
However, the step heats presented here are consistent with the 
interpretation that reaction is suppressed, encouraging its application for helium step heat experiments.

\subsection{Remarkable Helium Retentivity of Pyrite and Arsenopyrite}\label{ch3-discussion-retentivity}

The central finding of this chapter's step heat experiments
(\S\ref{ch3-sulfide-step-heat-experiments}) is that pyrite (and to a lesser extent, arsenopyrite) are extraordinarily retentive of
helium against thermal loss: cumulative fractional yields never exceeded 5\% for any pyrite or
arsenopyrite experiment, even after repeated heating cycles at temperatures between 500-600 \textdegree C. These cumulative fractional yields are difficult to interpret without a reference 
diffusive timescale, so these step heat results are contextualized against the helium retentivity of 
other phases in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-fo-comparison}. This in effect places the step heat experiments of
the sulfides of this study on the same diffusive-dose axis as
step heat experiments of several other minerals that are often used for U-Th/He dating: single-crystal and polycrystalline hematite, vacuum- and
hydrothermally-degassed goethite, zircon, and monazite. To do this, the activation energy of hematite 
from \citet{farley2018} is used to convert the time and temperature of each step heat into a cumulative diffusive time, expressed as a Fourier number ($Fo = \frac{D}{a^2}_{\mathrm{ref}}t$). 
The Fourier number can also be thought of as a dimensionless diffusive time, real time divided by the diffusive timescale of a reference domain, $\tau = \frac{a^2}{D}$. $\tau$ here is
referenced to a 1 $\mu$m single-crystal hematite domain. The aim of such a plot is to flatten complex heating histories into a single value so that 
phases with complex helium migration behavior can be compared on a common scale. Admittedly, this is not
strictly correct because the activation energies of different phases (and indeed diffusive domains within a single phase) are not identical; however, this plot preserves the relative ordering of phases
regardless of the choice of reasonable activation energy: we tested 149-171 kJ/mol. Therefore, this plot is a useful tool for comparing the relative retentivity of different phases, 
even though the absolute diffusive dose is not strictly correct.

On this comparison, pyrite and arsenopyrite sit at or beyond the
retentive extreme of the entire compilation. Both plot at diffusive doses
orders of magnitude beyond where the reference 1 $\mu$m single-crystal
hematite domain \citep{farley2018} has gone fully to completion, and
beyond essentially the full range spanned by zircon
\citep{guenthner2013}, comparable to or exceeding that of
monazite \citep{farley2007}. The polycrystalline
hematite and vacuum-degassed goethite aggregates, by contrast, sit
orders of magnitude further to the left. The hydrothermally degassed
goethite (Chapter~\ref{ch:goethite}) plots closer to but less
retentive than the sulfides here.

Taken together, this comparison indicates that sulfur-buffered,
silica-encapsulated pyrite and arsenopyrite are not merely retentive
relative to other sulfides (galena, chalcopyrite, chalcocite, sphalerite;
\S\ref{ch3-si-cu-pb-sulfides}), but are among the most helium-retentive
phases yet characterized by step heating, on par with or exceeding
zircon and monazite and substantially exceeding both single-crystal and
polycrystalline hematite and vacuum-degassed goethite. This is the
empirical basis for treating pyrite (and, by extension, arsenopyrite) as
a credible U-Th/He geochronometer in this chapter's Kidd Creek and
Gympie case studies (\S\ref{ch3-results-kiddcreek-ages}, \S\ref{ch3-results-gympie-ages}), notwithstanding complexities introduced by the
inclusion-hosted origin of the radiogenic budget
(\S\ref{ch3-discussion-radiogenic-he-origin}).

\subsection{Inferring Bulk Helium Migration Behavior from Low Fractional
Yields}\label{ch3-low-fractional-yields}

Many of the step heats on encapsulated sulfides presented here show very
high helium retentivity, and for those experiments only a very small
fraction of the total yield evolved (\textless5\% for the arsenopyrite
and Rustenburg pyrite; \textless2\% for the Leadville pyrite), similarly
to previous work on highly helium retentive phases such as baddeleyite
\citep{baughmanflowers2018}. For these sulfides, a
mechanism responsible for bulk helium loss besides desulfurization
cannot be identified. For some well-studied phases like zircon and
titanite, Arrhenius plots of the earliest fractions of evolved helium
often reflect processes or conditions that are accessory to the bulk
mechanism for helium migration through the mineral, such as radiation
damage annealing or surface roughness \citep{reinersfarley1999,
guenthner2013}, where the primary mechanism for helium
migration through the pristine mineral is volume diffusion. These
apparent departures from the bulk helium migration behavior can be
caused by properties of natural samples, like surface roughness or
fine-scale cracks and fractures \citep{reinersfarley1999} that produce
complex grain size distributions even in carefully sieved, (apparently)
single grains. Contrastingly, some other complexities in the helium loss
behavior at early loss fractions can be ephemeral and altered during
experimental heating, such as the annealing of radiation damage \citep{shusterfarley2009}. For radiation damaged samples, understanding the
migration behavior of helium before and during annealing is essential to
understand the sample's retentivity in nature and
therefore the earliest fraction of helium evolved in step heats is the
focus of many studies \citep{shusterfarley2009,flowers2009,flowers2007,shuster2006}.

The fraction of total helium evolved before the predominant migration
mechanism's behavior can be characterized in helium step heats varies:
\citet{guenthner2013} show that significantly non-zero delta or
ln(r/r0) values persist in zircon until about 2\% of the total helium is
degassed for typical natural samples; \citet{reinersfarley1999} observe
apparent diffusivities of helium in titanite converging to linearity
after 5\% of the total helium is evolved. Similar experiments
on proton-irradiated synthetic samples seem not to be marred by the same
complexities: helium step heat data for synthetic monazite converge
nearly immediately to a linear array at very low cumulative helium loss
fractions \citep{farley2007}.

The step heat experiments on pyrite and arsenopyrite in this study would
have benefited from longer steps at high temperature to degas more
helium to better understand the bulk behavior of helium, but even at
small loss fractions some key observations about helium loss in sulfides
can be made.

\subsection{Comparison of Helium Step Heat Experiments of Natural Pyrites}\label{ch3-compare-pyrites-discussion}

In this work, four encapsulated, sulfur-buffered aliquots of pyrite
taken from two localities are subjected to helium step heat experiments.
Step heats for the three irradiated pyrites from Leadville, CO are
broadly similar, but contrast markedly with step heats of non-irradiated
pyrite from Rustenburg, South Africa; therefore, differences between
pyrites of different localities are discussed before the variation within
the aliquots from Leadville, CO.

Radiogenic helium evolution during step heats of octahedral pyrite from
Rustenburg, South Africa shows that this sample is less retentive than
the pyritohedral pyrite from Leadville, Colorado (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-pyrite-delta-compilation}).
Apparent diffusivities of these samples also have fundamentally distinct
geometries when plotted in an Arrhenius plot. The Rustenburg pyrite
shows a striking feature of very steep slopes (corresponding to an
activation energy of \ChThreeRustEa\ $\pm$ \ChThreeRustEaErr\ kJ/mol) on
retrograde-prograde heating cycles at temperatures lower than the
previous maximum temperature step heat, whereas the maximum apparent
diffusivity for a given temperature seemed to follow a much shallower
slope, similar to the lower activation energy of the Leadville, CO pyrite: \ChThreeLVCOEa\ $\pm$ \ChThreeLVCOEaErr\
kJ/mol. The geometry of the step heats in an Arrhenius
plot for the irradiated Leadville, CO pyrite is generally simpler:
generally slowly decreasing in a delta plot, or for isothermal steps
(Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-pyrite-delta-compilation}) as would be expected for helium release governed by
MDD systematics \citep{farley2018}.

Natural pyrite is known to develop fractures and crystalline
dislocations in metamorphic and experimental conditions \citep{gill1969,
mcclayellis1983,barrie2011}. Abundant fractures of
micron-scale widths were clearly visible in the Rustenburg pyrite of
this study (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-rustenburg-control-fractures}). Those pyrites were surprisingly easy to crush,
presumably breaking easily along fine pre-existing fractures. Although
pyrites from Leadville are known to be associated with faulted
dolomites, especially along deformation planes which likely served as
pathways for mineralizing hydrothermal fluids \citep{nash1979}, the samples
used here were exceptionally sturdy and visually homogeneous under SEM.
Rare veins and coatings of dolomite within and around the pyrites were
present but were avoided when preparing the sample for irradiation.

Within this work, a pipe/short-circuit diffusion mechanism \citep{lee1992} is favored to
explain the helium loss behavior of the Rustenburg pyrite, where helium
populates the sample's fine fractures at increasing temperatures due to
the primary helium migration mechanism, perhaps volume diffusion through crystalline pyrite. 
This primary mechanism is interpreted to be the same for all pyrite samples
regardless of locality. On retrograde heating steps, short-circuit
diffusion of helium out of fine fractures in the Rustenburg pyrite is
hypothesized to become the rate-limiting process.

\subsection{Leadville, CO Pyrite: Evidence for a Grain Size Control?}\label{ch3-leadville-pyrite-sulfur-liquid-control}

Three helium step heat experiments for the irradiated Leadville, CO
pyrite are discussed here. Grain size was varied by a factor of 2
between a half-prism width of 200 microns for LVCOPy230630 and 400
microns for BigLVCOPy230707 to investigate the dependence of
helium migration in pyrite on grain size. The aim of the third experiment,
112123LVCOPyIrr, was to test the effect of isolating the pyrite from the
liquid sulfur during the experiment to investigate the role of
dissolution and (re)crystallization of pyrite by the ampoule's sulfur
fluid. Silica glass shards were added to this ampoule below and above
the pyrite aliquot, forcing the pyrite grains to remain in the middle of
the ampoule, and not at the edges/bottom, where sulfur liquid is
presumed to accumulate. All three samples overlap in their Arrhenius plots (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-sulfide-arrhenius-compilation}),
and all three samples record declining diffusivities consistent with MDD systematics (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-pyrite-delta-compilation}). 
The larger grained aliquot recorded lower cumulative fractional yields at these common apparent diffusivities. 
The smaller-grained aliquots, LVCOPy230630 and 112123LVCOPyIrr,
have essentially identical delta plots (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-pyrite-delta-compilation}), 
providing no evidence for a sulfur-liquid control in driving helium loss for these experiments. 

Very little helium was evolved from any of the exceptionally retentive Leadville pyrite samples: none evolved
more than 3\% of its cumulative fractional yield, in spite of the intense heating schedules (at temperatures up to 600\textdegree C) they were subjected to.
These samples are only resolved by grain size. Because all Arrhenius plots overlap, it is likely that the differences in the 
delta plots are due to the relative abundance of the smallest domain size: the smaller aliquots contained a greater proportion of the least retentive domains interrogated by
these experiments. One potential explanation is that only helium on extraordinarily small dusty material carried on the surfaces of the larger aliquots is degassed,
and thus the primary control on the delta plot is the surface area to volume ratio. This is consistent with the observation that the smaller aliquots, 
with half the half-prism width of the larger aliquot, record $\Delta$=0 at twice the cumulative fractional yield of the larger aliquot (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-pyrite-delta-compilation}).


\subsection{Relevance of Helium Step Heat Data of Encapsulated,
Sulfur-Buffered Sulfides in Nature}

Given the high helium retention of pyrite and arsenopyrite shown in this
work, thermal helium loss in nature may be rare. A more likely mechanism for helium loss in these common sulfides in
nature may indeed be recrystallization. Pyrite in metamorphic
conditions commonly recrystallizes and may either shrink or grow \citep{craig1998,mcclayellis1983}. Until more work has characterized helium
loss (or gain) for recrystallizing pyrite or arsenopyrite in metamorphic
conditions, helium should cautiously be considered to have behaved as an
open or partially open system in sulfides during metamorphic events, although \citet{yakubovich2020} report that 
helium in the Uzelga pyrite was retained through metamorphism.

\subsection{Arsenopyrite}\label{ch3-discussion-arsenopyrite}

Arsenopyrite (FeAsS) adopts a distorted, lower-symmetry variant of the
marcasite structure in which arsenic substitutes irregularly for sulfur
\citep{morimotoclark1961}, in contrast to pyrite's simple cubic lattice. 
Because the degree of distortion scales with arsenic content \citep{morimotoclark1961},
helium diffusion is likewise expected to vary with composition. 
Unfortunately, equilibrium arsenopyrite composition is not
itself fixed: arsenic content increases systematically with formation
temperature \citep{barton1969,morimotoclark1961}, so the same
crystal-chemical property expected to govern helium retentivity should
also vary with an arsenopyrite sample's thermal history, complicating
any attempt to generalize helium retentivity from a single experimental
aliquot to arsenopyrite as a mineral species. Similarly, the composition of the arsenopyrite subjected to
step heats herein may itself have changed during the experiment, further distancing 
these experimental results from helium loss from arsenopyrite in nature.

With that caveat, the step heat results obtained here
(\S\ref{ch3-sulfide-step-heat-experiments}) show the encapsulated arsenopyrite
experiment was less helium-retentive than the Leadville pyrite experiments of this
study, and it lacked a well-defined linear Arrhenius region. Still, arsenopyrite remained markedly more
retentive of helium than any other sulfide examined in this study besides pyrite, 
in agreement with the relative helium retentivity ranking of sulfides
reported by \citet{yakubovich2019}.

Qualitatively, arsenopyrite appears to be more retentive of helium than most zircon (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-fo-comparison}; \citealp{guenthner2013}).

\subsection{Thermal Helium Migration Systematics for Other Sulfides}

Discussion of the thermal helium migration systematics of the four
remaining, non-pyrite/arsenopyrite sulfides of this study (chalcopyrite,
chalcocite, sphalerite, and galena) is given in the Supplementary Information
(\S\ref{sec:ch3-SI}); neither copper sulfide nor galena is considered a
promising U-Th/He geochronometer or helium isotope target.

\subsection{Origin of Radiogenic Helium in Sulfides}\label{ch3-discussion-radiogenic-he-origin}

The pyrite and arsenopyrite of this study are not, themselves,
appreciably radiogenic minerals. Pyrite in particular has very little affinity for U, Th, or Sm, which are large, highly charged
cations poorly suited to substitution for Fe\textsuperscript{2+}. Consistent with this, only the
aliquots (of any locality or mineral in the compilation, main text or
SI) that host measurable U-Th-Sm-bearing accessory phases returned
appreciable eU and, correspondingly, appreciable radiogenic 4He. For example, 
the Leadville pyrite samples are the purest, most visually inclusion-free grains, selected for the sulfur-buffered step heat experiments of \S\ref{sec:ch3-experimental-methods-step-heats}, and they
are essentially non-radiogenic. The U-Th/He ages for sulfides may not date growth of the sulfide
lattice directly, even if it is perfectly retentive of helium, because some of the 
helium retentivity of the sample will be determined by the size, mode, and connectivity of inclusions. 
Sulfide ages are easiest to interpret for the case when the radiogenic helium-source inclusions 
are smaller than 10 microns across, when only the helium retentivity of the sulfide is significant (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-he-age-vs-inclusion-size}). 

Backscattered-electron imagery of the Kidd Creek arsenopyrite/pyrite directly shows a surprising abundance of apatite inclusions. Superficially, hand-picked crystals appear as optically clean, blocky euhedral 
crystals set within a finer
silicate groundmass (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-kcpy-groundmass}), but in
cross-section the same grains are pervasively spongy, riddled with
abundant tiny inclusions rather than being the pure, homogeneous pyrite
their exteriors suggest. These inclusions are dominantly apatite
(Figs.~\ref{fig:ch3-kcpy-aspy},~\ref{fig:ch3-kcpy-apatites}), ranging in
size from sub-6 micron grains at the limit of SEM resolution up to
$\sim$30 micron half-width. Because apatite
preferentially incorporates U, Th, and especially Sm relative to
coexisting sulfide and silicate phases, even a small volume fraction of
included apatite can dominate a pyrite aliquot's bulk eU budget and,
correspondingly, its radiogenic 4He production.

The Gympie arsenopyrite hosts a texturally distinct, but analogously
inclusion-hosted, radiogenic budget. SEM imagery
(Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-gympie-inclusions}) shows abundant fine, needle-like
rutile inclusions. These rutile needles are the most likely U-Th-Sm-He source for the Gympie samples,
and variable inheritance of older rutile engulfed by later sulfide may be responsible for 
its observed age dispersion (\S\ref{ch3-results-gympie-ages}). 

This inclusion-host origin of radiogenic helium in sulfides has a direct
methodological consequence: sulfide U-Th/He geochronology cannot be
treated as a single, generic protocol applied uniformly across
localities. The identity, size distribution, and digestion behavior of
the accessory phase or phases actually carrying the U-Th-Sm budget are
properties of each deposit's own mineral paragenesis, and differ sharply
even between the two localities studied here. Reliable
sulfide U-Th/He geochronology of a new locality therefore requires SEM
characterization of the dated material to identify its radiogenic
inclusion population before analysis, and a dissolution protocol
tailored to that population's specific mineralogy and grain size.

\subsection{Interpretation of Kidd Creek Pyrite/Arsenopyrite Ages: Mixing Between a Retentive, He-Th-Poor Host and Apatite Inclusions}\label{ch3-discussion-kiddcreek-ages}

The Kidd Creek pyrite/arsenopyrite ages are not scattered randomly
around some single ``true'' age. Eleven of the twelve dated aliquots
define a coherent array in which the Th- and He-richest aliquots give
the youngest ages and the Th- and He-poorest aliquots give the oldest,
ranging from 465 to 860 Ma (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-kiddcreek-age-th-he}). The
youngest of the eleven approach, but are always higher than, the
$392 \pm 32$ Ma regional apatite (U-Th)/He cooling age reported by
\citet{sturrock2024} for sample 01-OE-69, sampled only $\sim$90 km from
Kidd Creek; the oldest approach $\sim$860 Ma, implying that this
population is significantly more helium-retentive than the apatite it
includes.

  \centering

  \caption{Atmospherically corrected Kidd Creek pyrite/arsenopyrite age vs.
  Th content (left) and vs. measured 4He content (right; nccSTP/mg),

Given the inclusion-hosted origin of radiogenic helium established
above (\S\ref{ch3-discussion-radiogenic-he-origin}), the natural reading
of this array is a two-component mixture: an old end-member that is
essentially He-Th-poor but comparatively retentive, and a population of
included apatite with a much younger ($\sim$392 Ma) cooling age
\citep{sturrock2024}. A Kidd Creek aliquot's bulk age then falls between
these two end-members depending on what fraction of its total measured
Th and He budget was hosted in (and retained by) apatite inclusions
since 392 Ma, versus alpha-ejected into the surrounding, retentive
sulfide host and retained there since it was crystallized.

A simple model shows that lower age paired with higher Th and He can be
explained with a single-age apatite population of variable inclusion size.
Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-he-age-vs-inclusion-size} extends the alpha-ejection
retention framework of \S\ref{ch3-discussion-radiogenic-he-origin} to
compute the apparent bulk He age of a hypothetically perfectly
helium-retentive sulfide aliquot hosting a single apatite inclusion of
half-prism radius $R$, assuming the sulfide and apatite crystallized
2700 Ma ago, but the apatite itself only began retaining its own
self-implanted helium after 380 Ma. At small $R$, nearly all
the produced helium recoils out of the inclusion into the retentive
sulfide, and the aliquot's bulk age approaches the old, 2700 Ma
crystallization age, but at large $R$, an increasing fraction of the
inclusion's own production is retained within the apatite inclusion
itself, so the bulk age falls toward 380 Ma. Because a larger inclusion
also simply contains more total U-Th-Sm mass, the same increase in $R$
that drives the age down simultaneously drives up an aliquot's bulk Th
and He content, producing exactly the anti-correlation seen in
Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-kiddcreek-age-th-he}.

At the $\leq$30 $\mu$m half-width apatite inclusion size actually
observed in the Kidd Creek material (\S\ref{ch3-discussion-radiogenic-he-origin},
Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-kcpy-apatites}), Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-he-age-vs-inclusion-size}
predicts an apparent age of $\sim$1.6 Ga or older for a sulfide host that is
perfectly closed to helium loss over its entire 2.7 Gyr history. Eleven
of the twelve measured Kidd Creek ages, plotted at this inclusion size
in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-he-age-vs-inclusion-size}, fall far short of this
prediction. This model cannot produce ages as young as 465-860 Ma, so most of these sulfides must 
have lost a substantial fraction of their radiogenic
helium since crystallization, rather than behaving as a closed system
simply mixing with variably sized apatite inclusions.

The twelfth, oldest aliquot, 011123KCPyST05C, is the one exception: its
$1596 \pm 46$ Ma age sits close to the closed-system prediction above at
the observed $\sim$30 $\mu$m inclusion size, as though this one aliquot
alone retained essentially all of its radiogenic and inclusion-sourced
helium since crystallization while the rest of the suite lost helium
over time. A single old date is weak evidence for anything on
its own: an anomalously old U-Th/He age is also the expected signature of
incomplete acid digestion, which would leave some U-Th-Sm-bearing residue undissolved, overestimating
the apparent age. We cannot distinguish between these two explanations with the data at hand. 

This chapter's own step heat experiments show that sulfur-buffered
pyrite is consistently among the most helium-retentive phases measured with step heat experiments,
while arsenopyrite, though still strongly retentive, is notably less so
(Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-sulfide-arrhenius-compilation},
Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-fo-comparison}; \S\ref{ch3-discussion-arsenopyrite}).
If 011123KCPyST05C were confirmed to be closer to pure pyrite than the
rest of the Kidd Creek suite, this retentivity contrast would offer a
ready explanation for why it alone approaches closed-system behavior. Indeed, the arsenic measurement
(Table~\ref{tab:ch3-main-geochron-trace}) supports this interpretation: 011123KCPyST05C has the
lowest measured arsenic concentration of the Kidd Creek aliquots. Unfortunately, this study's arsenic measurements for both the 
Kidd Creek and Gympie sulfides are not reliable: they are inconsistent with observations of optical habit during sample picking, 
and they do not agree with estimates of arsenopyrite content implied by mass balance using sample mass and measured Fe. 

Whether the rest of the suite's helium loss was
thermal or accompanied recrystallization is similarly unclear, but
recrystallization seems the less likely explanation, since the most
recent regional metamorphic event that could have recrystallized these
sulfide porphyroblasts predates 2.6 Ga \citep{smith1993,hannington1999}, well before the 465-860 Ma ages recorded here.

\subsection{Arsenopyrite U-Th/He Ages for Gympie Gold Belt}

There is no obvious difference in the geochronological sulfide U-Th-Sm/He data between the HCl-leached and non-leached Gympie arsenopyrite populations (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-gympie-age-vs-eu}),
thus we interpret leaching to be a harmless but unnecessary step in the Gympie arsenopyrite U-Th/He geochronology protocol. 
This step was originally intended to remove any potential surface contamination, but these data do not suggest that the scatter in age is related to 
surface contamination. It is more likely that the scatter in age is related to the variable sizes and interconnectivity of inclusions, which could affect the sulfide's helium retentivity.

Lithostratigraphic age constraints that rely on foraminifera microfossils
seem to suggest an Early Permian age for the Gympie Group rocks that host the
gold-vein systems (\S\ref{sec:ch3-geologic-setting}; \citealp{holcombe1997}). However, the oldest ages reported here are indeed resolvably older than the inferred 
Early Permian upper bound, instead suggesting that the mineralized volcanic pile may be of Carboniferous age.
Indeed, these biostratigraphic constraints are sparse and rest mainly on
correlation higher in the sequence rather than direct radiometric dates
on the volcanic pile itself, and \citet{holcombe1997} note that the lower
Gympie Block volcanics ``may contain older volcanic units.'' The data here support the latter interpretation.

The oldest Gympie ages are interpreted as a record of hydrothermal alteration of the Gympie Block volcanics contemporaneous with $\sim$300-310 Ma regional granitoid magmatism 
(\S\ref{sec:ch3-geologic-setting-gympie}; \citealp{little1995,holcombe1997}). Because the rutile inclusions identified as the U-Th-Sm source in the
Gympie arsenopyrite (\S\ref{ch3-discussion-radiogenic-he-origin}) are small ($\le$20 \textmu m, Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-gympie-inclusions}),
they would self-implant little of their own produced helium, and thus the measured dates cannot be explained by rutile inheritance before a hypothetical sulfide crystallization event at $\sim$235 Ma \citep{cuneen1994,holcombe1997}. 
Thus, the arsenopyrites likely crystallized at or before $\sim$306 Ma, well
before the Au-quartz mineralization event at $\sim$235 Ma.
It is possible that the rutile population predates $\sim$306 Ma, and only started retaining helium after being incorporated into the sulfide hosts, permitting the 
volcanic pile's emplacement age to be older than the oldest sulfide ages. We prefer the interpretation that the sulfide crystallized contemporaneously with metasomatism 
related to the $\sim$300-310 Ma regional granitoid magmatism because it is more feasible that such large sulfides would be metasomatic rather than magmatic in origin. Therefore, the volcanic pile was likely emplaced
sometime before $\sim$306 Ma. Aside from a single young outlier, the youngest Gympie sulfide ages reported here are contemporaneous with the syntectonic Au-mineralization event at
$\sim$235 Ma \citep{cuneen1994,holcombe1997}, which we interpret to represent the timing of a recrystallization event for some of the sulfides. 
At the very least, it is somewhat remarkable that the sulfide U-Th/He ages demonstrate that most samples retained helium through the $\sim$235 Ma hydrothermal event.

Under this interpretation, the eleven reliable Gympie ages
(\S\ref{ch3-results-gympie-ages}) record variable, partial resetting of
the $\sim$300-310 Ma population during the $\sim$235 Ma
hydrothermal mineralizing event, rather than multiple discrete sulfide
generations.

The one clear exception is 062123GyPyHClb, at 191 Ma, the youngest age
in the suite, and the only one below the $\sim$235 Ma mineralization age
itself. A partial-resetting model bounded between a $\sim$306 Ma
inclusion age and a $\sim$235 Ma resetting event cannot, on its own,
produce an age younger than 235 Ma, so this aliquot requires
additional explanation. One speculative possibility is that this particular aliquot's U-Th-Sm-rich 
inclusions sat near or on the surface of the sulfide grains, and thus some of the radiogenic helium was implanted elsewhere and not measured in the aliquot.
Some Gympie samples contain coarser alkali feldspar inclusions identified alongside the rutile
(Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-gympie-inclusions}). If there were rutile crystals at the interface of the sulfide and the feldspar, then some 
of the rutiles' radiogenic helium may have been implanted into the feldspar and lost over geologic time, since feldspar is not normally considered a
particularly helium-retentive phase \citep{hurley1941}. In any case, we point out that this single age is only one of eleven, and therefore seems to be the exception rather than the rule.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:ch3-conclusions}

Sulfide geochronology has so far relied almost entirely on the Re-Os
system, which is compositionally restricted to a handful of phases and
analytically restricted to a few specialized laboratories
(\S\ref{sec:ch3-introduction}). This chapter tested whether U-Th/He
dating of pyrite and arsenopyrite can serve as an accessible complement to Re-Os geochronology
through two lenses: direct application to two independently
age-constrained deposits at Kidd Creek and Gympie
(\S\ref{sec:ch3-geologic-setting}), and a set of sulfur-buffered helium
step heat experiments designed to characterize the underlying
diffusion and migration behavior of helium in pyrite, arsenopyrite, galena, and chalcopyrite.

The step heat experiments show that pyrite and arsenopyrite are
remarkably helium-retentive against thermal loss: cumulative fractional yields never
exceeded 5\% for any pyrite or arsenopyrite experiment, and never
exceeded 2\% for the undeformed Leadville pyrite
specifically, even after repeated heating cycles up to 600\textdegree C 
(\S\ref{ch3-discussion-retentivity}). Placed on a common diffusive-dose
axis against independently characterized phases, pyrite and arsenopyrite
sit at or beyond the retentive extreme of the compilation, on par with
or exceeding zircon and monazite and substantially exceeding hematite
and goethite (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch3-fo-comparison}). Arsenopyrite is less
retentive than pyrite, consistent with its lower-symmetry,
arsenic-bearing structure (\S\ref{ch3-discussion-arsenopyrite}), but is similarly retentive to zircon and
markedly more retentive than galena or the copper sulfides,
which are not considered promising U-Th/He targets (\S\ref{ch3-si-cu-pb-sulfides}).

The step heat data also point to two distinct helium-loss pathways
within pyrite itself. Deformed, fractured Rustenburg pyrite shows steep,
retrograde-dependent Arrhenius behavior best explained by a
short-circuit or pipe-diffusion pathway along pre-existing fractures
\citep{lee1992}, whereas the visually homogeneous, irradiated Leadville
pyrite follows a simpler, shallower-sloped trend consistent with multiple-domain volume
diffusion (\S\ref{ch3-compare-pyrites-discussion}). Varying grain size
and sulfur-liquid contact within the Leadville suite did not resolve a
clear control on this behavior beyond a surface-area-driven nugget
effect, in which smaller aliquots simply carry a larger proportion 
of fine pyrite dust (\S\ref{ch3-leadville-pyrite-sulfur-liquid-control}).

Applied to natural samples, the U-Th/He ages obtained here are not
simple crystallization or cooling ages. Eleven of twelve Kidd Creek
pyrite/arsenopyrite aliquots give ages of 465-860 Ma (mean 639 $\pm$ 36
Ma, 1$\sigma$, n=11), decisively younger than the $\sim$2716 Ma host
rhyolite \citep{hannington1999} but older than the regional 392 $\pm$ 32
Ma apatite cooling age \citep{sturrock2024}; the twelfth instead gives a
much older, 1596 $\pm$ 46 Ma age. Gympie
arsenopyrite ages range from 191 to 306 Ma (mean 258 $\pm$ 35 Ma, 1$\sigma$,
n=11; \S\ref{ch3-results-gympie-ages}). Except for a single young outlier, these ages are bracketed by the regional
$\sim$300-310 Ma magmatic episode and the $\sim$235 Ma gold-vein
mineralizing event (\S\ref{sec:ch3-geologic-setting}), and thus the sulfides are interpreted 
to have crystallized during metasomatism related to regional plutonism at $\sim$306 Ma, then variably reset by the 
final mineralizing event at $\sim$235 Ma. 

Neither pyrite nor arsenopyrite itself hosts appreciable U or Th, instead relying on
U-Th-Sm-bearing accessory inclusions to supply the sulfide hosts'
alpha-emitters and radiogenic helium: apatite at Kidd Creek, rutile at
Gympie. The helium from small, dispersed inclusions is implanted
entirely within the sulfide host (and is thus subject to the sulfide's
own helium retentivity), but larger inclusions will partially
self-implant, complicating the interpretation of the sulfide U-Th-Sm/He ages
(\S\ref{ch3-discussion-radiogenic-he-origin}). At Kidd Creek, a simple
nugget-effect model explains the anti-correlation between age and Th/He
content among the eleven-aliquot array, but the observed sizes of the
apatite inclusions are too small to explain ages this young if this
population behaved as a closed system: most of it must have experienced
helium loss during its long history. The twelfth, oldest aliquot is
instead consistent with the closed-system prediction at the observed
inclusion size, though this rests on a single measurement that could
equally reflect incomplete sample digestion rather than a genuinely more
retentive phase (\S\ref{ch3-discussion-kiddcreek-ages}).

Together, these results support pyrite and arsenopyrite U-Th/He dating
as a viable complement to Re-Os geochronology in multistage sulfide
systems, but only once the identity and grain size of the actual
radiogenic host phase are established for a given locality
(\S\ref{ch3-discussion-radiogenic-he-origin}): no single digestion
protocol can be assumed to transfer between deposits. Extending this
framework to the other sulfide-hosted geochronometers proposed in
\S\ref{sec:ch3-introduction}, and to a wider range of deposits than the
two tested here, remains the natural next step.
