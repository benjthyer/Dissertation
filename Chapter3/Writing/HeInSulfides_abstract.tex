Sulfide mineralization is a near-ubiquitous process in the formation of
ore deposits of valuable chalcophile and siderophile elements. Helium
isotope studies of sulfides have been applied to infer the origins of
mineralizing hydrothermal fluids, and some pioneering studies have
applied the U-Th/He method to date sulfides, especially pyrite
\citep{yakubovich2020,yakubovich2021}. Yet, the interpretation of helium
isotope data or U-Th/He ages requires an understanding of the conditions
under which helium in sulfides behaves as an open or closed system, as
well as the mechanisms of helium loss, a process which is poorly
understood in sulfides. Furthermore, sulfides systematically reject U-Th and only 
produce appreciable radiogenic helium when they contain U-Th bearing inclusions, further
complicating the interpretation of U-Th/He data in sulfides. We explore the systematics of U-Th/He in
sulfides through two lenses: 1) an example application of the sulfide
U-Th/He geochronometer to two test cases, and 2)
helium step heat experiments of sulfide phases. We report
pyrite/arsenopyrite U-Th/He ages for Au-ores in the Permian-Triassic Gympie formation of
Queensland, Australia, and the Archean Kidd Creek VMS deposit of Ontario, Canada. The Gympie arsenopyrite yields a mean age of 258 $\pm$ 35 Ma and spans the range between
reported Ar/Ar and K/Ar ages of the country rock and the mineralized quartz veins \citep{holcombe1997, little1995}, suggesting the sulfides predate and were variably reset by the primary mineralization event. 
Pyrite/arsenopyrite from the 2.7 Ga Kidd Creek VMS deposit of Ontario contain large apatite inclusions ($r \leq 30~\mu$m) and span a range of ages from 465-1600 Ma. Although one aliquot can be explained as a perfectly retentive 2.7 Ga sulfide host of apatite inclusions that record a $\sim$370 Ma cooling age, the remaining aliquots are too young to be explained by a closed sulfide host and instead require that the sulfide host lost helium over geologic time.


Helium step heat experiments in sulfides are challenging because
sulfides readily react/decompose in the high vacuum, high temperature
conditions of the experiments, producing results that reflect phase
decomposition/reaction kinetics that are not geologically relevant. We
address this challenge by performing helium step heat experiments at
temperatures up to 600\degC on natural single-crystal pyrite, arsenopyrite,
and galena implanted with uniform 3He through proton bombardment sealed
in helium-permeable silica glass tubes stabilized under high sulfur (±
arsenic) fugacity to stabilize the phase against decomposition during analysis. We
were unsuccessful in extending this method to stabilize chalcopyrite,
chalcocite, or marcasite during step heat experiments: the helium
retentivity of these phases in nature remains unclear.
Retrograde-prograde thermal cycles on sulfur-stabilized galena support a
Multiple Diffusion Domain (MDD) helium loss mechanism with an activation
energy around \ChThreeCCpEa\ kJ/mol, but with realtively low helium retentivity 
that makes it unlikely to be useful as a target for helium isotope studies orU-Th/He geochronology.  
Comparisons of helium step heat data of
sulfur-stabilized pristine and deformed pyrite point towards a multipath
diffusion model for deformed pyrites, in which dislocations and fine fractures serve as high
activation energy (\ChThreeRustEa\ $\pm$ \ChThreeRustEaErr\ kJ/mol) fast
pathways, with bulk volume diffusion following a
lower activation energy that is constrained by step heats on pristine pyrite samples: \ChThreeLVCOEa\ $\pm$ \ChThreeLVCOEaErr\
kJ/mol. Helium step heat data of arsenopyrite are complex, potentially
complicated by exchange of sulfur and arsenic with the vapor phase, but
qualitatively demonstrate very high helium retentivity. These
experiments demonstrate that pyrite is similarly
helium-retentive to monzonite-structure phosphates and is more
retentive of helium than natural zircon, apatite, hematite, or goethite. Arsenopyrite
is less retentive, more similar to the most helium-retentive zircon.
