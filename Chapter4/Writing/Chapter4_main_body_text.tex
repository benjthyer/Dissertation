\section{Introduction}\label{ch4-introduction}

\subsection{Motivation}\label{ch4-motivation}
Free energy minimizers translate a bulk composition and two intensive variables into a stable phase assemblage, the
abundance and composition of every phase, and the physical properties that follow from them. The \textsc{MELTS} family of models
\citep{ghiorsosack1995,ghiorso2002,gualda2012,ghiorsogualda2015} serves this role for silicate liquids and crustal to
uppermost-mantle conditions, and \textsc{HeFESTo} \citep{stixrude2005,stixrude2011,stixrude2024} serves it for the solid mantle
from the surface to the core--mantle boundary. Perple\_X \citep{connolly2005} and \textsc{MAGEMin} \citep{riel2022} utilize the 
database and formulations of~\citet{holland2011},~\citet{holland2018},~and~\citet{holland1998}, which apply from the surface to the 
core--mantle boundary and support liquids. 
Each equilibrium requires the solution of a constrained, nonlinear optimization problem, and
within a larger workflow that solution is often the slowest step. A single \textsc{MAGEMin} minimization takes on the order of
$10^{-1}$~s depending on the water content of the system \citep{riel2022,candioti2026}, whereas \textsc{MELTS} is an order of magnitude faster ($10^{-2}$~s per assemblage), 
and \textsc{HeFESTo} is an order of magnitude slower (seconds per assemblage). Efficient coupling to a geodynamic
model requires less than 1 ms per point \citep{kerswell2024,candioti2026}.
Minimizers are also largely serial, while geodynamic solvers are built for distributed or shared-memory parallelism, so the
thermodynamic calculation dominates the run time of a coupled model \citep{yuan2026,candioti2026}. Robustness is a second cost. A
\textsc{MELTS} calculation that fails to converge can halt a batch of calculations or require the library to be reinitialized,
which has limited the use of Monte Carlo methods with these models \citep{gleeson2025}.

The common workaround is to precompute a lookup table of properties over pressure and temperature for one or a few bulk
compositions and to interpolate within it during the geodynamic calculation \citep{connolly2005,zunino2011,rummel2020,yuan2026}. This works
well when composition is fixed but becomes impractical when composition is itself a variable, because tracking compositional
evolution requires interpolation in a space of ten or more dimensions \citep{rummel2020,candioti2026}. The problems for which
thermodynamic coupling matters most are exactly those in which composition varies: open-ended searches over composition space,
inversions that treat composition as an unknown, and forward models in which every parcel of rock carries its own chemical history.
These problems need a forward model that is fast, robust, and as general in composition as the thermodynamic model it replaces.
Several such problems, described below, motivate this chapter.

\paragraph{Compositional inversion of seismic and geophysical data.} Gibbs energy minimization inside a stochastic sampler allows
geophysical observations to be inverted directly for mantle composition and thermal state, rather than for seismic velocity alone
\citep{khan2008}. \citet{khan2008} applied this approach to the Love number, tidal dissipation, mean density and moment of inertia
of Mars in the system CaO--FeO--MgO--Al$_2$O$_3$--SiO$_2$. \citet{khan2021} later inverted InSight body-wave travel times for the
upper mantle structure of Mars, but assumed a single homogeneous bulk composition derived from the martian meteorites, so that the
sampled parameters were primarily thermal. Each forward model in such an inversion is a column of equilibria, and a sampler must
evaluate many such columns, so the cost of minimization limits how many compositional dimensions can be explored. On Earth, the
seismic character of the mantle transition zone is sensitive to composition at a level that such inversions could exploit. The
post-spinel transition in the Mg--Fe binary spans only $\sim$0.01\,GPa, or about 250\,m, at mantle compositions and temperatures,
which accounts for the observed sharpness of the 660-km discontinuity without requiring chemically distinct upper and lower mantles
\citep{ishii2019}. The dissolution of aluminous garnet into bridgmanite, in contrast, is expected to produce steep but gradual
gradients in velocity and density between about 660 and 750\,km \citep{wood1996}. Surrogate-based sensitivity analysis suggests that
the wadsleyite--ringwoodite transition and the akimotoite-out reaction near 660\,km both shift with mantle fertility
\citep{hartmeier2026}. Whether lateral variations in discontinuity depth and sharpness reflect temperature, composition, or both is
therefore a question that a fast, composition-aware forward model could address directly.

\paragraph{Composition of the lowermost mantle.} The large low-velocity provinces (LLVPs) and ultralow-velocity zones (ULVZs) at the
base of the mantle are commonly interpreted as compositionally distinct material. Proposed origins vary wildly, including a crystallizing basal
magma ocean \citep{labrosse2007}, subducted banded iron formations \citep{dobson2005}, and iron-rich mantle material from the
Moon-forming impactor that is 2.0--3.5\% denser than the ambient mantle \citep{yuan2023}. \citet{jackson2021} found that assemblages
containing iron-rich (Mg,Fe)O match the seismic velocity reductions and density increases of many ULVZ case studies, and that the
range of observations allows multiple distinct but coincident origins. Testing these hypotheses requires searching for compositions,
including unusual ones unknown on the Earth's surface, whose equilibrium assemblages reproduce observed velocity reductions at core--mantle boundary conditions.
That search is open-ended in composition space.

\paragraph{A chemically heterogeneous mantle.} The Sr--Nd--Pb isotopic record of oceanic island basalts is described by four
principal mantle end-members (DMM, HIMU, EMI and EMII) with distinct compositions and proposed origins, from recycled sediment to intra-mantle
metasomatism \citep{hart1988,stracke2005}. The major
element and lithological consequences of this heterogeneity are also observable. A mechanical mixture of basalt and harzburgite
has a different phase assemblage and seismic velocity from pyrolite of the same bulk composition, and such a mixture matches
transition-zone seismic observations better than pyrolite \citep{xu2008,stixrude2012}. Geodynamic models that compute mineral
physics self-consistently, however, often represent this heterogeneity with a single variable, the basalt fraction in a
basalt--harzburgite mixture (e.g., \citealp{nakagawa2010,yuan2026}). That parameterization cannot represent the full range of diverse compositions implied by
the geochemical record, nor the fate of subducted material that falls off the basalt--harzburgite join, such as sediments, altered
oceanic crust, or banded iron formations. A flexible approach to modeling this heterogeneity is needed to capture the full complexity 
of the mantle's composition and dynamics to be able to explain the observed seismic anomalies and plumes \citep{keller2023,dobson2005}.

\paragraph{Magma ocean solidification.} Fractional crystallization of a magma ocean is expected to produce a gravitationally unstable
cumulate stratigraphy that overturns \citep{elkins-tanton2003,elkins-tanton2011}. One-dimensional overturn models assume that the
cumulates reorganize on every scale into a monotonic density profile, and \citet{elkins-tanton2003} note that viscosity is likely to
limit that reorganization and leave a laterally heterogeneous mantle. \citet{miyazaki2022} proposed that Rayleigh--Taylor
instabilities with length scales below 100\,km leave iron-rich blobs embedded in a high-Mg\# pyroxenite matrix, and that a wet
mantle of this kind permits plate tectonics and removes atmospheric CO$_2$ more than ten times faster than a homogeneous pyrolitic
mantle. Testing this hypothesis in a forward geodynamic model requires an equation of state for every parcel as its composition
and depth change, which is the case that lookup tables handle worst.

\paragraph{Igneous processes at smaller scales.} Recent works have made impressive progress developing physical models for the mechanical behavior of open-system magma mushes \citep{bergantz2015}. 
Such models aim to describe thermally and chemically heterogeneous and evolving material whose physical properties are a direct consequence of composition and temperature as
described by thermodynamics, but are necessarily simplified for computational efficiency. Similarly, melt migration within a magma mush is a complex process that is well studied, 
but produces geochemical signatures which are poorly understood \citep{ohara1981,lesher1988,maier2020,yao2021}. Thermodynamic models of reactive porous 
flow may elucidate these signatures, but current models treat each melt--mush reaction as a single step, run until the percolating melt and the mush reach thermal equilibrium, 
whereas natural samples record a protracted, open-system process \citep{boulanger2023} that may be better approximated by spatiotemporally-resolved, local-equilibrium thermodynamics that 
is significantly more computationally expensive.

\subsection{Previous Work}\label{ch4-prev-work}

Machine-learning surrogates have been developed to address the cost of phase equilibrium calculations, and those built to date
are each scoped to a particular application. \citet{kerswell2024} trained models that predict bulk physical properties, such as
density and seismic velocity, of peridotite. \citet{candioti2026} validated the \citet{holland2018} igneous database in
\textsc{MAGEMin} against crystallization experiments on arc magmas and then trained a multi-task neural network on
$\sim$980{,}000 \textsc{MAGEMin} equilibria. Their training data span 0.1--10\,kbar, 650--1300\,\textdegree C, and 100 synthetic
compositions. The network predicts the presence, fraction and
density of liquid and fluid, the liquid composition, and the system density. Solid properties are computed afterward by difference
from the system, liquid and fluid properties. Individual minerals are not predicted. The authors report that it runs about 3{,}000 times 
faster than \textsc{MAGEMin} on 64 CPU threads, framing their results as a proof of concept. They recommend retraining on application-specific data, 
and argue that a single surrogate spanning porous, mush and suspension flow regimes is impractical \citep{candioti2026}.

\citet{hartmeier2026} present a particularly impressive example of a machine-learning surrogate. They trained a combined classifier 
and regressor on two million \textsc{MAGEMin} equilibria computed with the \citet{stixrude2022} database. It predicts the fractions of 
20 phases and the compositions of the solid solutions in the system Na$_2$O--CaO--FeO--MgO--Al$_2$O$_3$--SiO$_2$ over 1--40\,GPa and 500--2500\,\textdegree C. 
Conservation of mass and compositional closure are softly enforced, entering the loss function as penalties. They achieve relative errors around 1\% 
for phase fractions and 0.5\% for solid solution compositions. The model's classifier delivers impressive precision and recall, mostly 95\%
and up.  \citet{hartmeier2026} also showed that the surrogate's analytical gradients
identify which inputs control each phase boundary, a use of differentiability that conventional minimizers do not offer.

The IGyTheM model (Integrating Geodynamics with Thermodynamics using Machine Learning; \citealp{yuan2025igythem}) couples a
\textsc{HeFESTo}-trained surrogate to the Citcom mantle convection code, so that thermodynamically derived density enters the
momentum equation. Its training database covers 0--140\,GPa and 273--4273\,K. It represents the mantle as a mechanical mixture
of basalt and harzburgite, so composition enters as a single mixing variable.

These surrogates show that neural networks can replace minimizers at a fraction of the cost. Each, however, emulates a narrow
slice of its parent model, defined by a compositional subspace (arc magmas, or the basalt--harzburgite join), a pressure range, a
volatile treatment, or a limited choice of outputs. None of them can be used in place of its parent model for an arbitrary task that the
parent model could perform.

\subsection{Properties and Innovations of nGibbs}\label{ch4-innovations}

\emph{nGibbs} is designed as a general emulator: one that can stand in for its parent thermodynamic model for essentially any
calculation that model would be used for, within the parent model's own range of validity. Its released emulators reproduce
\textsc{HeFESTo} for Earth (0--140\,GPa) and Mars (0--25.5\,GPa), and the closed-system isothermal forms of \textsc{MELTS} 1.0.2 and 1.2.0, with
and without chromium. Emulators for open-system (buffered oxygen fugacity) \textsc{MELTS} calculations have been
trained but are not yet released. Similarly to \citet{hartmeier2026}, each emulator predicts the quantities that define the equilibrium assemblage: the 
phase abundance and chemistry. Building upon \citet{yuan2026}, \emph{nGibbs} computes physical properties which are the thermodynamic consequence of an equilibrium assemblage. 

Four properties distinguish \emph{nGibbs} from the surrogates above. First, its training and validation compositions are drawn from whole-rock
databases of igneous and, for \textsc{MELTS} 1.2.0, sedimentary rocks, that are
perturbed to cover exotic compositions that are rare or unknown at Earth's surface (\S\ref{sec:datagen}). Second, conservation of mass is enforced at
inference rather than only encouraged during training: an iterative, constrained correction adapted from \citet{asimow1998} adjusts
the predicted assemblage until it reproduces the input bulk composition exactly, without allowing any elemental abundance to become
negative (\S\ref{sec:massbalance}). Third, phase abundances are continuous across phase boundaries, because the gate and the magnitude of each phase are the same variable
(\S\ref{sec:continuous}). Fourth, through \textsc{PetThermoTools} \citep{gleeson2025}, they are called through the same interface as
\textsc{alphaMELTS}, so that existing workflows can seamlessly switch between the emulator and the parent model for benchmarking
(\S\ref{ch4-ptt-integration}).

\section{Methods}\label{ch4-methods}

\subsection{Network architecture}
\label{sec:continuous}

\subsubsection{Motivation: the discontinuity problem}
\label{sec:continuous-motivation}

Before the introduction of the continuous-saturation framework described below, the early \emph{nGibbs} architecture predicted phase presence with a binary classifier
and phase abundance with a distinct regressor, multiplying the two so that the predicted
abundance is the regressed magnitude gated by a thresholded presence probability. 
This gated model was simple and easy to train, but it has a structural defect.
Because the gate and the magnitude were distinct functions,
nothing tied the magnitude to zero as the gate closes. Therefore, when the presence probability of a phase
crosses its threshold, the predicted abundance jumps discontinuously from a finite
value to exactly zero, which is (for most phases) an unexpected behavior for a mixed system.

Natural systems of interest to geologists and planetary scientists are typically complex, chemically-mixed systems. These systems, and the thermodynamic models that describe them, 
behave continuously. In addition to the continuous quality of phase abundance, thermodynamic models like \textsc{MELTS} and \textsc{HeFESTo} also track each phase's chemical affinity, 
which is a continuous quality that measures the free energy cost of introducing a metastable phase to the system. 
Even when the phase is absent, the chemical affinity is a meaningful, continuous quantity that smoothly goes to zero as the phase approaches saturation. 
Likewise, at a phase-out boundary, the abundance of the departing phase goes to zero smoothly, linearly in reaction progress. A gated model is
therefore structurally wrong precisely at the phase boundaries that constitute the most
interesting content of a phase diagram.

The architecture used for all released models removes this defect by making the gate
and the magnitude the same variable, analogously to a prediction of chemical affinity. Some emulators predict a scaled chemical affinity, while 
others simply activate their continuous molar output with a ReLU function. The models trained with a scaled affinity perform slightly better, 
but require a more complex data processing pipeline that cannot be applied to datasets where the data are not structured in paths that hold 
a state variable constant. Because the HeFESTo data were generated along mock-isentropic pressure-temperature paths (and not true isentropes), 
the HeFESTo emulators were trained with the simpler, ReLU-activated architecture, hereafter referred to as the affinity-naive architecture. 
The MELTS emulators, which were trained on data generated by isobaric cooling, were trained with scaled affinities with an architecture 
hereafter referred to as affinity-aware.

\subsubsection{Inputs}

Each emulator takes pressure and one further intensive variable (temperature
for the isothermal models, specific entropy for the isentropic models) and a bulk
composition as inputs. The emulators are trained on and expect the same units for pressure, temperature, and entropy that
the real thermodynamic MELTS and HeFESTo use: kelvin and GPa for HeFESTo, and degrees Celsius and bars for MELTS. Both expect specific 
entropy in units of J/g/K. nGibbs receives as input normalized cation atomic abundances, following $\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{E}$ with $\sum_e b_e = 1$. 
Compositions are supplied by the user either as oxide weight percents or as element
molar abundances for any model; oxide inputs are automatically converted internally to the normalized cation abundances expected by nGibbs.

Entropy is offered as a useful alternative to temperature because isentropic paths are the
natural coordinate for the convecting mantle \citep{davies1977,brown1981,asimow1997,asimow2001,ganguly2005,tirone2016}. Supplying $S$ directly removes an inner iteration for $T$ at every pressure step, which is the dominant cost of constructing
an adiabat by conventional means.

The full non-compositional feature vector (i.e. pressure, temperature, entropy, \emph{f}O\textsubscript{2}) is min--max normalized to $[0,1]$. The minima and maxima used for these normalizations are fit on the
training data, and are carried by the emulator and automatically applied during inference.
This avoids presenting the network with inputs on a
different scale than the one it was trained on, leading to errors that would be difficult to diagnose.

The neural networks driving \emph{nGibbs} expect that the input composition vector sum to a total of one. 
During inference, this is explicitly enforced by a renormalization so that, similarly to \textsc{MELTS} and \textsc{HeFESTo}, 
the user can supply a composition that sums to values other than one for convenience. 
The outputs of \emph{nGibbs}, however, are not rescaled and will always map to a cation sum of one regardless of user input.


\subsubsection{Compositional basis}

Three compositional bases are used. \emph{Components} ($m$) are the endmembers of the reference code's
solution models ($C$ in total across all phases); \emph{oxides} ($O$) are accepted as an input
because it is conventional to measure and report rock compositions as oxides; and \emph{elements} ($E$) are the conserved
quantities that \emph{nGibbs} conserves. Ferric and ferrous iron are distinguished as oxides but not as
elements for models with oxygen buffering, for which $O = E+1$, but closed system models distinguish ferric and ferrous 
iron in the element basis as well so that they are conserved: $O = E$.  Conversion between bases is exact stoichiometry, expressed as
fixed matrices that relate components to oxides and oxides to elements. The product of the two, $A$, is the most commonly
used stoichiometry matrix because it relates model outputs to the bulk elemental composition it conserves. These matrices are model constants,
not fitted parameters. The phases and components of the \textsc{MELTS} and \textsc{HeFESTo} emulators, with the formula each component
carries in the component-to-oxide matrix, are listed in Tables~\ref{tab:ch4-components-melts} and~\ref{tab:ch4-components-hefesto}.

\emph{nGibbs} models closed systems that conserve oxygen content, as well as open systems where oxygen fugacity is imposed. 
Iron is the major redox-active element in \emph{nGibbs}. Every element besides iron is tracked at a single, 
fixed oxidation state (with a limited exception for carbon) and therefore the oxygen content of the system is fully specified by the ferric-to-total iron ratio. 
The only other redox-active element is carbon in \textsc{MELTS} 1.2 emulators, wherein carbon exists as an oxide in melt, 
fluid, and calcite, and as reduced carbon in graphite. Graphite is represented as C(FeO)\textsubscript{4}(Fe\textsubscript{2}O\textsubscript{3})\textsubscript{-2} in oxide space 
in order to conserve oxygen, which introduces strong coupling between Fe$^{2+}$/Fe$^{3+}$ and graphite for closed-system \textsc{MELTS} 1.2.0 models. This coupling vanishes for 
open system models where oxygen is not conserved. 
Similarly, native iron phases are represented as (FeO)\textsubscript{3}(Fe\textsubscript{2}O\textsubscript{3})\textsubscript{-1} in oxide space in \emph{nGibbs}.

\emph{nGibbs} accepts one of two inputs that define the redox state: an explicit, distinct definition of both 
FeO and Fe$_2$O$_3$, which the closed-system models conserve; or an oxygen fugacity (\emph{f}O\textsubscript{2}) 
in log unit offset from the fayalite-magnetite-quartz buffer ($\Delta$FMQ), which the open-system models impose. 
Oxygen buffering is available in neither \textsc{HeFESTo} nor its \emph{nGibbs} emulator. 
In these open models, only cation mass is conserved and the oxygen content of the system is a consequence 
of the predicted phase assemblage, except for the liquid, for which total iron is speciated following the procedure 
of \citet{kress1991}.

\subsubsection{Forward pass}

The network sees the input vector state variables $u$ and bulk composition $b$, represented as the vector $[u, b]$ and:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item a shared encoder produces a central working representation of the system's state;
  \item one small network per phase gives the signed abundance/affinity $g$;
  \item one softmax-activated network per chemically-variable phase gives that phase's endmember fractions $\hat x$.
\end{enumerate}

This workflow is illustrated for a single phase in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch4-forward-pass}.

During inference, the predicted abundance is the rectified affinity,
  \begin{equation}
    \hat n = \max(g, 0).
  \end{equation}
Phases and chemical endmembers that contain an element that is absent from $b$ are forced to be zero. 

Component moles and the reconstructed bulk composition follow from stoichiometry:
\begin{align}
  \hat m &= (\hat x V + b) \circ (\hat n M), \label{eq:m}\\
  \hat b &= \hat m A. \label{eq:bhat}
\end{align}
Here $\circ$ is the element-wise product, so $\hat m$ is each component's phase abundance
multiplied by its fraction in that phase. Conservation of mass (adjusting $\hat m$ until $\hat b = b$) is enforced only at inference (\S\ref{sec:massbalance}).

\subsubsection{Thermodynamic justification for softmax activated phase composition prediction}\label{ch4-softmax}

The softmax activation function produces a positive, nonzero output that sums to 1, and is most often applied in machine learning for classification problems in tandem with 
cross-entropy loss, as its outputs are often interpreted as a probability or likelihood distribution over categorical classes \citep{holm2023,pearce2021,li2019,yang2018}.  
For class $i$ of $\phi$ categorical classes, the softmax function is $e^{z_i} / \sum_{j\in\phi} e^{z_j}$ where $z_i$ is the logit (linear dense layer output) for class $i$. 

The softmax function is used here for a bounded regression problem, to predict the mole fractions of chemical endmembers in a phase, which are also positive, nonzero, and sum to 1. 
Originally, we chose the softmax function because of this quality, and early tests showed it outperformed alternative activations (such as a renormalization of ReLU outputs) 
for predicting chemical endmember fraction. Although this application of the softmax is unusual, it is justified in that its functional form mirrors that of the thermodynamic 
problem of predicting chemical endmember fractions in a phase at equilibrium.

For a general solution, the chemical potential of endmember $i$ in phase $\phi$ is
$\mu_i = \mu_i^{\circ}(P,T) + RT\ln a_i$, with activity $a_i = \gamma_i x_i$ \citep[e.g.,][]{anderson2005,ganguly2008}. The activity coefficient $\gamma_i \equiv a_i/x_i$
collects the departure from ideal molecular mixing, including excess (interaction)
terms and multi-site configurational effects. At equilibrium, $\mu_i$ equals the apparent chemical potential
$\mu_i^{*}$ that the coexisting assemblage imposes on that endmember, so
\begin{equation}
  \ln x_i \;=\; z_i \;\equiv\; \frac{\mu_i^{*} - \mu_i^{\circ}(P,T) - RT\ln\gamma_i}{RT}.
  \label{eq:nonideal_z}
\end{equation}

The standard state chemical potential $\mu_i^{\circ}(P,T)$ and the non-ideal contribution ($RT\ln\gamma_i$) may be gathered together in 
an effective reference chemical potential $\tilde{\mu}_i^{\circ}(P,T) \equiv \mu_i^{\circ}(P,T) + RT\ln\gamma_i$.

\begin{equation}
  \ln x_i \;=\; z_i \;\equiv\; \frac{\mu_i^{*} - \tilde{\mu}_i^{\circ}(P,T)}{RT}.
  \label{eq:gathered_z}
\end{equation}

Imposing the constraint that the sum of the chemical end members is 1 ($\sum_{i\in\phi} x_i = 1$), it follows that

\begin{equation}
  x_i \;=\; \frac{x_i}{\sum_{j\in\phi} x_j} \;=\; \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_{j\in\phi} e^{z_j}}
  \;=\; \text{softmax} (\bm{z})_i .
  \label{eq:ideal_softmax}
\end{equation}

so the softmax is the exact functional form describing the equilibrium composition as a function 
of the chemical potential with respect to a reference state, normalized by $RT$. The network does not 
need to track the non-ideal terms because it predicts the logits $\hat{z}_i$ that satisfy it
directly, which absorbs the activity model into the learned mapping from
$(P, T, \text{bulk composition})$ to $\bm{z}$. In the ideal limit ($\gamma_i = 1$),
the logit output z represents a unitless chemical potential of each endmember relative to the reference state.

\subsection{A non-negative, closure-preserving reparameterization of the
pyroxene and spinel components of the \textsc{MELTS} models}
\label{sec:pxsp}

The softmax function always produces outputs that are strictly positive and sum to one, so the network
cannot emit a non-physical composition (e.g. negative atoms) for phases composed of components that are themselves 
legal compositions, regardless of its unconstrained raw output. For spinels and pyroxenes, however,
\textsc{MELTS} predicts legal compositions that are outside the simplex defined by the end members 
(i.e., some components can be negative). A softmax activation therefore cannot be applied to these components directly, 
but these compositions can be remapped to another basis that is compatible with a softmax activation: 
components that are non-negative and sum to one.

Even for spinels and pyroxenes, which can contain negative components, there exists a limit lying outside
the simplex that expresses legal compositions, implying that there exists some compositional basis that 
can remap legal compositions to lie within the simplex. We were able to analytically derive a solution 
for pyroxene, but spinel was instead solved numerically. All other phases' compositional bases in 
\textsc{MELTS} and \textsc{HeFESTo} are already strictly non-negative.

\subsubsection{Pyroxene} We write the mole fractions of the seven \textsc{MELTS} pyroxene
components (diopside, clinoenstatite, hedenbergite, alumino-buffonite, buffonite,
essenite, jadeite) as $x_0,\dots,x_6$. Pyroxene has the general formula
M2\,M1\,T$_2$O$_6$, with one large octahedral site (M2), one small octahedral site
(M1), and two tetrahedral sites (T); the site occupancies of the seven components
follow \citet{sackghiorso1994} and are given in Table~\ref{tab:pxsites}. The
atoms-per-formula-unit totals implied by that table are
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{Ca} &= x_0+x_2+x_3+x_4+x_5, &
  \mathrm{Na} &= x_6, \nonumber\\
  \mathrm{Mg} &= x_0+2x_1+\tfrac12(x_3+x_4), &
  \mathrm{Al} &= x_3+x_5+x_6, \label{eq:pxapfu}\\
  \mathrm{Fe}^{3+} &= x_4+x_5, &
  \mathrm{Ti} &= \tfrac12(x_3+x_4). \nonumber
\end{align}

Seven components subject to closure leave six independent directions. These separate
naturally into three groups. Three elements are carried by exactly one component
each and can be read off directly: Fe$^{2+}$ (hedenbergite), Na (jadeite), and Mg on
the M2 site specifically (clinoenstatite, the Mg--Ca exchange vector distinct from
the M1 magnesium already carried by diopside). Three further elements---Fe$^{3+}$,
Al and Ti---are each split across two or three components, so no single component
fraction stands in for them; the corresponding coordinates are obtained by requiring
their pairwise sums to equal the element totals of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pxapfu},
\begin{equation}
  r_1+r_3 = \mathrm{Al}, \qquad r_2+r_3 = \mathrm{Fe}^{3+}, \qquad
  r_1+r_2 = 2\,\mathrm{Ti},
\end{equation}
which is a $3\times3$ system with the unique solution given below. The remaining two
quantities, Ca and Si, are never tracked directly; they are pinned by requiring each
crystallographic site to be exactly full---a second, local layer of closure nested
inside the overall constraint $\sum_i x_i = 1$. Collecting these,
\begin{equation}
  r_0=x_2,\quad r_1=x_3+\tfrac12 x_6,\quad r_2=x_4-\tfrac12 x_6,\quad
  r_3=x_5+\tfrac12 x_6,\quad r_4=x_6,\quad r_5=x_1 .
  \label{eq:pxr}
\end{equation}
Each $r_i$ is bounded for any physically legal pyroxene, but not on $[0,1]$.
Rescaling and recombining gives six quantities that are individually bounded on
$[0,1]$:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{gathered}
    u_0=\tfrac12 r_0,\quad u_1=\tfrac12(r_1+r_2),\quad u_2=\tfrac12(r_1+r_3),\\
    u_3=\tfrac12(r_2+r_3),\quad u_4=\tfrac12 r_4,\quad u_5=\tfrac12 r_4+r_5 .
  \end{gathered}
  \label{eq:pxu}
\end{equation}

Unit-sum closure of the full transformed vector is a separate matter and
does \emph{not} follow from site positivity. Because $\sum_i x_i = 1$, the constant 
$u_C \equiv 1 = \sum_i x_i$ is itself a linear function of the mole fractions and can 
be appended as a seventh coordinate. This makes the map square (and hence invertible), 
and gives the diopside reference endmember, for which $u_0,\dots,u_5$ all vanish, a non-zero value in the transformed space. 
The resulting vector $(u_C,u_0,\dots,u_5)$ is non-negative for any legal pyroxene but does not sum to one. 
Closure was imposed by evaluating the linear map of
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:pxr}--\eqref{eq:pxu} at each of the seven pure endmembers and dividing
each resulting row by its own row sum, yielding the fixed matrix
$\mathsf{U}_{\mathrm{px}}$ of Table~\ref{tab:pxmatrix}, in which every row is
individually non-negative and sums to unity.

compositions in the training data yields vectors that are non-negative and sum to
unity, and $\mathsf{U}_{\mathrm{px}}^{-1}$ recovers the original mole fractions 
within numeric precision.

\subsubsection{Spinel} We were unable to reduce the spinel components (chromite, hercynite, magnetite, spinel
\emph{sensu stricto}, ulv\"ospinel) to a comparably clean set of site-occupancy inequalities. No analogous
analytic transform was therefore pursued. Instead a masked, invertible linear
transform $\mathsf{U}_{\mathrm{sp}}$ was fit numerically.

The matrix was parameterized as lower triangular, which guarantees invertibility for
any non-zero diagonal. Chromite uniquely expresses Cr contents and is therefore always 
non-negative. It was mapped onto its own dedicated output coordinate and excluded from the fit
so that chromium-free models would be able to use the same transform. The
remaining entries were optimized by gradient descent (Adam; PyTorch) against
approximately $3\times10^{6}$ \textsc{MELTS} spinel compositions drawn from the
training data, minimizing a two-term objective: a mean-squared term driving each
transformed composition's row sum toward unity, and a leaky-ReLU penalty on negative
transformed entries driving them toward zero. The learning rate was annealed by a
factor of ten every 2{,}500 of 10{,}000 steps; the diagonal was reset to unity
whenever it decayed toward zero, preserving invertibility; and coefficients falling
below $0.05$ in magnitude were periodically hard-thresholded to zero to keep the
fitted matrix sparse. The solution satisfyingly converged to one that can be expressed 
in terms of relatively simple fractions.

\subsection{Legality constraint for pyroxene and spinel compositions}
\label{sec:polish}
%=====================================================================================

The transforms derived above in \S\ref{sec:pxsp} guarantee that every legal composition lands in the simplex 
($\hat x \ge 0$, $\sum\hat x = 1$), but not the reverse: the legal compositions fill only a portion
of the simplex, and other regions of the simplex are forbidden because they non-physically map to negative atoms. 
Because the softmax can return any point in the simplex, it is possible 
for the predicted output to be illegal without the addition of further constraints. To address this, 
we apply a post-processing step to the softmax outputs of pyroxene and spinel 
to force them to be legal compositions. This correction is commonly applied to orthopyroxene (6-7\% of predicted OPX), for which
CaO is low generally and is easily pushed negative. For spinel and clinopyroxene, these corrections are more rare, affecting less than 2\% 
of predicted spinel and 0.1\% of predicted clinopyroxene.

\subsubsection{Correcting a single negative oxide}

A single negative oxide can be safely corrected with trivial arithmetic. 

The first step in correcting a single negative oxide is to separate the positive and negative oxide contributions from each component ($S_+$ and $S_-$ respectively). 
For oxide $i$, let $q_i$ be the column of oxide stoichiometry for the transformed components as given in Table~\ref{tab:Kcomp}. 
\begin{equation}
  S^+ = \sum_{q_i > 0} q_i\,\hat x_i, \qquad
  S^- = \sum_{q_i < 0} |q_i|\,\hat x_i, \qquad
  \text{oxide moles} = S^+ - S^-
\end{equation}
The oxide is negative if $S^- > S^+$. We employ a simple fix that aims to preserve as much of the original prediction as possible. 
We find two scaling factors to scale the positive and negative contributing components to balance the oxide. We multiply every component fraction on
the positive side by one factor $a$ and every fraction on the negative side by one factor $b$. If we set the 
oxide to zero and enforce the closure constraint, we formulate a system of two equations that 
fix the two unknowns $a$ and $b$:
\begin{align}
  \text{oxide becomes exactly zero:}\quad & a S^+ = b S^-, \label{eq:zero}\\
  \text{fractions still sum to one:}\quad & a P + b N = 1, \label{eq:close}
\end{align}
where $P$ and $N$ are the plain sums of the component fractions on each side.
\begin{equation}
  P = \sum_{\hat x_i > 0} \hat x_i, \qquad
  N = \sum_{\hat x_i < 0} \hat x_i, \qquad
  P + N = 1
\end{equation}

From Eq.~\eqref{eq:zero}, $b = a S_+/S_-$; substituting into
Eq.~\eqref{eq:close} gives
\begin{equation}
  a = \frac{S^-}{S^- P + S^+ N}, \qquad
  b = \frac{S^+}{S^- P + S^+ N}.
  \label{eq:ab}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Pyroxene: CaO $< 0$}

From Table~\ref{tab:Kcomp},
\begin{equation}
  S^+ = \hat x_C + \hat x_0 + \hat x_1 + \hat x_2 + \hat x_3, \qquad
  S^- = 2\hat x_4 + \hat x_5 .
\end{equation}
All positive coefficients are 1, so $P = S^+$ and $N = \hat x_4 + \hat x_5$, and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ab} simplifies to:
\begin{equation}
  b = \frac{1}{3\hat x_4 + 2\hat x_5}, \qquad
  a = \frac{S^-}{S^+\,(3\hat x_4 + 2\hat x_5)} .
\end{equation}

The CaO correction is applied to clinopyroxene only.

\subsubsection{Pyroxene: MgO $<0$}

\begin{equation}
  S^+ = \hat x_C + \hat x_1 + 3\hat x_5, \quad P = \hat x_C + \hat x_1 + \hat x_5; \qquad
  S^- = 2\hat x_0 + \hat x_2 + \hat x_3 + 4\hat x_4, \quad N = \hat x_0 + \hat x_2 + \hat x_3 + \hat x_4,
\end{equation}
$a$, $b$ then can be calculated using Eq.~\eqref{eq:ab}. The MgO correction
is applied to clinopyroxene only.

\subsubsection{Spinel: FeO $< 0$ and Al$_2$O$_3$ $< 0$}

Spinel has two oxides that can go negative, sometimes simultaneously, substantially complicating the problem. From Table~\ref{tab:Kcomp},
\begin{align}
  \text{FeO} &= \hat x_{\mathsf{Chr}} + \hat x_0 + \hat x_1 + \tfrac94\hat x_3 - 19\,\hat x_2, \label{eq:spFe}\\
  \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 &= \hat x_0 + \hat x_2 - \tfrac23\hat x_1 - \tfrac14\hat x_3 . \label{eq:spAl}
\end{align}
In models without Cr, $\hat x_{\mathsf{Chr}} = 0$.

\paragraph{Three pools.} Because the two constraints share components, the components are
grouped by the signs they carry in the two equations, and each group gets its own factor:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l c c c@{}}
\toprule
Pool & Components & FeO & Al$_2$O$_3$ & Factor \\
\midrule
$A = \hat x_{\mathsf{Chr}} + \hat x_0$ & Chr, $u_0$ & $+$ & $+$ & $a$ \\
$B = \hat x_1 + \hat x_3$         & $u_1$, $u_3$ & $+$ & $-$ & $b$ \\
$C = \hat x_2$                    & $u_2$ & $-$ & $+$ & $c$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
In all cases, the closure constraint is enforced:
\begin{equation}
  aA + bB + cC = 1, \label{eq:spclose}
\end{equation}
If only one oxide is negative, then only the violated oxide is set to zero, 
and the pool it does not involve held fixed.
Then, the second equation for each scenario is:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{FeO only} ($b = 1$): Eq.~\eqref{eq:spclose} and
        $aA + \hat x_1 + \tfrac94\hat x_3 = 19\,cC$;
  \item \emph{Al$_2$O$_3$ only} ($c = 1$): Eq.~\eqref{eq:spclose} and
        $a\hat x_0 + C = b\big(\tfrac23\hat x_1 + \tfrac14\hat x_3\big)$.
\end{itemize}

below zero, all three factors are solved together with three constraints: closure, FeO $= 0$, and
Al$_2$O$_3$ $= 0$,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{pmatrix}
    A & B & C\\
    A & \hat x_1 + \tfrac94\hat x_3 & -19C\\
    \hat x_0 & -\big(\tfrac23\hat x_1 + \tfrac14\hat x_3\big) & C
  \end{pmatrix}
  \begin{pmatrix} a\\ b\\ c \end{pmatrix}
  =
  \begin{pmatrix} 1\\ 0\\ 0 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Conservation of mass}
\label{sec:massbalance}
%=====================================================================================

The assemblage predicted by the emulator is not strictly constrained to reproduce the bulk composition;
it is only encouraged to with a loss term during training (\S\ref{ch4-training}). 
Therefore, a post-processing step is necessary to ensure that the final assemblage is consistent with the requested bulk composition.
\emph{nGibbs} uses a conservation-of-mass correction adapted from \citet{asimow1998}, who find the smallest relative 
change to $\hat m$ that makes $\hat b = b$. The implementation in \emph{nGibbs} differs from their procedure most
strongly in two ways: (1) the minimization is solved in parallel for thousands of assemblages at once on a GPU, and (2)
the correction is done iteratively, with constraints enforced at each step so that no elemental abundance becomes negative. 

The section makes use of variables defined in Table~\ref{tab:key} as well as those unique to this section
in Table~\ref{tab:mbkey}. Index forms are given alongside the matrix forms
throughout, with $c$ running over components and $e, e'$ over elements.

\centering
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{@{}c c >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{11.6cm}@{}}

\midrule

  i.e.\ $D_{ce} = \hat m_c A_{ce}$ \\

The neural network returns the component moles $\hat m$ (the endmembers of every phase, in the
transformed basis of Section~\ref{sec:polish}). The element moles they imply are $\hat m A$
($[1\times\text{C}][\text{C}\times\text{E}] \to [1\times\text{E}]$). The reconstructed bulk is normalized to sum to one, then
its residual is given by
\begin{equation}
  \delta b = b - \hat m A 
  \qquad .
  \label{eq:mbr}
\end{equation}

Conservation of mass seeks a correction $\delta\hat m$ to the component moles such that
\begin{equation}
  \delta\hat m\, A = \delta b,
  \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad
  \sum_c \delta\hat m_c\, A_{ce} = \delta b_e \quad \text{for each element } e .
  \label{eq:mbcond}
\end{equation}

This is E equations (about ten) in C unknowns (several tens), so the system is underconstrained and 
has infinitely many solutions. Because the network's prediction is the best available estimate of the assemblage, it is 
most reasonable to choose the solution that changes it least. We define this as the solution that minimizes the sum of 
squared relative changes to the component moles. For convenience, we define the relative change $\zeta_c$ for each component $c$ as

\begin{equation}
  \zeta_c = \frac{\delta\hat m_c}{\hat m_c},
  \label{eq:zeta}
\end{equation}
We minimize the sum of squared relative changes,
\begin{equation}
  \tfrac12\, \zeta\, \zeta^{\!\top}
  = \tfrac12 \sum_c \left(\frac{\delta\hat m_c}{\hat m_c}\right)^{\!2}.
  \label{eq:mbobj}
\end{equation}
The factor $\tfrac12$ is arbitrarily added to elegantly cancel the 2 produced by the differentiation below. We introduce
a new variable: $D$, the component abundance-weighted stoichiometry matrix, to relate $\zeta$ to the bulk residual $\delta b$.
\begin{equation}
  \zeta\, D = \delta b,
  \qquad
  D = \operatorname{diag}(\hat m)\, A \quad (\text{C}\times\text{E}),
  \label{eq:agweighted}
\end{equation}

We therefore formulate the problem as a minimization of Eq.~\eqref{eq:mbobj} subject to the constraints of Eq.~\eqref{eq:agweighted}. 
In other words: our solution is the smallest relative change to the component moles necessary to satisfy conservation of mass. 

\subsubsection{Solving with Lagrange multipliers}
\label{sec:mb-lagrange}

Lagrange multipliers reformulate this problem into a set of ordinary equations. One new unknown, the
multiplier $\nu_e$, is introduced for each element. Each constraint is written as an expression that must equal zero.
The multipliers $\nu_e$ relate the derivatives of the objective function to the constraints. Requiring every derivative 
of the result to vanish then expresses two conditions at once: the constraints hold, and no change that respects them
can lower the objective $ \tfrac12\, \zeta\, \zeta^{\!\top}$. This is the Lagrangian,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\zeta, \nu)
  = \tfrac12\, \zeta\,\zeta^{\!\top} - \big( \zeta D - \delta b \big)\, \nu^{\!\top},
  \label{eq:lagrangian}
\end{equation}
with $\nu$ the row of E multipliers. Now, differentiating with respect to the
relative correction $\zeta$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \zeta} = \zeta - \nu\, D^{\!\top} = 0
  \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
  \zeta = \nu\, D^{\!\top}
  \quad [1\times\text{E}][\text{E}\times\text{C}] \to [1\times\text{C}].
  \label{eq:ystat}
\end{equation}
This reveals that the C relative corrections are not independent: they are all determined
by just E numbers, the Lagrange multipliers. The problem has been reduced from C unknowns to E,
and now the system is well-constrained.

Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:ystat} into the $\zeta\, D = \delta b$ constraint gives
\begin{equation}
  \nu\, D^{\!\top} D = \delta b,
\end{equation}

and now the only unknowns are the E multipliers $\nu$, which can be solved with an LU (lower-upper) decomposition as 

\begin{equation}
  \nu^{\!\top} = SOLVE(D^{\!\top} D, \delta b^{\!\top}) .
  \label{eq:nu}
\end{equation}

With $\nu$ known, Eq.~\eqref{eq:ystat} relates the relative correction $\zeta$ to the multipliers.

\begin{equation}
  \zeta = \nu A^{\!\top} \operatorname{diag}(\hat m)
\end{equation}

The absolute correction $\delta\hat m$ is then obtained by multiplying the relative correction by the component moles (Eq.~\eqref{eq:zeta}).

\begin{equation}
  \delta\hat m = \zeta \operatorname{diag}(\hat m) = \nu A^{\!\top} \operatorname{diag}(\hat m)^2
  \quad [1\times\text{E}][\text{E}\times\text{C}][\text{C}\times\text{C}] \to [1\times\text{C}],
  \label{eq:share}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Why the Lagrange form is used.} \citet{asimow1998} obtain the same solution by
SVD; however, their problem is distinct from this one in that they correct one assemblage at a
time, whereas \emph{nGibbs} applies this operation to tens of thousands of assemblages in parallel. 
The Lagrange form is used here because batched SVD handles these matrices poorly on a GPU.
In an informal test on 15{,}000 assemblages from the \textsc{HeFESTo} Earth validation dataset 
(B = 15{,}000, E = 8, C = 68), \code{torch.linalg.pinv} took 10.1\,s on the GPU (implying that the calculation was run 
sequentially, perhaps because the matrices were too large) against 0.29\,s on the CPU (single precision), whereas the Lagrange solve took 25\,ms 
on the GPU (double precision; 48\,ms on the CPU). The Lagrange form, however, requires double precision floating point arithmetic to recover an accurate solution,
whereas the SVD is robust to single precision arithmetic because the $\nu\left( D^{\!\top} D \right)$ solve of the Lagrange formulation
squares the condition number. This cost is marginal, however, beside the speedup of the Lagrange form on both CPU and GPU, and it elegantly
avoids the need to bus data between CPU and GPU when \emph{nGibbs} is run on a GPU.

\subsubsection{Regularization}
\label{sec:mb-reg}

In practice, a small number $\kappa$ is added to every diagonal entry
of $\left( D^{\!\top} D \right)$,
\begin{equation}
  \nu\,\big( D^{\!\top} D  + \kappa I\big) = \delta b,
  \qquad \kappa = 10^{-12},
  \label{eq:regsolve}
\end{equation}
where $I$ is the E$\times$E identity matrix. This is because $ D^{\!\top} D $ has all-zero columns and rows for 
any bulk composition lacking any particular element and is therefore singular. For a row lacking an element, 
its equation becomes $\kappa\,\nu_e = 0$ for the regularized case and $\nu$ is zero because $\delta b_e = 0$ for an absent element. 
Without regularization, the equation for this row would be $0 \nu_e = 0$, for which $\nu_e$ is undefined. 

On the \textsc{HeFESTo} Earth Model validation dataset, the smallest non-zero diagonal entries of $ D^{\!\top} D $ are $ \thicksim 3\times10^{-7}$ 
(median over rows; Fig.~\ref{fig:mb-dtd-diag}), so $\kappa$ must be far smaller than that. A $\kappa$ as small as 10\textsuperscript{-12} requires
double precision floating point values, but this is already required by the solve of the Lagrange multipliers and 
therefore poses no additional complication.

\subsubsection{Seeding phases for unexplained elements}
\label{sec:seed}

The conservation of mass correction can only move an element through components
that contain it and are present ($\hat m_c > 0$). If the predicted assemblage contains no
carrier of an element that is in the bulk, that element's residual cannot be
removed: the regularized solve leaves it in place (Section~\ref{sec:mb-reg}).

Before the mass conservation correction is applied, \emph{nGibbs} first identifies unexplained elements. 
Element $e$ is unexplained if $b_e > 0$ and no component with $\hat m_c > 0$ contains it. For each
of these unexplained elements, the phase that is predicted by \emph{nGibbs} to be closest to saturation
out of the set of phases that contain the unexplained element is forced to saturation. Its abundance, naturally,
is set to the amount that exactly supplies the unexplained element. We first calculate $a_e$, the moles of element $e$ 
in one mole of a given phase $p$. This is 
        \begin{equation}
          a_e = M_{pc}\,diag(\hat x V + f)_{cc}\, A_{ce} 
          \quad [\text{P}\times\text{C}][\text{C}\times\text{C}][\text{C}\times\text{E}] \to [\text{P}\times\text{E}]
          \label{eq:seedcomp}
        \end{equation}
The phase is added with the abundance $\hat n_p$ that supplies the missing element in its proportion. 
For unexplained element $\mathcal{U}$, 
        \begin{equation}
          \hat n_p = a_e\,  b_e \quad \text{for}\, e\, \in \mathcal{U} 
          \label{eq:seed}
        \end{equation}
        
The phase's components get $\hat m_c = \hat n_p\,(\hat x V + f)_c$ for every $c$ in $M_{pc} = 1$ for 
phase $\mathcal{U}$.

The sequence loops until all elements for each row are explained. $\hat m$ is then renormalized so that
$\sum_c \hat m_c = 1$. Rows that need no seeding are unaffected.

\subsubsection{The iterative correction}
\label{sec:mb-iter}

The mass conservation correction of Section~\ref{sec:mb-lagrange} 
places no bound on $\delta\hat m_c$, so $\delta\hat m_c$ is able to force $\hat m$ to become negative. 
A negative $\hat m_c$ is impossible; it means that a phase would have negative atoms, which is non-physical. 
Generally, this is a rare occurrence because the high magnitude relative change $d \hat m_c /\hat m_c < -1$ is highly disfavored 
by the objective function of \S\ref{sec:mb-lagrange} and therefore is indicative of a poor prediction. This is the ``overstepping'' failure \citet{asimow1998} note. 
Setting such a component to zero restores a physically sensible assemblage but
breaks the conservation of mass again, because the moles it would have supplied are now missing.
The step is therefore repeated (three times by default) in order to spread the remaining residual over the
components that are still present. For each repetition, $\hat m$ is updated as:
$\hat m \leftarrow \max\big(\hat m + \zeta \operatorname{diag}(\hat m),\ 0\big)$

\textsc{MELTS} emulators in \emph{nGibbs} have another complexity for pyroxenes and spinel: the physically allowed 
range of components is only a region within the broader accessible range of the transformed component predictions, unlike
all other phases. Thus, \emph{nGibbs} additionally pushes these components to lie within their legal simplexes after each iteration following the
method described in \S\ref{sec:polish}.

mass-conserving assemblage with the requested bulk composition for the \textsc{HeFESTo} Earth isentropic emulator.

\subsection{Data generation}
\label{sec:datagen}

\subsubsection{Bulk composition sampling}

Bulk compositions are sampled from whole-rock analyses in the \href{https://georoc.eu/georoc/new-start.asp}{GEOROC} database in 2022 and the EarthChem 
\href{https://earthchem.org/synthesis?mapViewCenter=0,0}{PetDB} database in April 2025. Only samples with total weight percent within 2\% of 100 were kept, then 
all samples were renormalized to a total = 100\%. Evolved compositions are more common in the GEOROC database in particular, so evolved compositions were downsampled 
to be symmetric with respect to mafic and ultramafic compositions. The prefiltered dataset included all GEOROC entries, whereas only mafic and ultramafic compositions were pulled from PetDB. 
A document containing a supersample of citations for the studies that measured these compositions can be found on the nGibbs \href{https://github.com/benjthyer/nGibbs}{GitHub} page. 

For the \textsc{HeFESTo} models, one fifth of the training data are drawn from the population of compositions between 5 and 20 wt\% MgO, three tenths of the data are drawn from the population 
of compositions with greater than 20 wt\% MgO, and the remaining half is a mixture of these two mafic and ultramafic populations in random proportions. 
Roughly 80\% of these base compositions were substantially randomly altered so that \emph{nGibbs} would perform well in a wider variety of parameter spaces, 
including rock compositions that are uncommon or unknown on Earth's surface. For the Earth model, one quarter of the compositions are not perturbed. 
For the \textsc{MELTS} emulators, half of the training data is generated from any composition in the compiled database, one fifth of the data are pulled from 
ultramafic compositions with greater than 25 wt\% MgO, and three tenths of the data are pulled from compositions with greater than 5  wt\% MgO. 
These bulk compositions are perturbed by Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of 5\%, a substantially more modest perturbation than that applied to 
the \textsc{HeFESTo} emulator training data. For the \textsc{MELTS} 1.0.2 emulators, half of the isobaric cooling runs are performed with fractional crystallization 
and half are performed with batch crystallization, whereas only half of the igneous compositions are performed with fractional crystallization for the \textsc{MELTS} 
1.2.0 emulators.

For volatile-focused \textsc{MELTS} 1.2.0 emulators in particular, 35\% of the training and validation datasets are drawn from batch crystallization of sedimentary compositions 
within the Gard database \citep{gard2019}. Of these sedimentary bulk compositions, about one quarter are drawn from carbonate rich compositions, 
with CO\textsubscript{2} contents greater than 10 wt\%, whereas the remaining sedimentary compositions contain less than 10 wt\% CO\textsubscript{2}. 
The remaining 65\% of the training data were drawn from igneous whole rock data from GEOROC and PetDB, similarly to the other models. 

In order to train emulators that perform well in a variety of redox conditions, 
each sampled composition is randomly assigned an oxygen fugacity from -5 to +5 log$_{10}\Delta$FMQ for \textsc{MELTS} training data, or -7 to +3 log$_{10}\Delta$FMQ for \textsc{HeFESTo} training data. Open 
system models simply carry this oxygen fugacity as a feature, whereas closed models translate this oxygen fugacity into a molar \textsuperscript{+3}Fe/\textsuperscript{+2}Fe ratio using an empirical parameterization. This is not meant to be explicitly correct; it is meant to generate training data with an appropriate distribution of molar \textsuperscript{+3}Fe/\textsuperscript{+2}Fe ratios.

\begin{equation}
^{+3}Fe/^{+2}Fe \approx 10^{\left(0.2 \times \text{log}_{10}\Delta\text{FMQ} \right) - 1}
\label{eq:fO2ratio}
\end{equation}

Manganese and nickel are excluded from the compositional basis of all released
models. Manganese and nickel are not handled by \textsc{HeFESTo}, and we have 
observed that \textsc{MELTS} solutions that carry MnO and NiO frequently stabilize
phases with compositions rarely observed in nature, and there is little value in
emulating the regions of a model that are not themselves trusted. Excluding these
oxides also removes two dimensions from the component basis at small cost to
the applications the emulators target.

\subsubsection{State-variable sampling}\label{state-var-sampling}

For the \textsc{MELTS} 1.0.2 and 1.2.0 emulator families, each sampled composition is isobarically cooled from 1800\textdegree C in 1\textdegree C increments
at a random pressure between 1 and 12000 bars. These computed assemblages have well-defined entropies and therefore are acceptable for use in training isentropic models. 

\textsc{HeFESTo} Earth and Mars models are each trained around a core of (approximately) adiabatic simulations at reasonable potential temperatures 
(1200--1650\textdegree C for Earth; 500--1750\textdegree C for Mars). These adiabats are supplemented with cooler conditions due to a conductive lid regime near the surface, 
which is assumed to be between 0-250 km thick for Earth and 100-700 km thick for Mars. % \todo{Potential temperature citations?}. 
Similarly, warmer conditions are modeled at the approach to the core-mantle boundary (CMB) for the Earth model, which assume a mantle temperature between 
3200--4700\textdegree C at the CMB and a 100--300~km wide conductive layer.

Although \textsc{HeFESTo} natively calculates isentropes, such a calculation requires an iteration of T at every step to retrieve the imposed entropy, which roughly doubles the computation time. Therefore, adiabatic PT paths were instead approximated to reduce computational expense in the generation of the training dataset. To accomplish this, a second-order linear regression of temperature as a function of specific entropy and pressure was fit to a dataset of dozens of \textsc{HeFESTo} isentropes at representative compositions and potential temperatures. A similar linear regression was fit for specific entropy as a function of temperature at P = 0 GPa on the same dataset to translate potential temperatures to specific entropy. Similarly to the iron speciation parameterization described above, this approach is not intended to be explicitly correct; it is intended to control the distribution of the training dataset in pressure-temperature-entropy space without unnecessary computational expense. These regressions are used to impose a roughly adiabatic pressure-temperature path for training data. These paths are perturbed with small random noise to prevent the emulators from learning relationships between composition and entropy. 

\subsubsection{Execution}

\textsc{MELTS} simulations are orchestrated with Python scripts preparing, running, and parsing MELTS simulations using \textsc{alphaMELTS} versions
2.3.1 and 2.3.2 \citep{smithasimow2005} run with GNU parallel \citep{tange2021}. Batch crystallization was run with \textsc{alphaMELTS} 2.3.1 and fractional crystallization 
was run with \textsc{alphaMELTS} 2.3.2. In future work, we will rebuild the MELTS datasets to consistently use \textsc{alphaMELTS} 2.3.2, but the results
are unlikely to significantly differ. Separate datasets are generated for the closed-system emulators, and for the open-system \textsc{MELTS} 1.2.0 emulator. 
All MELTS calculations were carried out on a 20-core Intel Core i7-14700H CPU @ 3.40GHz with 32 GB of RAM. Additionally, each simulation was run with parameters
adjusted to prohibit multiple crystallization of any phase, including clinopyroxenes and spinel, for which different endmembers are well-known to coexist in nature
\citep{lindsley1983,stosch1980}. It is important to note in this regard that orthopyroxene and clinopyroxene, as well as alkali-feldspar and plagioclase, are treated as separate phases in 
\textsc{MELTS} and therefore can coexist in nGibbs as well. Future work will explore the division of clinopyroxenes into distinct low-Ca and high-Ca phases 
in \textsc{nGibbs}. Additionally, some phases that are modeled by \textsc{MELTS} were deliberately suppressed: sphene, melilite, aegirine, aenigmatite, cummingtonite, 
orthoamphibole, cristobalite, kalsilite, corundum, rutile, perovskite, ortho-oxide and sillimanite. Both of these deliberate omissions were made for simplicity 
and to avoid learning \textsc{MELTS} idiosyncrasies for phases that are not well-modeled. 

Some \textsc{HeFESTo} simulations were run on the same machine as the MELTS runs, but the majority were run on the Caltech High Performance Computing cluster 
as SLURM array jobs.

After pressure transects are calculated with \textsc{HeFESTo}, phase changes are identified and iteratively resampled to 
improve the model resolution around phase boundaries. A random subset of these phase changes is chosen (up to 3x the
original simulation count), and the HeFESTo states
bounding the phase transition are used to build a P-T grid sampling with higher resolution. 
The pressure interval is divided into five steps, which is evaluated at at least one isotherm, but more isotherms are added 
for every full 25 K between the bounding conditions of the phase. Along an isentrope both P and T change between the bracketing states, so this first
resampling step produces a two-dimensional PT search of the region in which the boundary must lie. Each of these isotherms again bounds the phase boundary, 
but now at a fixed temperature. A second iteration of resampling is performed at 0.01 GPa resolution, between the states that
bound the phase boundary. Again, as many as 3x the original number of simulations are called in this step.

A fraction of simulations fail to converge or terminate without usable output. These
are detected and discarded at import. Attrition differs sharply between codes and
compositional domains: approximately $0.1\%$ for
\textsc{HeFESTo}, approximately $15\%$ for \textsc{MELTS} on igneous bulk
compositions, and approximately $40\%$ for \textsc{MELTS} 1.2 on sedimentary bulk
compositions.

\subsection{Data preparation}
\label{sec:prep}

Four operations are applied to prepare \textsc{MELTS} and \textsc{HeFESTo} outputs for training.

\subsubsection{Consistency filtering} Rows are removed where the reference code's output
is internally inconsistent, e.g. where the composition reconstructed from the
reported assemblage disagrees with the stated bulk composition beyond tolerance. 
%% TODO? Add table with filter loss? "The fractions actually removed are reported in Table~\ref{tab:filters}."

\subsubsection{Compositional filtering} A series of filters is applied to remove rows containing phases with unusual compositions generally not seen in nature. These
compositions are considered to be MELTS idiosyncrasies that needn't be passed on to nGibbs. 
These filters include:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item K-feldspar: at least 6~wt\% $\mathrm{K_2O}$ and at most 4~wt\% $\mathrm{CaO}$.
  \item Clinopyroxene: at least 6~wt\% $\mathrm{CaO}$, at most 9~wt\% $\mathrm{TiO_2}$,
    and at most 12~wt\% $\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$.
  \item Orthopyroxene: at most 5~wt\% $\mathrm{TiO_2}$, 10~wt\% $\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$,
    and 6~wt\% $\mathrm{CaO}$.
  \item Silicate melt: at least 30~wt\% $\mathrm{SiO_2}$, at most 13~wt\% $\mathrm{TiO_2}$,
    and at most 10~wt\% $\mathrm{P_2O_5}$.
  \item Plagioclase: the sanidine component may not exceed 0.2 (mole fraction).
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Rare phase removal} Phases occurring in less than 1 assemblage per thousand are considered to be too rare in the training set
to be effectively learned and are removed from the component basis entirely to simplify the model.
This operates on entire rows; assemblages with rare phases are discarded
for containing a rare phase at any low modal proportion. It is presumed that this operation is minimally damaging, because very few assemblages are affected by design. 

\subsubsection{Shuffling} The training and validation datasets are shuffled to remove any correlation between the order of the rows and the order in which they were generated.
Conventional dataloaders in PyTorch can automatically shuffle data during training, but this option is not available for \emph{nGibbs}, because
its datasets are too large to fit in memory and instead were streamed in static batches of 1,000,000 rows each from disk (SK hynix Platinum P41, 2 TB). 
The order in which these batches are streamed is shuffled every epoch, but the contents of each bundle are fixed.

\subsubsection{Partitioning}
\label{sec:split}

To monitor model overfitting during training, a validation dataset distinct from the training set is used. Training and validation datasets are generated with the same pipelines, 
but are separated \emph{by unique bulk composition}. Bulk compositions were partitioned between training and validation datasets and are treated independently as 
separate datasets throughout the processing pipeline. This ensures that the validation dataset tracks the same fundamental structure as the training dataset while 
avoiding data leakage. Successive states along one isobaric or isentropic path are strongly autocorrelated, so a row-wise random
split would place very similar data in both the training and validation datasets. The resulting error would measure interpolation between adjacent states of a rock the
model had already seen, rather than generalization to a rock it had not, and would be
optimistic by a wide and unquantifiable margin. Splitting by composition, before the reference thermodynamic
models were applied, makes the validation dataset's error track overfitting to the training dataset more faithfully. 
This overfitting metric is instrumental to develop an emulator that can be effectively applied to new data. 

Compositions were assigned in the ratio 80:20 between training and validation. 
The validation partition is used every epoch for model selection, to tune adaptive regularization, and for early stopping. 
Unless otherwise stated, all performance metrics presented here in Section~\ref{ch4-results} are for the validation datasets; the emulator's performance on the training datasets is not reported.

\subsection{Training}\label{ch4-training}

\subsubsection{The objective}
%=====================================================================================

For each batch the trainer minimizes
\begin{equation}
  L = \lambda_s L_s + \lambda_x L_x + \lambda_n L_n + \lambda_b L_b
\label{eq:total}
\end{equation}
covering phase presence ($L_s$), chemistry ($L_x$), phase abundance ($L_n$) and bulk
($L_b$). There is no phase presence term in the affinity-aware model (\S\ref{sec:affinity}). 
The weighting factors $\lambda$ are tuned to balance the contributions of each term to the total loss, 
preferentially weighting the chemistry and abundance terms. When training some models, the loss from present 
examples of important rare phases was additionally up-weighted by a per-phase factor to encourage the model
to learn phases that otherwise would contribute little to the total loss.

Chemistry and bulk losses use the symmetric relative squared error:
\begin{equation}
  \rho(\hat y, y) = \frac{(\hat y - y)^2}{|\hat y| + |y|},
  \label{eq:rho}
\end{equation}
with the denominator floored at $10^{-6}$. 
The abundance loss uses the same function in the affinity-naive model, 
but plain mean squared error (MSE: $\rho(\hat y, y) = (\hat y - y)^2$) is used in the affinity-aware model (\S\ref{sec:affinity}).
The symmetric relative squared error proved effective in training the model to predict both small and large values accurately, 
for positive targets that span orders of magnitude. MSE is used for the affinity-aware model abundance heads because the network maps
to negative labels (affinities) as well as positive labels which behave continuously across zero: a property that 
MSE preserves but the symmetric relative squared error does not.

\paragraph{Chemistry loss.} The chemistry loss $L_x$ is only evaluated for phases that are present in the ground truth: \emph{nGibbs}
is not trained to predict metastable phase chemistry. The loss is zeroed for phases without ground truth compositions, 
but not for phases that are present in the ground truth and not the predicted assemblage: this loss is evaluated 
everywhere a ground truth composition is available. Because this masking behavior strongly changes the absolute value of 
the loss as a function of the number of present phases, the loss is normalized by the number of present phases in each batch.

\paragraph{Bulk loss.}
The reconstructed bulk (Eq.~\ref{eq:bhat}) is compared with the input bulk over the elements present in it:
\begin{equation}
  L_b = \sum_{e:\, b_e > 0} \rho(\hat b_e, b_e),
\end{equation}
This measures how far the raw predictions are from satisfying conservation of mass, which reduces
the correction that has to be applied at inference (\S\ref{sec:massbalance}). The backpropagation graph for this loss is complex
and covers the entire network, thus it can cause exploding gradients when applied to untrained networks. Here it is only applied late in training 
to further improve the model's performance after it already produces strong predictions.

\paragraph{Abundance loss.} For the abundance loss $L_n$, every phase is supervised, including absent ones. \emph{nGibbs} emulators can be trained 
to predict chemical affinities for absent phases (affinity-aware) or it can be trained to predict phase abundances and presence 
without this information (affinity-na\"{i}ve). In both cases, they produce an unbounded, unactivated output $\hat g$, that corresponds to a phase abundance when present and 
an effective chemical affinity when negative, tracking how close the phase is to saturation. The affinity-aware models learn this behavior with direct 
supervision: they are trained with a scaled affinity value, whereas the affinity-naive models are not provided with any negative labels, and instead are 
trained to treat this negative space as a confidence in the prediction of phase absence, rewarding confidently correct predictions of phase absence, 
and likewise penalizing confidently incorrect predictions of phase absence.

\paragraph{Affinity-na\"{i}ve abundance loss.} The labels for the affinity-na\"{i}ve model are the literal phase abundances ($n$), which are zero for absent phases, 
never negative. Therefore, the loss is not computed on the predicted abundance $\hat n = \max(g, 0)$ itself but on a smoothed version of it ($\tilde n$), which is $\hat g$ 
activated with a softplus function (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch4-softplus}).

\begin{equation}
  \tilde n = \Upsilon \operatorname{softplus}(g/\Upsilon) = \Upsilon \ln\!\big(1 + e^{g/\Upsilon}\big)
  \label{eq:smooth}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{fig_thermal_softplus.pdf}
  \caption{The softplus function (Eq.~\ref{eq:smooth}) generalized as a function of $g/\Upsilon_0$ for different values of the scheduled annealing parameter $a$. 
  The softplus function is a smooth approximation of the ReLU function, which is used to activate the predicted phase abundances $\hat g$ in the affinity-na\"{i}ve model. As training
  proceeds, the softplus function collapses to the ReLU function, while allowing the model to learn a smooth transition from zero to positive values in the earlier stages of training.
  The smoothing scale $\Upsilon$ controls the strength of the smoothing, with larger values producing a smoother transition from zero to positive values. The lower plot is $\tanh(g/\Upsilon)$, 
  which is the weight of the BCE loss on binary phase presence/absence. This binary loss is similarly scaled by the smoothing scale $\Upsilon$ so that the loss is weak near saturation to avoid 
  teaching the model discontinuous behavior across 0.}
  \label{fig:ch4-softplus}
\end{figure}

The softplus function is continuous for all real inputs and only approaches zero asymptotically. It is effectively a smoothed version of the ReLU function, 
where gradients are alive throughout and the transition from positive to zero values is smooth instead of kinked, following the thermodynamic logic that a phase generally 
grows from zero abundance at saturation and gradually decays to zero as it becomes undersaturated.
The utility of this function applied to this problem is clear given its limits:

\begin{equation}
  \lim_{g \to -\infty} \tilde n = 0,
  \qquad
  \tilde n \approx g \text{  for  } g/\Upsilon > 3 .
\end{equation}

The cost of the softplus activation in this context is that the smoothed $\hat n$ systematically overestimates the true abundance of phases near zero, and therefore the strength 
of the smoothing needs to be reasonably restricted for the model to learn phase abundances well. The input to the softplus is thus scaled by a smoothing scale $\Upsilon = a\Upsilon_0$, 
where $\Upsilon_0$ is the 5th percentile of that phase's abundance where the phase is present and a is a scaling factor within [0,1]. This limits the range of values for which this 
smoothing error is high to only the smallest abundances that are approaching desaturation. The smoothing is further weakened as training progresses by annealing the $a$ parameter from 1 to $\approx 0$, 
and the softplus function anneals into the ReLU function ($\tilde n \approx \max(g,0)$), matching \emph{nGibbs}'s behavior during inference. 

\paragraph{Presence loss $L_s$ for the affinity-na\"{i}ve model.} As a means to further incentivize the model to learn the correct assemblages, the affinity $g$ is also 
used as the logit of a binary cross-entropy against $s$:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{gathered}
    L_s = \frac{\sum_j c_j \Big[ -s_j \ln\operatorname{sigmoid}\!\big(5g_j/\Upsilon_{0,j}\big)
          - (1-s_j)\ln\Big(1-\operatorname{sigmoid}\!\big(5g_j/\Upsilon_{0,j}\big)\Big) \Big]}{\sum_j c_j},
    \\[4pt]
    c_j = \big|\tanh(g_j/\Upsilon)\big|,
  \end{gathered}
  \label{eq:sat}
\end{equation}
where the bracketed term is the binary cross-entropy (BCE) of the logit $5g_j/\Upsilon_{0,j}$ against binary label $s_j$.
The logit is scaled by the fixed $\Upsilon_0$, not the annealed $\Upsilon$, so the classifier's confidence means the same thing throughout training: it reaches
$\operatorname{sigmoid}(5) \approx 0.99$ at $g = \Upsilon_0$.
The weight for this binary loss $|\tanh(g/\Upsilon)|$ uses the annealed $\Upsilon$. It is 0 at the boundary and
close to 1 a few $\Upsilon$ away. The gradient is carried by the abundance loss near the boundary, where an almost-saturated phase
is thermodynamically similar to a just-saturated phase, a subtlety that the BCE loss cannot capture.
The weight $c$ depends on $g$, a model output. Thus, we normalize the BCE loss by the sum of the weights $\sum_j^P c_j$ so that the model 
is not encouraged to make near-zero predictions for $g$ simply to minimize the BCE loss.

\subsubsection{Affinity-aware abundance loss}\label{sec:affinity}
The affinity-aware model is trained to predict an activated abundance $y$, a transformed version of the signed abundance $g$ that remaps negative values 
but preserves positive values. Unlike an affinity-na\"{i}ve model, there exists a ground truth $g$ that is used to supervise the affinity-aware model even
for phases that are absent.   

\paragraph{Estimating affinities ($g$) from $dn/dT$ of isobaric transects.} Affinities are positive for absent phases and 0 for present phases. They are expressed in units of energy, not molar abundances. This unit mismatch produces an undesirable discontinuity at zero if unaccounted for, reducing the information
content of the affinities within the context of the molar abundances that we seek to predict well. If the affinities are scaled well, they smoothly transition into molar abundances at saturation, 
reinforcing physical $dn/dt$ values even near zero, where other approaches might learn spurious patterns near the boundary in response to the objective function changing 
character across the boundary. This smoothness constraint is simply that $g$ be continuous at exactly 0, which can be written:
\begin{equation}
  \lim_{g \to 0^-} \left(\frac{dg}{dT}\right)_P = \lim_{g \to 0^+} \left(\frac{dg}{dT}\right)_P = \lim_{n \to 0^+} \left(\frac{dn}{dT}\right)_P.
  \label{eq:affinity_continuity}
\end{equation}

Affinities are therefore rescaled into a ``virtual abundance'' unit with a constant unique to each phase and each transect. For an affinity $\alpha$ in an arbitrary unit:

\begin{equation}
  \begin{cases}
    g = n, & n > 0 \\
    g =  \alpha \frac{lim_{n \to 0^+} (dn/dT)_P}{lim_{\alpha \to 0^+} (d\alpha/dT)_P}, & n = 0 .
  \end{cases}
\label{eq:affinity_rescaling}
\end{equation}

$(dn/dT)_P$ and $(d\alpha/dT)_P$ always have opposite signs, so $g \leq 0$ while $n=0$ because the rescaling factor is always negative. 
This operation erases the memory of the units of the affinity and instead privileges the slope of the molar abundance ($dn/dT$) at saturation. 
Indeed, the affinity is locally linear near zero, as is the molar abundance. The rescaled affinity is therefore well-approximated by a linear 
extrapolation of the abundance into negative values. 
Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-affinity-extrapolation} compares labels built both ways along a \textsc{HeFESTo} transect.

\begin{equation}
\alpha(T) \frac{lim_{n \to 0^+} (dn/dT)_P}{lim_{\alpha \to 0^+} (d\alpha/dT)_P} \approx  \left(lim_{n \to 0^+} (dn/dT)_P\right) \times (T - T_{sat}) 
\end{equation}

With this in mind, there is little benefit to collecting real affinity values from the reference thermodynamic models. We evaluate the ground truth $g$ for 
each phase as: 

\begin{equation}
  \begin{cases}
    g = n, & n > 0 \\
    g = \left(lim_{n \to 0^+} (dn/dT)_P\right) \times (T - T_{sat})  & n = 0 .
  \end{cases}
\label{eq:affinity_extrapolation}
\end{equation}

The MELTS training data were generated as isobaric cooling runs, either as batch crystallization (constant bulk composition) or
fractional crystallization (solids are removed from the system at every step). Each run is split into
temperature transects, and the slope $dn/dT$ is estimated at n=0 with differentials of $n$ and $T$. Let $n_i$ refer to a phase's molar
abundance just after saturation, i.e. the first nonzero value of $n$ going either up or down in $T$. Then, $n_{i-1} = 0$, and 


\begin{align}
  \text{batch:}\quad & \left(lim_{n \to 0^+} (dn/dT)_P\right) \approx \frac{n_{i+1}-n_i}{T_{i+1} - T_i}, \label{eq:rbatch}\\
  \text{FxTal:}\quad & \left(lim_{n \to 0^+} (dn/dT)_P\right) \approx \frac{n_i}{T_{i} - T_{i-1}}, \label{eq:rfx} .
\end{align}

It is incorrect to use this approach to estimate the affinities for fractional crystallization \emph{sensu stricto}, because the composition (and therefore $(dn/dT)_P$) is not constant as $T$ decreases. 
However, we found that this approximation is good for small $|T-T_{\text{sat}}|$, and the accuracy of the affinity is less important further from the saturation boundary. Therefore, we
accept this rough approximation so that we are able to weight the training datasets towards liquidus assemblages, which are often of interest to the community. 

Far from the saturation boundary, the first-order approximation of $g$ is less accurate, and accurate predictions of $g$ for absent phases do little to improve the quality of
the predicted assemblage. Therefore, we activate the raw affinity $g$ with a hyperbolic tangent function to compress the negative range of $g$ to limit the influence of strongly undersaturated phases 
on the abundance loss. Because phases have very different abundances in common rocks (e.g. $n_{\text{clinopyroxene}} \gg n_{\text{apatite}}$), we need to define
a scale factor $c$ for each phase which corresponds to some characteristic abundance for a phase. We choose to define $c$ as the typical (median) abundance that a
phase accumulates over a temperature interval $\Delta = 25~\text{K}$ after saturation:

\begin{equation}
  c = \operatorname{median}(| \left(lim_{n \to 0^+} (dn/dT)_P\right) \times \Delta |) , \qquad \Delta = 25~\text{K}.
  \label{eq:c}
\end{equation}

Intuitively, the temperature window $\Delta$ represents the window of temperature approaching saturation for which we would like to accurately predict affinities. 
Phases beyond $\Delta$ \textdegree C from saturation are considered arbitrarily far and so we place them on the plateau of the $\tanh$ function. Any confident prediction that
such a phase is absent will contribute little to the loss. We use the median rather than the mean slope ($\left(lim_{n \to 0^+} (dn/dT)_P\right)$) because it is 
log-normally distributed. 

We define an activated label ($y(g,c)$) that is used during training:

\begin{equation}
  y(g) = \begin{cases} g, & g \ge 0,\\ c\tanh(g/c) + \varepsilon g, & g < 0, \end{cases}
  \qquad \varepsilon = 0.02
  \label{eq:y}
\end{equation}

where epsilon is a small proportionality constant used to invigorate the loss gradient when the predicted $\hat g/c$ is lower than -3, for which the $\tanh$ term is nearly flat ($\tanh(-3)\approx-1$).
This allows the network to learn well at the beginning of training, when predicted $|\hat g|$ can be large, inaccurate, and unstable. Additionally, ground truth $g$ is floored at $-3c$ to 
prevent systematic predictions of very low $g$ values where the gradient is weak (but the network has no such constraint, which would kill the gradient below -3c). Note that $g$, $y$, and $n$ are identical for $n \ge 0$.

\paragraph{The affinity-aware objective.} Within a single isobaric transect, most phases never appear anywhere. These phases in these rows do not permit an estimate of $dn/dT$ at saturation, and therefore
$g$ and $y$ are undefined for them. We refer to these phases as ``untrusted'' ($y \notin \mathbb{R}$), and apply a distinct, weaker loss to them. 
We also reject low-side outlier estimates of $dn/dT$ at saturation (defined as when $dn/dT$ is a factor of $\sqrt{10}$ smaller than the median $dn/dT$ for that phase across all transects). 
These values are also untrusted. For present phases, $y \in \mathbb{R}$ and is positive. Unlike the affinity-na\"{i}ve abundance objective, 
the affinity-aware objective wraps terms for both regression error and binary phase presence/absence into a single loss function with its own weighting scheme.

\begin{equation}
  \ell = \begin{cases}
    \frac{(\hat y - y)}{c}^2 + s*[w_p * \operatorname{ReLU}\!\big(\min(y/c,\ h_p) - \hat y/c\big)^2], & y \in \mathbb{R} ,\\[2pt]
    w_n\max(0,\ \hat y/c - h_n)^2 + w_\eta \,(\hat y/c + 1)^2, & y \notin \mathbb{R} .,
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:affloss}
\end{equation}

For trusted phases ($y \in \mathbb{R}$), the loss function is a combination of the MSE and a corrective positive hinge loss. The first term is the MSE of the predicted activated abundance $\hat y$ against the ground truth $y$, normalized by the scale factor $c$. 
The corrective positive hinge loss penalizes the underprediction of phase abundance for low-abundance phases. This term is a response to the observation that
the loss for untrusted phases was leading to underprediction of present phases near saturation, which most obviously manifests in an imbalanced high precision and low recall. 
A hinge loss uses $\min$ or $\max$ functions to apply a loss only to one side of a threshold. This hinge loss is applied only to phases which are present in the ground truth ($s=1$) 
with abundances less than the threshold $h_p = 0.03$, that are underpredicted by $\hat y/c$. 

For untrusted phases ($y \notin \mathbb{R}$), the loss function is a combination of a negative hinge loss and a weak pull towards $-c$. The hinge loss is stronger, but is only
applied to predictions that are above the threshold $h_n = -0.2$. The combination of these losses is a compromise that 
recognizes that untrusted phases are absent and probably would have affinities mapping to decisive undersaturation at $y\approx-c$, however, because this is not known explicitly,
only unlikely near-saturation predictions ($\hat y$) are substantially penalized.

\subsubsection{Training schedule}\label{ch4-training-schedule}
%=====================================================================================

All models were trained on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5070 Ti GPU with 16 GB of VRAM. Datasets were often too large to fit in memory, and so were streamed from disk in batches of n=$10^6$.


Quality prediction of equilibrium phase assemblages that conserve mass is a complex task with highly covariate errors. These models
are thus trained in episodes that first isolate the distinct abundance and chemistry partitions of the model, training with a high ($10^{-3}$) maximum learning rate 
on a single-cycle learning rate scheduler described by \citet{smith2019}. Then, the model is trained as an interdependent whole with a more cautious maximum learning rate 
($10^{-4}$) that is periodically annealed to zero then restored following a cosine function. This is effectively a more gentle implementation of the warm restart method described by
\citet{loshchilov2017}. Initializing the model in this sequential manner ensures that the model does not learn a single task at the expense of 
the others due to unstable solutions, but optimizes the full unified problem.
The model's encoder feeds into both the molar and chemical heads. The encoder must be instantiated in tandem with either the molar or chemical heads, so that it
has an objective to optimize. We choose to instantiate the encoder with the molar heads because they perform the more complex task. Thus, the chemistry heads 
are frozen and the chemistry loss is zeroed during the first stage of training. During the second stage, the chemistry heads are trained with the chemistry loss,
the abundance loss (and phase presence loss for the affinity-na\"{i}ve model) is zeroed. The weights of both the encoder and molar heads are frozen for this second stage.
The encoder feeds into the chemistry heads, but it cannot be optimized for the chemistry loss while the mole heads are frozen, or the abundance predictions will be clobbered
as the encoder evolves in isolation from the mole heads that depend on it. It is presumed that an encoded representation of the system state that is useful for 
predicting phase abundances would also be useful for predicting phase chemistry. Thus, after the second training episode completes, the whole model produces reasonably good
abundance and chemistry predictions that both are built upon the same encoder. 

Finally, for the third stage, all heads are unfrozen and the model is trained jointly with all losses except the bulk loss. For these two stages, an early stopping patience is 
implemented that stops training after 10 epochs of no improvement in the model's performance against the validation dataset. For the affinity-na\"{i}ve model, the
smoothing scale $\Upsilon=a\Upsilon_0$ is annealed during this third stage onward, with $a$ linearly decaying from 1 to 0 by the end of training. Bulk loss is the most complex 
and best captures the systemic error covariation between the abundance and chemistry predictions within the constraint that mass be conserved; it is
applied in concert with the other losses in the fourth stage of training, and the adaptive dropout is disabled for stability. Instead, an adaptive input noise is applied as a regularization.

This sequential training schedule is summarized as follows (the losses from an example training history are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch4-train-history}):
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item train the uninitialized encoder and chemistry heads, with the mole heads frozen and
        $\lambda_n = \lambda_s = 0$;
  \item train the uninitialized mole heads, with the encoder and chemistry heads frozen and
        $\lambda_x = 0$;
  \item whole model annealing by training everything jointly with $\lambda_b = 0$.
  \item whole model annealing with a mass conservation constraint, by training everything jointly with $\lambda_b > 0$.
\end{enumerate}

The AdamW optimizer is used with weight decay set at $10^{-4}$ for the encoder
and chemistry heads. No weight decay is used on the mole heads. 

\emph{nGibbs} uses an adaptive dropout during training. After each epoch, if the validation 
loss exceeds 1.05 times the training loss, the dropout rate (outside the mole heads) is raised by a small amount, such as 0.005. If the validation loss is below the
training loss, dropout is lowered by an even smaller amount, such as 0.001.

\subsubsection{Temperature model for isentropic emulators}
 
The isentropic emulators take pressure and specific entropy as inputs instead of 
pressure and temperature. Temperature, therefore, is not known \emph{a priori} for an isentropic \emph{nGibbs} 
emulator, unlike for true free energy minimizers for which temperature emerges as a consequence of the minimization. Because temperature is a required input to calculate material properties
(see \S\ref{ch4-material-props}), we must retrieve the temperature from the isentropic emulator's output. Thermodynamically, 
temperature is uniquely defined by the entropy and pressure for an equilibrium assemblage, but recovering it requires an iterative 
optimization. To avoid this, \emph{nGibbs} implements a separate temperature satellite model for isentropic emulators that predicts the temperature of 
a state given its pressure, specific entropy and emulated assemblage. This task is divided into two parts: a closed-form reference regression that captures 
most of the variance, and a fully connected neural network that learns the remaining residual.
 
The reference regression is an ordinary least-squares fit of temperature to $P$,
$P^2$, $S$ and $S^2$, together with a linear and a quadratic term for each element in
the bulk composition. Element abundances are expressed as atoms per formula unit, with
cations plus oxygen normalized to 24 and total iron counted as a single species. On its
own, this smooth polynomial captures the first-order shape of an adiabat and how it
shifts with composition. It cannot, however, represent the changes in slope associated with phase transitions.
A small neural network corrects for this. It receives the same input as the reference regression, supplemented with the phase abundances and
compositions of the equilibrium assemblage predicted by the isentropic emulator. The network then predicts a residual with 
respect to the reference regression, allowing for more subtle variations in the adiabat's shape to be learned. Because the
emulator's predictions are used as inputs during inference, they also serve as the training data. The
network has three hidden layers of 256, 128 and 64 units with leaky-ReLU activations.
It is trained on a mean-squared-error loss using min--max normalized residuals, with
adaptive dropout and early stopping enforced by performance metrics against the validation dataset. The learned residuals span
roughly $\pm\SI{200}{K}$. Both the regression and the network are fit only to states with
$S \geq \SI{1.75}{J\,g^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$, which encompasses the range spanned by the mantle adiabats
the models are designed for, without including temperature conditions at the conductive surface or core-mantle boundary (CMB).

\subsection{Material Properties from Phase Equilibria}\label{ch4-material-props}

To support its use in geodynamic or other physical simulations, \emph{nGibbs} implements the equations of state used by 
\textsc{MELTS} and \textsc{HeFESTo} to determine the material properties of phases and of the bulk assemblage. These calculations are cheap 
relative to the free energy minimizations performed by \textsc{MELTS} and \textsc{HeFESTo}, so there is little benefit to emulating them
with a neural network. Furthermore, because these properties are computed from the predicted phase assemblage, they are self-consistent, thermodynamically grounded, and flexible to 
adapt to changes with the parameters of the reference thermodynamic models. These are implemented as vectorized Python (NumPy/PyTorch) translations 
of the \textsc{MELTS} models taken from the open source MAGMA C library (\href{https://github.com/magmasource/MAGMA/commit/705a0fb}{commit \texttt{705a0fb}}), 
and the \textsc{HeFESTo} models from the open source \textsc{HeFESTo} Fortran repository (\href{https://github.com/stixrude/HeFESToRepository/commit/9566177}{commit \texttt{9566177}}). Parameters 
for the equations of state are taken from the MAGMA C library for \textsc{MELTS} except for the apatite reference volume, and for \textsc{HeFESTo} from the library
(\href{https://github.com/stixrude/HeFESTo_Parameters_010123/commit/ff99dd0}{commit \texttt{ff99dd0}}). The \textsc{MELTS} emulators in \emph{nGibbs} use $15.8228$ J\,bar$^{-1}$ for the apatite reference volume,
instead of the $16.4025$ J\,bar$^{-1}$ given in the MAGMA C library so that the volume and density calculations would be consistent with \textsc{alphaMELTS 2.3.1} output. 

Given a phase assemblage, its phase compositions, and a pressure and temperature, \emph{nGibbs} returns density, $G$, $S$, $V$, $C_p$, for both \textsc{MELTS} and 
\textsc{HeFESTo} assemblages, $H$, $\partial V/\partial T$,  $\partial V/\partial P$, $\partial C_p/\partial T$ for \textsc{MELTS} only, and $V_s$, $V_p$, $K_S$, 
$K_T$, $\alpha$ (referring to the shear wave velocity, compressional wave velocity, isentropic 
and isothermal bulk moduli, and the thermal expansivity respectively) for \textsc{HeFESTo} only. These properties are computed for each phase and are aggregated 
to bulk properties. The equations differ from the source codes only in how they are organized and the algorithms used to execute them.

For the \textsc{HeFESTo} properties, the emulator is able to vectorize the isomorphic terms most effectively, whereas the metamorphic terms are more computationally expensive,
because they require solving the molar pressure and temperature derivatives $(dn/dp)_T$ and $(dn/dT)_P$. Because the metamorphic terms are only necessary for 
accurate calculation of the bulk $C_p$, $K_S$, $K_T$, and $\alpha$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch4-eos-terms}), these terms are only evaluated by nGibbs when the user requests properties that need them 
(Listing~\ref{lst:ch4-hefesto-props}). Thus, it is significantly cheaper to provide $S$, $\rho$, $V_p$, and $V_s$ without the other terms, if they are not 
necessary.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=thesispython, caption={Computing bulk \textsc{HeFESTo} properties for an \emph{nGibbs} assemblage,
requesting either the isomorphic terms only or the metamorphic terms as well (pseudocode).},
label={lst:ch4-hefesto-props}]
from ngibbs.engine.models import HeFESToEmulatorCPU
input_array = pd.read_csv('bulk_composition.csv')
PROPERTY_VARIANTS = {
    'isomorphic':  ('rho', 'Vp', 'Vs', 'S'),
    'metamorphic': ('rho', 'Vp', 'Vs', 'S', 'cptot', 'KStot'),

    output['component_moles'],
    PT=torch.tensor(input_array[['P(GPa)(System_main)', 'T(K)(System_main)']]),
    property_names=PROPERTY_VARIANTS['isomorphic'])
\end{lstlisting}

\textsc{MELTS} and \textsc{HeFESTo} evaluate these equations of state sequentially for one phase at one $(T,P,\mathbf{X})$ state per call, whereas
\emph{nGibbs} operates on thousands of states in parallel. The translation, therefore, is a reorganization of the source code to operate on arrays.

These equations of state were empirically validated through comparison against property outputs from \textsc{MELTS} and \textsc{HeFESTo}. Against \textsc{alphaMELTS} 2.3.1
output on the \textsc{MELTS} 1.2.0 reference calculations, bulk $S$, $V$, $H$ and $C_p$ computed from the true assemblage agree to within $0.07\%$ (mean relative error). 
There remains an undiagnosed systematic disagreement in the \emph{nGibbs} implementation for plagioclase $H$ and $S$.
Figs.~\ref{fig:ch4-sections-property-hefesto-earth}--\ref{fig:ch4-sections-property-melts102}
compare the bulk properties reported by each reference code with those computed by \emph{nGibbs} from the reference code's own
assemblage and from the emulator's predicted assemblage.

\subsection{Performance benchmarking}\label{ch4-benchmarking}

Both \textsc{HeFESTo} and \textsc{MELTS} are parallelized by running independent simulations in separate processes, so their wall time depends on the
number of workers as well as on the number of assemblages $N$. Each was timed on two machines: at 1, 4 and 8 workers on a 4-core, 8-thread Intel i7-11370H laptop CPU with
24 GB of RAM, and at 1, 4, 8, 16 and 20 workers on the 20-core Intel Core i7-14700H desktop CPU with 32~GB of RAM.

\textsc{MELTS} 1.0.2 was timed through \textsc{PetThermoTools} (\code{multi_path}) on isobaric cooling paths at 2\,kbar from 1200 to 1000\,\degC{} in 1\,\degC{}
steps (201 equilibria per path), each with its own MORB-like bulk composition (every oxide of a typical MORB multiplied by
$\mathcal{N}(1, 0.1)$ noise and renormalized). Every worker count was run with 1 to 5 paths per worker. A \textsc{PetThermoTools} worker process can solve only one
\textsc{MELTS} path, so $p$ paths per worker are run as $p$ consecutive calls with one path per worker, and wall time includes process
start-up and \textsc{alphaMELTS} library initialization for every path. Equilibria that \textsc{PetThermoTools} failed to return are not counted toward $N$. Beyond about $10^3$ assemblages the wall time is roughly linear in
$N$, so the per-assemblage rate measured for the largest call at each worker count is used to extrapolate to larger $N$. 

\textsc{HeFESTo} was timed on whole-mantle adiabats prepared with the same structure as the training data (\S\ref{state-var-sampling}): 141 states each from 0 to 140 GPa. 
A total of 3 adiabats per worker are measured, run through GNU parallel \citep{tange2021}. Preparing the simulation directories 
was not timed. Both reference codes are timed along paths, where each state is seeded from the last. This is their most efficient mode of use, so the comparison 
does not favor the emulators. The relative speedup of \emph{nGibbs} is particularly impressive when applied to independent states.

The emulators were timed on a single \code{ForwardMB} call per batch after an untimed warm-up call. The \textsc{MELTS}
1.0.2 emulator was given the same cooling paths as \textsc{PetThermoTools}, truncated to the same batch sizes as the \textsc{HeFESTo} emulator, $N = 16$--$262{,}144$ rows. The \textsc{HeFESTo}
Earth isentropic emulator was given a 0--140\,GPa pressure sweep at $S = 2.5$\,J\,g$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$, including conservation of mass and temperature
prediction. Material properties are then added on top of each emulator. For \textsc{HeFESTo} this is the \emph{nGibbs} equations of state with every
term evaluated (\S\ref{ch4-material-props}). For \textsc{MELTS} it is the \textsc{PetThermoTools} property calculator
(\code{calc_phase_props_MELTS_parallel}), which calls the \textsc{alphaMELTS} supplemental calculator for the predicted assemblage
without minimization, in parallel over \textsc{PetThermoTools}' default worker count. This route is faster than the vectorized
translation for large batches (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch4-melts-props-performance}). Some rare low-temperature liquids develop negative atoms after the mass conservation
correction. For these, the liquid properties cannot be evaluated by \textsc{alphaMELTS} and are removed before timing. \textsc{MELTS} emulator times are the 
median of three repeats; all other times are single wall-clock measurements.

The released emulators were evaluated against validation test subsets of 15{,}000 assemblages per model,

\subsection{Phase presence}\label{ch4-results-presence}

Tables~\ref{tab:ch4-quality-melts} and~\ref{tab:ch4-quality-hefesto} give the precision and recall of the predicted phase presence.
For the \textsc{MELTS} emulators, liquid is predicted with precision and recall of \PresLiquidRange{}, and the common silicate phases
(clinopyroxene, orthopyroxene, plagioclase and olivine) with \PresMeltsCommonRange{}. The \textsc{HeFESTo} emulators reach \PresHefFloor{} or better for the
silicate and oxide phases present in more than about 10\% of test assemblages, including garnet, the (Mg,Fe)$_2$SiO$_4$
polymorphs, bridgmanite, post-perovskite, ferropericlase and Ca-perovskite. The exceptions are the Ca-ferrite phase in the Mars
isentropic emulator (recall \PresCaFerriteMarsIsenRecall) and $\gamma$-iron, which is present in \PresGammaFreqRange\% of test assemblages but has a recall as low as
\PresGammaRecallLow. The isothermal and isentropic \textsc{HeFESTo} emulators perform similarly.

Classification quality declines for phases that are rare in the training data and stable only over narrow ranges of pressure,
temperature or composition. In the \textsc{MELTS} emulators these are graphite (precision \PresGraphitePrec), Fe--Ni alloy (\PresAlloyRange{} in
either metric), leucite (\PresLeuciteRange) and nepheline (\PresNephelinePrec{} precision in \textsc{MELTS} 1.2.0 without Cr), each present in \PresRareFreqMax\% of test
assemblages or fewer. Spinel recall falls to \PresSpinelRecallNoCr{} in the \textsc{MELTS} 1.2.0 emulator without Cr, where spinel is less frequent than
in the Cr-bearing model. In the \textsc{HeFESTo} emulators the weakest phases are akimotoite (\PresAkimotoiteRange), $\alpha$-iron
(\PresAlphaIronRange), $\varepsilon$-iron in the Mars models (recall \PresEpsRecallMars, frequency \PresEpsFreqMars) and the NAL phase in the Mars models (recall
\PresNalRecallMars).

\subsection{Mass conservation}\label{ch4-results-mass}

Without correction, the elemental bulk composition reconstructed from the raw network output differs from the requested
composition by a median of \MassRawMedianRange\% per element, depending on the emulator, and \MassRawFracRange\% of element abundances are in error by
more than 1\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch4-bulk-parity}). The largest raw errors are in minor and trace components, such as Cr and Fe$^{3+}$,
where the network assigns small amounts of an element to assemblages whose bulk composition contains little of it. After the
correction of \S\ref{sec:massbalance}, the median relative error falls to about $\MassMBMedian\%$ for every emulator, the 99th
percentile to \MassMBPNinetyNine\% or less, and fewer than \MassMBFracGtOne\% of element abundances differ from the request by more than 1\%.

\subsection{Phase proportions along reference paths}\label{ch4-results-sections}

Figures~\ref{fig:ch4-sections-phase-hefesto-earth} and~\ref{fig:ch4-sections-phase-hefesto-mars} compare phase proportions along
isentropes of reference compositions computed with \textsc{HeFESTo} and with the isentropic emulators, given pressure, entropy and
bulk composition. For basalt, depleted MORB mantle and Si-enriched pyrolite on Earth, the emulator reproduces the sequence of phase
assemblages from the surface to 140\,GPa, including the olivine--wadsleyite--ringwoodite transitions, the post-spinel transition near
23--25\,GPa and the post-perovskite transition between about 120 and 130\,GPa, with phase proportions that are visually
indistinguishable over most of each path. The visible differences are confined to thin fields, such as the narrow akimotoite field
in basalt near 25--30\,GPa, and to small ripples in otherwise smooth phase proportions. The Mars emulator reproduces the basalt,
harzburgite and shergottite paths to 25\,GPa with the same fidelity, including the stishovite, kyanite and $\gamma$-iron fields of
the enriched compositions. The largest differences on the Mars paths are small shifts of the clinopyroxene--garnet boundary in the
shergottite between about 15 and 18\,GPa.

\subsection{Liquid lines of descent}\label{ch4-results-lld}

Figure~\ref{fig:ch4-liquid-harker-melts} compares the liquid compositions along the lherzolite and MORB cooling paths. The
emulator follows the \textsc{alphaMELTS} liquid lines of descent closely for MgO, FeO, Fe$_2$O$_3$, Al$_2$O$_3$ and Cr$_2$O$_3$,
including the inflections at which phases enter or leave the assemblage. The differences grow as the liquid fraction becomes small.
For the most evolved lherzolite liquids (SiO$_2 > 52$\,wt\%), the emulator underestimates Na$_2$O and K$_2$O and overestimates
CaO, and its path extends to about 56\,wt\% SiO$_2$ where \textsc{alphaMELTS} stops near 54.5\,wt\%. For the last MORB liquids,
H$_2$O and P$_2$O$_5$ diverge from \textsc{alphaMELTS} by up to about 0.4 and 0.15\,wt\%, respectively. These are the states in
which the liquid holds a small fraction of the system's mass, so an error in the liquid composition has little effect on mass
balance and is weakly constrained by it.

The speed-up of \emph{nGibbs} over \textsc{MELTS} is smaller than for HeFESTo because \textsc{alphaMELTS} is itself much faster than \textsc{HeFESTo}, and is timed favorably
here along cooling paths on which each equilibrium starts from the previous one. On the laptop, \textsc{alphaMELTS} through
\textsc{PetThermoTools} reached at most about 67 equilibria per second (8 workers), against about 8{,}500 assemblages per second for
the emulator on a single CPU call of 262{,}144 assemblages, a factor of about 130. On the desktop the corresponding rates are about
800 equilibria per second (20 workers) and 24{,}000 assemblages per second, a factor of about 30. When properties are added through
the \textsc{PetThermoTools} property calculator, which dominates the cost for large batches, the factors fall to about 26 (laptop)
and 15 (desktop). The emulator's cost is nearly constant below about $10^3$ assemblages, where fixed overheads dominate, and grows
roughly linearly above about $10^4$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch4-performance}). The crossover points between the vectorized and sequential
property routes for \textsc{MELTS} are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch4-melts-props-performance}.

For the \textsc{MELTS} property calculation benchmark, the sequential approach is more efficient for evaluating fewer states because previous calculations can be reused and previous states 
become good initial guesses for optimization problems, but a vectorized approach becomes faster when many states are evaluated at once. A multi-threaded approach, however, is able to leverage 
benefits afforded to both sequential and parallel workflows. For MELTS properties, the 
vectorized translation is faster than the single-threaded sequential workflow, but slower than the multi-threaded workflow leveraged by the \textsc{PetThermoTools} 
\code{calc_phase_props_MELTS_parallel()} function \citep{gleeson2025} for more than about 2000 states (Fig.~\ref{fig:ch4-melts-props-performance}). The single-threaded sequential workflow 
avoids the overhead cost of deploying multiple threads, and is fastest for fewer than about 700 states. The vectorized approach is the fastest between 700 and 2000 states evaluated at once, but because this speedup is marginal and 
\emph{nGibbs} is meant for applications at much larger batch sizes, we generally recommend that users calculate properties using the multi-threaded implementation in \textsc{PetThermoTools}.

\subsection{\textsc{PetThermoTools} Integration}\label{ch4-ptt-integration}

\textsc{PetThermoTools} \citep{gleeson2025} is a Python package that runs \textsc{alphaMELTS} for Python
\citep{antoshechkinaghiorso2018} and \textsc{MAGEMin} \citep{riel2022} through one interface. It serves as a front end interface that 
allows users to interact with different thermodynamic models through a single, consistent API, which accesses different underlying models through a
\code{Model} keyword argument. Its outputs are \textsc{pandas} DataFrames keyed by phase in the
\textsc{alphaMELTS} naming convention, and it runs independent calculations in separate child processes so that a failed
\textsc{MELTS} calculation does not halt a batch \citep{gleeson2025}. The \textsc{MELTS} emulators of \emph{nGibbs} are now also integrated within
\textsc{PetThermoTools}, accessible with the \code{Model} keyword of a \textsc{MELTS} model preceded by the letter \texttt{n} (e.g.\ \code{"nMELTSv1.0.2"}), and their predicted
assemblages are returned in the same structure as an \textsc{alphaMELTS} result. This serves two purposes. First, it makes
\emph{nGibbs} accessible to users who already work in \textsc{PetThermoTools} and who need not learn a separate interface. Second,
it makes the emulators straightforward to benchmark, because the same function call can be run with the emulator and with the
\textsc{MELTS} model it replaces, and the results compared directly. \citet{gleeson2025} argued that a common interface would
encourage systematic comparison of thermodynamic models across $P$--$T$--$X$ space, and the same reasoning applies to comparing an
emulator with its parent.

By default, the emulator output is passed to the \textsc{PetThermoTools} property calculator, which calls the \textsc{alphaMELTS} 
supplemental calculator for each predicted phase without performing a minimization, returning material properties for each phase and for the bulk assemblage. 
For applications where these properties are not required, this step can be bypassed by appending \texttt{NoProp} to the \code{Model} keyword (e.g.\ \code{"nMELTSv1.0.2NoProp"}),
resulting in significantly faster performance. At this time, \emph{nGibbs} through \textsc{PetThermoTools} is only supported on the CPU. Users interested in running nGibbs on their 
CUDA-enabled GPU may do so through the \emph{nGibbs} interface directly.

\subsection{Seismic inversions of the thermochemical structure of planetary mantles}\label{ch4-mcmc-inversions}

Seismic data constrain the thermal and compositional structure of planetary mantles \citep{stixrude2012,jackson2021,khan2018,wood1996}. 
A thermochemical inversion of seismic data begins with a forward model: bulk composition and thermal profiles are converted to 
density and seismic velocities that follow from a phase assemblage predicted through free energy minimization. These seismic and density profiles 
then are compared with seismic and physical observations \citep{khan2008,khan2021}. A Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampler 
requires many thousands of such likelihood evaluations, which in turn each require many free energy minimizations. Every additional
compositional parameter enlarges the volume the sampler must explore. This is why compositional dimensions are often fixed, as
in the InSight inversion of \citet{khan2021}, which assumed a single bulk composition derived from the martian meteorites.

The \textsc{HeFESTo} emulators are well-suited to this problem. Listing~\ref{lst:ch4-earth-mantle} builds 201 whole-mantle profiles
which span Earth's interior from 0--135\,GPa, as an example workflow for this problem. Each profile is a conductive lid over an isentropic mantle over a conductive
thermal boundary layer at the core--mantle boundary; the lid and isentrope follow the geophysical parameterization of
\citet{khan2021}. Across the set, adiabat entropy, pressure at the adiabatic-conductive interfaces, temperature of the mantle at the core--mantle boundary, 
and composition of the mantle are varied. The isentropic segments are evaluated first in parallel as a single batch with the isentropic emulator, which also predicts their temperatures. 
Both conductive layers are then evaluated as a second batch with the isothermal emulator, and the vectorized \textsc{HeFESTo} equations of state
return density, $V_P$, $V_S$ and bulk modulus using isomorphic terms only for every node (Figs.~\ref{fig:ch4-earth-mantle} and~\ref{fig:ch4-earth-mantle-phases}). 
In the run shown, the full set of 35{,}175 assemblages completes in under 5 seconds on a GPU, whereas the true HeFESTo calculation would take about 10 hours. At that rate an inversion can sample 
bulk composition alongside the thermal parameters, rather than fixing it in advance, enabling future studies to use seismic data to constrain composition with a level of detail
that has never before been possible. The Mars emulators can be used in the same way: they are trained to 25.5\,GPa, which spans the pressure at the martian core--mantle
boundary of $\sim$20\,GPa estimated by \citet{khan2008}. We are already exploring this avenue in ongoing work.

On Earth, where seismic data are abundant, density and velocity profiles are more tightly constrained and carry more chemical information.
A joint inversion for temperature and composition beneath plumes and subduction zones could separate the thermal
contribution to discontinuity topography and sharpness from the compositional one, and could test whether the transition zone is
compositionally stratified relative to the mantle above and below it. The 2024 \textsc{HeFESTo} database, which the Earth emulator
reproduces, includes ferric iron, the high-spin to low-spin transition in ferropericlase, and native iron phases
\citep{stixrude2024}. Those additions make the emulator a candidate forward model for the iron-rich compositions proposed for ULVZs
and LLVPs \citep{dobson2005,jackson2021,yuan2023}. Some of these candidates, however, such as banded iron formation with up to 85\,wt\% iron
oxide \citep{dobson2005}, lie far outside the composition range of the current training data and would require targeted
training data before the emulator can be applied to them.

\subsection{Mantle thermochemistry beneath spreading centers}\label{ch4-morb-inversion}

An emulator of \textsc{pHMELTS} is planned. \textsc{pHMELTS} uses the \textsc{pMELTS} calibration for mantle melting and it approximates 
the storage of water in nominally anhydrous minerals by
partitioning H$_2$O into a hidden reservoir \citep{asimow2004,smithasimow2005}. This model shows that trace amounts of water can have a
significant effect on mantle melting \citep{asimowlangmuir2003,asimow2004}. A fast emulator of polybaric melting followed by crystallization
may allow individual mid-ocean ridge basalts to be inverted for the potential temperature, source composition and water content
of the mantle that produced them, and repeating the inversion along ridge segments would build a map of mantle thermochemistry
beneath spreading centers.

Existing methods for recovering primary magmas show why such an inversion is difficult. PRIMELT2 restores a lava to its primary
magma by adding or subtracting olivine alone and infers potential temperature from the primary magma's MgO content
\citep{herzberg2008}. It cannot be applied to lavas that have also fractionated clinopyroxene, which usually happens once the
liquid has evolved below 7--10\,wt\% MgO, and applying olivine addition to such lavas yields primary magmas with too much MgO and
source temperatures that are too high \citep{herzberg2008}. PRIMARSMELT, the martian member of the same family, has the same
restriction: liquids that have fractionated olivine, clinopyroxene and plagioclase cannot be easily inverted by this method without
a significant increase in computational cost and uncertainty \citep{hernandezmontenegro2024}. This restriction effectively excludes every lava that has
evolved beyond olivine-only fractionation.

Multiple saturation is also ambiguous evidence of origin. \citet{asimowlonghi2004} showed that aggregate liquids from polybaric
near-fractional melting can display near-intersections of liquidus surfaces that cannot be distinguished experimentally from true
multiple saturation points, so a multiply saturated liquid need not be a single batch that equilibrated with its residue. They did show, however, 
that such multiple saturation points preserve information about the average pressure at which the constituent increments of
liquid were extracted \citep{asimowlonghi2004}. Lavas with complex melting and differentiation history likely encode some of this information in their
geochemistry, but attempting to recover it requires a fast and flexible forward model that properly treats melting and multi-phase fractionation
together. A \textsc{pHMELTS} emulator would provide such a forward model. Whether the
inversion is well posed for a given suite of lavas, given the trade-offs among potential temperature, source fertility and water
content, remains to be tested.

\section{Discussion}\label{ch4-discussion}

\subsection{Strengths}\label{ch4-discussion-strengths}

The central result of this chapter is that a single emulator per parent model can reproduce that model's phase equilibria across
a broad range of composition, pressure, and temperature conditions and needn't be restricted to a single compositional path or magma series. 
The \emph{nGibbs} \textsc{HeFESTo} Earth emulator follows basaltic, peridotitic and Si-enriched compositions from the surface to 140\,GPa, and the
\textsc{MELTS} emulators follow compositions from lherzolite to rhyolite, with the same network in each case
(\S\ref{ch4-results-sections}). The surrogates reviewed in \S\ref{ch4-prev-work} were each trained on a narrower slice: arc magmas
with fixed water content \citep{candioti2026}, or compositions near the basalt--harzburgite join
\citep{hartmeier2026,yuan2025igythem}.

The emulators predict the same quantities as their parent models, the abundance of every phase and the composition of every
solution phase, so physical properties are computed with the parent model's own equations of state rather than learned as separate
targets. These differences are far smaller than the uncertainty of the thermodynamic databases themselves \citep{ghiorsosack1995,ghiorso2002,stixrude2005,riel2022,holland1998}. 
For most applications, therefore, the choice of database matters more than the choice between the emulator and its parent.

As in the parent models, mass balance is enforced exactly rather than encouraged. This is a requirement for reactive transport and for any calculation that
removes or adds material step by step, such as fractional crystallization, where a small bias per step would accumulate.
\citet{hartmeier2026} penalize mass-balance violations during training and report small residual deviations, while the surrogate of
\citet{candioti2026} closes mass balance only for the bulk solid, by difference.

The emulators are fast without a GPU. On a 4-core laptop CPU, the \textsc{HeFESTo} Earth emulator is four orders of magnitude faster
than \textsc{HeFESTo} on the same machine, and a GPU adds a further factor of 3--8 (\S\ref{ch4-results-performance}). Because \emph{nGibbs} 
reaches its largest CPU speed-up on a 4-core laptop, it is usable on ordinary workstations. Through \textsc{PetThermoTools}, it is also usable without
learning a new interface (\S\ref{ch4-ptt-integration}).

\subsection{Limitations}\label{ch4-discussion-limitations}

An emulator can be no more accurate than its parent model. The \textsc{MELTS} emulators inherit the known limitations of
\textsc{MELTS}, including its calibration toward low-pressure, nearly anhydrous experiments \citep{ghiorsosack1995} and its poor
treatment of hydrous minerals such as amphibole \citep{candioti2026}. The \textsc{HeFESTo} emulators inherit a database that has no
silicate liquid. The emulators are also reliable only inside their training coverage
(Figs.~\ref{fig:ch4-coverage-pt}--\ref{fig:ch4-coverage-harker-melts120}). Compositions far from that coverage, such as banded iron
formation or carbonatite, need targeted training data and validation before use.

Three systematic weaknesses appear in the results. First, phases that are rare and stable over narrow ranges are predicted less
reliably (\S\ref{ch4-results-presence}). Akimotoite is the most consequential of these for mantle applications, because its stability
near the 660-km discontinuity is one of the composition-sensitive reactions that a transition-zone inversion would aim to resolve
\citep{hartmeier2026}; oversampling its stability field is a priority before that application. Second, the emulator smooths sharp features 
in the parent model, such as the MORB solidus in \textsc{MELTS}, over a few tens of degrees. This follows from the
continuous architecture (\S\ref{sec:continuous}), which is correct for continuous phase-out reactions but blurs changes that are nearly
discontinuous, as at this solidus. Third, properties that depend on derivatives of the phase proportions ($C_p$, $K_S$
and $\alpha$) and liquid compositions at small melt fractions carry larger errors than phase proportions do
(\S\ref{ch4-results-lld} and~\ref{ch4-results-properties}) and require higher fidelity predictions. Applications that rely on these quantities, such as latent-heat
effects at phase transitions or the composition of the last liquids, should check them against the parent model.

The speed advantage also has limits. Fixed overheads make the emulators no faster than the parent model for very small batches,
and the advantage over \textsc{alphaMELTS} on a multicore machine is about one to two orders of magnitude, not four, especially once
properties are computed through \textsc{PetThermoTools} (\S\ref{ch4-results-performance}). The emulators pay off most when many
states are evaluated together, as in the inversions and coupled models of \S\ref{ch4-application}.

Finally, the release is deliberately partial. Only the closed-system isothermal \textsc{MELTS} emulators and the \textsc{HeFESTo}
emulators are released; the open-system \textsc{MELTS} emulators await further testing. Emulator error is not yet 
propagated into downstream calculations, which an
inversion would need in its likelihood. Planned work includes a \textsc{pHMELTS} emulator (\S\ref{ch4-morb-inversion}), targeted
training for rare phases and unusual compositions, and a systematic map of the depth, width and velocity contrast of the
transition-zone discontinuities as functions of composition.

\section{Conclusions}\label{ch4-conclusions}

This chapter introduced \emph{nGibbs}, a set of deep-learning emulators of the \textsc{MELTS} 1.0.2 and 1.2.0 and \textsc{HeFESTo}
thermodynamic models. Its goal is to stand in for its parent models in essentially any calculation they would be used for, rather
than for one composition range or one set of outputs. Four design choices support that goal:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item  Each emulator predicts the full equilibrium assemblage, so thermodynamically consistent physical properties follow from the parent model's own equations of state. 
  \item  Phase abundances are predicted as rectified affinities, which keeps them continuous across phase boundaries like the parent model they emulate.
  \item  Mass is conserved exactly at inference by a constrained correction that itself is constrained to produce legal (non-negative) atomic abundances.
  \item  The training compositions are drawn from whole-rock databases of igneous rocks that span the breadth of compositional diversity of igneous rock compositions known on Earth. 
\textsc{MELTS} 1.2.0 is additionally trained on a database of sedimentary rock compositions, and \textsc{HeFESTo} models are strongly perturbed to cover compositions that are rare or unknown 
at Earth's surface. The validation data are drawn from distinct bulk compositions to ensure that the emulators generalize to new compositions.
\end{enumerate}

The released emulators reproduce their parent models closely within their training coverage. The major silicate and oxide phases are predicted
with precision and recall of 0.93 or better. Element abundances are conserved to a median relative error of about $5\times10^{-6}\%$.
Phase proportions, densities and seismic velocities along reference paths are nearly indistinguishable
from those of \textsc{alphaMELTS} and \textsc{HeFESTo}. Subtle behaviors observed in liquid lines of descent are learned and reproduced by the emulators. 
Errors are largest for rare phases with narrow stability fields, such as akimotoite and the native iron phases. They are also larger where sharp changes occur in the parent model, 
for material properties such as heat capacity, bulk modulus and thermal expansivity that depend on molar derivatives; and for liquid compositions at small melt fractions. 
These differences remain smaller than the uncertainties of the underlying thermodynamic databases for most applications. They do, however, mark where
targeted training data are needed before specific applications, such as resolving akimotoite stability near the 660-km discontinuity.

The emulators are fast enough to change what is computationally practical. On a 4-core laptop CPU, the \textsc{HeFESTo} Earth
emulator evaluates $2^{15}$ assemblages about 22{,}000 times faster than \textsc{HeFESTo}, and about 1{,}100 times faster with the
full equations of state. A set of 201 whole-mantle profiles of phase equilibria, density, and sound velocity, comprising about 35{,}000 assemblages, takes under 5\,s on a GPU,
compared with about 10 hours for \textsc{HeFESTo}. The \textsc{MELTS} emulators run two orders of magnitude faster than
\textsc{alphaMELTS} and, through \textsc{PetThermoTools}, can replace it in existing workflows by changing a single keyword.

Several applications follow directly from this speed and generality. In seismic inversions of the Earth's and Mars' mantles, bulk
composition can be sampled alongside the thermal parameters rather than fixed in advance. Composition-dependent transition-zone
structure and candidate compositions for ultralow-velocity zones can be explored systematically. Geodynamic models can carry a
chemically heterogeneous mantle, or the cumulates of a solidifying magma ocean, without reducing composition to a single mixing
variable. Planned extensions include an emulator of \textsc{pHMELTS} for inverting mid-ocean ridge basalts for the thermochemical
state of the mantle beneath spreading centers, and spatially resolved models of reactive porous flow in crystal mushes.
