
\section{Derivations for Bounding Method Equations}\label{estimate-permeation-lag-uncertainty-for-uncorrected-long-heating-steps}

The uncertainty of the helium evolution of the encapsulated natural
material during a heating step (\(\omega\)) due to permeation lag
correction can be estimated by calculating the maximum possible helium
residing in the tube between steps, i.e. for the values for
\emph{n\textsubscript{0, }}and \emph{n\textsubscript{t}}: \(n_{0}^{UB}\)
and \(n_{t}^{UB}.\ \)From the permeation lag equation (main text Eq.~\MainEqPermlag), upper bound estimates for
\emph{n\textsubscript{0, }}and \emph{n\textsubscript{t}} bound the
encapsulated material contribution \(\omega\) within:

\begin{equation}
m - n_{0}^{UB} \leq \omega \leq m + n_{t}^{UB}
\label{eq:s6-1}
\end{equation}

Upper bounds for \emph{n\textsubscript{t}} for a given high temperature
experimental step can be calculated if step heats are long enough to
assume steady-state permeation, assuming G and K are known or can be
conservatively estimated. To proceed, one must make the generally
conservative assumption that the production rate of helium within the
ampoule due to degassing of the encapsulated natural sample
(\(\Omega = \frac{\omega}{t}\)) is constant over time at a given
temperature. In reality, \(\Omega\) will decrease over time for helium
loss governed by first order processes \citep{yakubovich2010,wilson1973} or diffusion (except for the case of initially
inhomogeneous distributions, the memory of which is often erased early
in any diffusion experiment: \citealp{shuster2004}). Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-3}, given
below, shows that the amount of helium trapped in the tube (\(n_{t}\))
is proportional to the rate of helium outgassing from the natural
encapsulated sample (\(\Omega\)), so the approximation that this rate is
constant will overestimate the amount of unanalyzed helium remaining in
the ampoule after a high temperature experimental step. The rate of
helium degassing over time will decrease for degassing controlled by
second-order volume diffusion \emph{except} for the minor case of the
alpha-ejection effect at low He-release fractions \citep{farley1996,ketcham2011} or for the more serious effects of radiation
damage annealing \citep{goldsmith2020} and mineral decomposition
\citep{farley2024}. In any case, sequential isothermal steps
immediately reveal when \(\frac{\omega}{t}\) increases over time.

A new first order differential equation like Eq.~\MainEqPermode\ is formulated, now
incorporating a constant source term (\(\Omega\)) to conservatively
approximate the helium influx into the ampoule from the degassing
natural sample.

\begin{equation}
\frac{dn}{dt} = \Omega - \lambda n
\label{eq:s6-2}
\end{equation}

Where \(\Omega = \frac{\omega}{t}\) and \(\lambda = KGRT\). Immediately,
the steady-state quantity of helium atoms (\(n_{ss}\)) present in the
ampoule can be found by solving Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-2} for the steady-state condition
where \(\frac{dn}{dt} = 0\).

\begin{equation}
n_{ss} = \frac{\Omega}{\lambda} = \frac{\omega}{\lambda t}
\label{eq:s6-3}
\end{equation}

Additionally, the helium concentration within the ampoule during the
approach to the steady-state concentration can be solved for by
integrating Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-2} with respect to \emph{t}. First, the integrating
factor for Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-2} is identified: \(I = e^{\lambda t}\), which is
multiplied to both sides to yield Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-4}.

\begin{equation}
e^{\lambda t}\frac{dn}{dt} + e^{\lambda t}\lambda n = e^{\lambda t}\Omega\
\label{eq:s6-4}
\end{equation}

Then both sides are integrated with respect to \emph{t:}

\[\int_{}^{}\left( e^{\lambda t}\frac{dn}{dt} + e^{\lambda t}\lambda n \right)dt = \int_{}^{}{e^{\lambda t}\Omega}dt\]

Because
\({\frac{d}{dt}(ne^{\lambda t}) = e}^{\lambda t}\frac{dn}{dt} + e^{\lambda t}\lambda n\)
by the product rule Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-4} reduces to:

\begin{equation}
n_{t}e^{\lambda t} = \frac{\Omega}{\lambda}e^{\lambda t} + C
\label{eq:s6-5}
\end{equation}

Where \emph{C} is an unknown constant introduced by indefinite
integration. This expression becomes more intuitive when both sides are
divided by the integration factor and by substituting the steady-state
concentration from Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-3}.

\begin{equation}
n_{t} = n_{ss} + Ce^{- \lambda t}
\label{eq:s6-6}
\end{equation}

The integration constant \emph{C} can be solved by enforcing the
condition that \(n_{t}(t = 0) = n_{0}\), leading to:

\begin{equation}
n_{0} = n_{ss} + C
\label{eq:s6-7}
\end{equation}

Solving Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-7} for \emph{C} and substituting into Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-6} yields
Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-8}. Now an inequality is assigned to the equation to reflect the
initial assumption that the isothermal helium outgassing rate
(\(\Omega\)) is constant \emph{or decreasing}.

\begin{equation}
n_{t} \leq \ n_{ss} + \left( n_{0} - n_{ss} \right)e^{- \lambda t}
\label{eq:s6-8}
\end{equation}

For long timescales (i.e. when \(e^{- \lambda t} \approx 0\)), Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-8}
reduces to \(n_{t} \leq \ n_{ss}\). This is effectively true for steps
long enough such that \(t \geq \frac{5}{\lambda}\), for which
\(e^{- \lambda t} \leq 0.007.\) It is advisable to only consider the
Approximation with Uncertainty method for steps of sufficiently long duration such that
\(t \geq \frac{5}{\lambda}\) so that the estimation for \(n_{t}\) can be
reduced to simply the steady-state value and not be dependent upon the
estimation for \(n_{0}\) or its uncertainties. 

To estimate the upper bound on the permeation lag (\(n_{t}^{UB}\)), one
must consider the circumstances that would produce the highest possible
value for \(n_{t}\). In addition, the constraint that
\(t \geq \frac{5}{\lambda}\) is enforced so that previous heating steps
are not significant, simplifying the problem significantly. Because
\(n_{t}\) scales with $\omega$ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-3}, the upper bound estimate
\(n_{t}^{UB}\) must assume the upper bound estimate for $\omega$, which is
given in Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-1} as \({m + n}_{t}^{UB}\). Substituting
\(\omega^{UB} = {m + n}_{t}^{UB}\) into Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-3} to express
\(n_{t}^{UB}\) in terms of the measured value \emph{m}:

\[
n_{t}^{UB} = n_{ss}(\omega = m + n_{t}^{UB}) = \frac{m + n_{t}^{UB}}{\lambda t}
\]

Solving for \(n_{t}^{UB}\) yields Eq.~\eqref{eq:6}:

\begin{equation}
n_{t}^{UB} = \frac{m}{\lambda t - 1}
\label{eq:6}
\end{equation}

\section{Analytical Methods for Hydrothermal Goethite Step Heat Experiment}\label{sec:si-analytical-methods}

\subsection{Uncertainty Quantification for Bounding Method}

\paragraph{Helium-in-Goethite Diffusivity Inversions}\label{helium-in-goethite-diffusivity-inversions}

Apparent values of D/a\textsuperscript{2} for \textsuperscript{3}He and \textsuperscript{4}He were
calculated from times and cumulative measured yields using the equations of \citet{fechtigkalbitzer1966}. 
Lower and upper bounds for diffusivity were calculated 
using the upper bound for the helium contents of the 
ampoules before and after each step, evaluated using \MainEqSteadystate.
Diffusivity for step $i$ is a function of the cumulative fractional yield for step i 
$\Sigma ^{i}F$ and the cumulative fractional yield of the previous step $\Sigma ^{i-1}F$. The diffusivity
is maximized when the difference between the fractional yields is largest, and minimized when the difference is smallest. 
This difference is maximized for the extreme case where the previous step ended with no permeation lag, and the current step ended 
with the maximum amount of permeation lag $\left(_{SS}^{i}n_t\right)$, whereas the opposite is true for the minimum diffusivity. 


\begin{equation}
\label{eq:diffusivity-bounds}
\begin{gathered}
{}^{UB}D_\omega = D\!\left(\Sigma^{i-1}F_m ,~ \Sigma^{i}F_m + {}_{SS}^{i}n_t\right) \\
{}^{LB}D_\omega = D\!\left(\Sigma^{i-1}F_m + {}_{SS}^{i-1}n_t ,~ \Sigma^{i}F_m\right)
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}

For the \textsuperscript{3}He diffusivities, a linear regression was fit to the steps with cumulative \textsuperscript{3}He fractional
yield $\geq$7\% (\ChTwoN\ steps, Fig.~\MainFigArrheniusZoom),
yielding an R\textsuperscript{2} statistic of \ChTwoRtwo. The fit's slope and intercept are used as a reference line to
calculate ``Delta'' values for the diffusivities of \textsuperscript{3}He and \textsuperscript{4}He (Fig.~\MainFigDeltaZoom). No uncertainty on
the total amounts of \textsuperscript{3}He and \textsuperscript{4}He are propagated into the diffusivities
because the experiment operated at low cumulative yields where this
error affects all diffusivities approximately homogeneously, so it is only
relevant for the y-intercept of the regression and the corresponding
calculated closure temperature. The uncertainty from the
intercept and slope parameters of the linear regression is significantly
larger than the uncertainty related to the total yield, so it is
neglected.

\paragraph{Helium Isotope Ratio Uncertainty}\label{helium-isotope-ratio-uncertainty}

The upper and lower bounds of the $\frac{^4He}{^3He}$ isotope ratios for each heating step are taken as the maximum and minimum $\frac{^4He}{^3He}$
isotope ratio calculated out of the 4 hypothetical end-member permeation scenarios, which would have been experienced by both helium isotope systems:

\begin{enumerate}
\item $n_t = 0,\ n_0 = 0$
\item $n_t = n_t^{\mathrm{UB}},\ n_0 = 0$
\item $n_t = n_t^{\mathrm{UB}},\ n_0 = n_0^{\mathrm{UB}}$
\item $n_t = 0,\ n_0 = n_0^{\mathrm{UB}}$
\end{enumerate}

Intuitively, higher differential permeability between the two helium isotopes ($\delta = \frac{K_{3He}}{K_{4He}}$ here) will result in greater isotope fractionation between the measured $\left(\frac{m_{4}}{m_{3}}\right)$ and true $\left(\frac{\omega_{4}}{\omega_{3}}\right)$.
Furthermore, lower absolute permeabilities will result in stronger permeation lag, additionally producing higher isotope fractionation in the measured value. The \textsuperscript{4}He permeability is thus conservatively taken to be identical to \citet{swets1961}, an anhydrous
literature value which this experiment's own isothermal \textsuperscript{3}He permeation measurements show to underestimate the ampoule's true permeability (Fig.~\MainFigAmpoulePermeability); \textsuperscript{4}He permeability itself could not be independently measured in this experiment. For the purposes of evaluating the measured isotope ratios, the relative \textsuperscript{3}He/\textsuperscript{4}He permeability ($\delta$) is taken to be 1.29, the 2$\sigma$ upper bound of the value measured independently for a helium-soaked water-filled ampoule:
1.10 $\pm$ 0.19 (2$\sigma$). 1.29 is in great excess of realistic values of the relative \textsuperscript{3}He/\textsuperscript{4}He permeability, which is expected to be between 1.06--1.11 for the temperature range relevant to this experiment \citep{jones1953}. These
conservative assumptions taken together guarantee that the calculated upper and lower bounds for the isotope ratios accurately constrain the true value. The upper bounds of the
ampoule contents are calculated using the steady-state approximation of Eq.~\MainEqSteadystate\ (main text \S\MainRefBounding): $n_{ss} = \frac{m}{\lambda t - 1}$. For the four extreme cases listed above, the following equations apply:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \scalebox{1.5}{$\frac{\omega_{4}}{\omega_{3}} = \frac{m_{4}}{m_{3}}$}
\item \scalebox{1.5}{$\frac{\omega_{4}}{\omega_{3}} = \frac{m_{4}+\frac{m_{4}}{\lambda t - 1}}{m_{3}+\frac{m_{3}}{\delta \lambda t - 1}}$}
\item \scalebox{1.5}{$\frac{\omega_{4}}{\omega_{3}} = \frac{m_{4}+\frac{m_{4}}{\lambda t - 1} - \frac{m_4^{i-1}}{\left(\lambda t\right)^{i-1} - 1}}{m_{3}+\frac{m_{3}}{\delta \lambda t - 1} - \frac{m_3^{i-1}}{\delta\left(\lambda t\right)^{i-1} - 1}}$}
\item \scalebox{1.5}{$\frac{\omega_{4}}{\omega_{3}} = \frac{m_{4} - \frac{m_4^{i-1}}{\left(\lambda t\right)^{i-1} - 1}}{m_{3} - \frac{m_3^{i-1}}{\delta\left(\lambda t\right)^{i-1} - 1}}$}
\end{enumerate}

Where $m_{x}^{i-1}$ and $\left(\lambda t \right)^{i-1}$ refer to the measured helium content and $\lambda t$ of the previous step, respectively. $\lambda$ here applies explicitly to \textsuperscript{4}He, whereas $\delta \lambda$ applies to \textsuperscript{3}He. The upper and lower bounds of the isotope ratios are then taken to be the maximum and minimum of these four extreme cases.


\subsection{Sensitivity and Uncertainty Calculations}\label{sec:si-sensitivity-uncertainty}

\textsuperscript{3}He and \textsuperscript{4}He sensitivities were calculated for each analysis of the MM
standard gas, assuming that each standard aliquot contained
157.50 $\pm$ 0.41 (2$\sigma$) nccSTP \textsuperscript{4}He and 3.567 $\pm$ 0.040 (2$\sigma$) pccSTP \textsuperscript{3}He. These values
were obtained from sensitivities from a known amount of a standard gas
with similar helium abundances and from the signals from 10 aliquots of
the new MM standard tank in June 2023.

1$\sigma$ uncertainties related to internal reproducibility were estimated
for each sensitivity calculation as the rolling standard deviation of
sensitivities calculated from 5 MM standards. In other words, the
internal reproducibility of the sensitivity was estimated as the
standard deviation of each calculated sensitivity and the 2
sensitivities immediately before and after. Uncertainties related to
external reproducibility from the amount of \textsuperscript{3}He and \textsuperscript{4}He in each standard
aliquot were added to the total uncertainty as well.

Sensitivities and their uncertainties were interpolated to times between
standard analyses using a Piecewise Cubic Hermite Interpolating
Polynomial (PCHIP) from the SciPy package in python. These interpolated
sensitivities and uncertainties were used to convert the \textsuperscript{3}He and \textsuperscript{4}He
signals (cps and fA respectively) to absolute atomic abundances and
uncertainties in units of pccSTP and nccSTP respectively. The
sensitivities and interpolations are graphically displayed in the
``STDs'' tab in a plot generated using the Matplotlib package in python.

\section{Finite Difference Diffusion Model of Cooling Tube}\label{sec:finite-difference-diffusion-model-of-cooling-tube}

\subsection{Numerical Methods}\label{sec:numerical-methods}

A numerical model to estimate diffusive permeation is invaluable, as it
provides a flexible way to estimate the permeation fraction
\(\left( \frac{m}{n_{0}} \right)\) even when the required assumptions
for the analytical solutions are not met. For example, a numerical
approach can calculate the expected permeation fraction for a
non-isothermal time-temperature history, and it needn't obey the
assumption that the helium concentration profile in the glass is in
diffusive equilibrium with the partial pressure of helium in the
encapsulated gas phase. As will be investigated below, the equilibrium
concentration profile assumption is ephemerally broken when temperatures
are changed, and for ampoules with high glass/gas ratios it is never
true.

\citet{carslawjaeger1959} give the equation for the change in
concentration by diffusion in a cylinder (eqn. 5, page 17, second
edition), expressed here in terms of concentration and diffusivity.

\begin{equation}
\frac{dC}{dt} = D\left\lbrack \frac{\partial^{2}C}{\partial r^{2}} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial C}{\partial r} + \frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}C}{\partial\theta^{2}} + \frac{\partial^{2}C}{\partial z^{2}} \right\rbrack
\label{eq:s4-1}
\end{equation}

For typical ampoules used in this work, the surface area of the top and
bottom together is about 5--10\% of the surface area of the walls, but
they are neglected here for simplicity. Assuming that the concentration
profiles are radially symmetrical and uniform with height (z), Eq.~\eqref{eq:s4-1}
is reduced to the form used throughout this section:

\begin{equation}
\frac{dC}{dt} = D\left\lbrack \frac{\partial^{2}C}{\partial r^{2}} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial C}{\partial r} \right\rbrack
\label{eq:s4-2}
\end{equation}

We numerically solve Eq.~\eqref{eq:s4-2} using the Forward-Time Central-Space (FTCS) Finite Difference
Method (FDM) along a radial transect of a model ampoule of arbitrary
geometry for small, discrete time intervals. The system is spatially
discretized into 100 cells along a radial transect, and numerical
stability was ensured by solving for the time interval of a single step,
$\Delta t$, such that \(\frac{D\Delta t}{\Delta r^{2}} = 0.5\), satisfying the
Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) condition.

Helium concentrations along the transect (except for the boundaries,
where r = a and r = b) are updated at each step using an FTCS
discretization of the radially symmetric diffusion equation for a
cylinder (Eq.~\eqref{eq:s4-2}), repeated below for reference before its
discretized form:

\[\frac{dC_{r}}{dt} = D\left\lbrack \frac{\partial^{2}C}{\partial r^{2}} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial C}{\partial r} \right\rbrack\]

\begin{equation}
\Delta C_{r} \approx \Delta t\  \bullet D\left\lbrack \frac{C_{r - \Delta r} - 2C_{r} + C_{r + \Delta r}}{\Delta r^{2}} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{C_{r + \Delta r} - C_{r - \Delta r}}{2\Delta r}\  \right\rbrack
\label{eq:s5-0}
\end{equation}

Dirichlet boundary conditions were enforced at the inner and outer
boundaries. The concentration at the model ampoule's outer boundary
(where r = b) was held at 0, while the concentration at the inner
boundary (where r = a) was calculated at each discrete step by
multiplying the solubility (in units of
\(\frac{atoms}{atm \times cm^{3}}\)) by the helium partial pressure
within the ampoule, according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:s5-1} or \eqref{eq:s5-1a}.

\begin{equation}
C_{He}^{glass} = S(T) \bullet P_{He}^{gas}
\label{eq:s5-1}
\end{equation}

Eq.~\eqref{eq:s5-1} can be related to the atomic abundance of helium in the gas
phase using the ideal gas law and the formula for the volume of a
cylinder:

\[P_{He}^{gas} = \frac{n_{gas}RT}{V_{gas}}\]

\[V_{gas} = \pi a^{2}h\]

\begin{equation}
C_{He}^{glass} = S(T) \bullet \frac{n_{gas}RT}{\pi a^{2}h}
\label{eq:s5-1a}
\end{equation}

For helium permeation out of glass ampoules, the internal pressure is not constant. \(n_{gas}\)
changes after every step and Eq.~\eqref{eq:s5-1} is reapplied to the inner
boundary cell where r = a. While the helium concentrations in the glass
and in the encapsulated gas phase are both dependent on the length of
the ampoule (\emph{h}), they compensate for one another such that the
absolute atom flux into and out of the ampoule is not a function of
\emph{h}. 

In order to simulate permeation, the atomic abundances of helium within
the gas phases both outside the ampoule (at high vacuum) and inside the
ampoule must be tracked, as do the fluxes between the glass and these
two gas phases. These fluxes were calculated simply as the diffusive
flux in \(\frac{atoms}{s \bullet cm^{2}}\) as is given by Fick's law. 
For spatially discrete cells throughout the ampoule at any
radius except for the boundaries, the diffusive flux can be approximated
with the finite difference:

\begin{equation}
J_{r} = - D\frac{\partial C_{r}}{\partial r} \approx \  - D\frac{C_{r + \Delta r} - C_{r - \Delta r}}{2\Delta r}
\label{eq:s5-3}
\end{equation}

However, Eq.~\eqref{eq:s5-3} cannot be applied at the ampoule's inner wall when r
= a or outer wall when r = b because there is no remaining glass on one
side of the cell. In that case, second order terms from Taylor
expansions centered at \(a + \Delta r\ \)and \(b - \Delta r\) can be
used to better approximate the \(\frac{\partial C_{a}}{\partial r}\) and
\(\frac{\partial C_{b}}{\partial r}\) terms. Letting
\(f(r) = \ \frac{\partial C_{r}}{\partial r}\) and
\(f'(r) = \ \frac{\partial^{2}C_{r}}{\partial r^{2}}\):

\begin{equation}
\resizebox{0.98\linewidth}{!}{$\displaystyle
J_{a} = - D\frac{\partial C_{a}}{\partial r} \approx \  - D\left( \frac{\partial C_{a + \Delta r}}{\partial r} + \frac{\partial^{2}C_{a + \Delta r}}{\partial r^{2}}\Delta r \right) \approx - D\left( \ \frac{C_{a + 2\Delta r} - C_{a}}{2\Delta r} + \frac{C_{a} - {2C_{a + \Delta r} + C}_{a + 2\Delta r}}{2\Delta r^{2}}\Delta r \right)\
$}
\label{eq:s5-3a}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\resizebox{0.98\linewidth}{!}{$\displaystyle
J_{b} = - D\frac{\partial C_{b}}{\partial r} \approx \  - D\left( \frac{\partial C_{b - \Delta r}}{\partial r} - \frac{\partial^{2}C_{b - \Delta r}}{\partial r^{2}}\Delta r \right) \approx - D\left( \frac{C_{b} - C_{b - 2\Delta r}}{2\Delta r} - \frac{C_{b} - {2C_{b - \Delta r} + C}_{b - 2\Delta r}}{2\Delta r^{2}}\Delta r\  \right)\
$}
\label{eq:s5-3b}
\end{equation}

The helium contents in the measurable (external, high vacuum) reservoir
and the inner encapsulated gas phase are updated every step using fluxes
determined from Eq.~\eqref{eq:s5-3b} and \eqref{eq:s5-3a} respectively. To convert these
area-normalized fluxes into absolute atomic abundances, these fluxes are
multiplied by the time increment ($\Delta t$) and the surface area of the
cylinder at the outer and inner walls respectively.

Simulations require an initial helium concentration profile in the glass
appropriate for a fresh charge of helium at a given temperature. Rather
than deriving a closed-form analytical solution for this profile, it is
obtained numerically: the inner and outer boundary conditions are held
fixed at their values for that charge and temperature (Eq.~\eqref{eq:s5-1a}
and 0, respectively) while the model iterates Eq.~\eqref{eq:s5-0} until the
concentration profile no longer changes, i.e.\ a numerical `burn-in' to
steady state. This produces the same result as solving Eq.~\eqref{eq:s4-2}
analytically for the steady-state profile, without requiring a separate
closed-form derivation.

\subsection{A Parameterized Cooling Rate of the HT-Win-06-01b Ampoule}\label{sec:si-ampoule-cooling-rate}

One potential concern about the results of the internally measured permeabilities is that some additional helium was permeated
during the cooling period after each permeation step. In order to evaluate the significance of 
this effect, a finite difference model was built in python to simulate diffusion, influx, and
outflux of helium across a hollow glass cylinder. The glass
cylinder was given the same dimensions of the ampoule used in the
Win-06-01b hydrothermal experiment, and was subjected to the same
heating and cooling schedule as the hydrothermal experiment's permeation
steps. The tube was charged with 100 arbitrary units of helium atoms at
the end of each experimental step, and the concentration profile for
the new arbitrary charge was initialized at T = 24\textdegree C using the
numerical burn-in procedure described in \S\ref{sec:numerical-methods}. Permeability
values for \textsuperscript{3}He were assumed as the product of the \textsuperscript{4}He permeability
values given by \citet{swets1961} and the \textsuperscript{3}He/\textsuperscript{4}He relative
permeabilities given by \citet{jones1953}.

To simulate cooling, a cooling rate had to be assumed. To most
accurately simulate the cooling of the experimental ampoule, Watlow temperature records
of the experimental ampoule during cooling steps were used to calculate the cooling rate dT/dt for discrete time intervals.
A quadratic function was fit to these data points to model the cooling rate as a function of temperature with a single root at 
T = 24\textdegree C such that the temperature would stabilize at T = 24\textdegree C by design.
\[
\frac{dT}{dt} = -\left(9.23081\times10^{-6}\right)(T-24)^{2} - \left(7.74304\times10^{-4}\right)(T-24)
\]
(dT/dt in \textdegree C/s, T in \textdegree C; \(R^{2} = 0.9633\)).
