
\section{Derivations for Bounding Method Equations}\label{estimate-permeation-lag-uncertainty-for-uncorrected-long-heating-steps}

The uncertainty of the helium evolution of the encapsulated natural
material during a heating step (\(\omega\)) due to permeation lag
correction can be estimated by calculating the maximum possible helium
residing in the tube between steps, i.e. for the values for
\emph{n\textsubscript{0, }}and \emph{n\textsubscript{t}}: \(n_{0}^{UB}\)
and \(n_{t}^{UB}.\ \)From the permeation lag equation (main text Eq.~\MainEqPermlag), upper bound estimates for
\emph{n\textsubscript{0, }}and \emph{n\textsubscript{t}} bound the
encapsulated material contribution \(\omega\) within:

\begin{equation}
m - n_{0}^{UB} \leq \omega \leq m + n_{t}^{UB}
\label{eq:s6-1}
\end{equation}

Upper bounds for \emph{n\textsubscript{t}} for a given high temperature
experimental step can be calculated if step heats are long enough to
assume steady-state permeation, assuming G and K are known or can be
conservatively estimated. To proceed, one must make the generally
conservative assumption that the production rate of helium within the
ampoule due to degassing of the encapsulated natural sample
(\(\Omega = \frac{\omega}{t}\)) is constant over time at a given
temperature. In reality, \(\Omega\) will decrease over time for helium
loss governed by first order processes \citep{yakubovich2010,wilson1973} or diffusion (except for the case of initially
inhomogeneous distributions, the memory of which is often erased early
in any diffusion experiment: \citealp{shuster2004}). Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-3}, given
below, shows that the amount of helium trapped in the tube (\(n_{t}\))
is proportional to the rate of helium outgassing from the natural
encapsulated sample (\(\Omega\)), so the approximation that this rate is
constant will overestimate the amount of unanalyzed helium remaining in
the ampoule after a high temperature experimental step. The rate of
helium degassing over time will decrease for degassing controlled by
second-order volume diffusion \emph{except} for the minor case of the
alpha-ejection effect at low He-release fractions \citep{farley1996,ketcham2011} or for the more serious effects of radiation
damage annealing \citep{goldsmith2020} and mineral decomposition
\citep{farley2024}. In any case, sequential isothermal steps
immediately reveal when \(\frac{\omega}{t}\) increases over time.

A new first order differential equation like Eq.~\MainEqPermode\, main text, is formulated, now
incorporating a constant source term (\(\Omega\)) to conservatively
approximate the helium influx into the ampoule from the degassing
natural sample.

\begin{equation}
\frac{dn}{dt} = \Omega - \lambda n
\label{eq:s6-2}
\end{equation}

Where \(\Omega = \frac{\omega}{t}\) and \(\lambda = KGRT\). Immediately,
the steady-state quantity of helium atoms (\(n_{ss}\)) present in the
ampoule can be found by solving Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-2} for the steady-state condition
where \(\frac{dn}{dt} = 0\).

\begin{equation}
n_{ss} = \frac{\Omega}{\lambda} = \frac{\omega}{\lambda t}
\label{eq:s6-3}
\end{equation}

Additionally, the helium concentration within the ampoule during the
approach to the steady-state concentration can be solved for by
integrating Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-2} with respect to \emph{t}. First, the integrating
factor for Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-2} is identified: \(I = e^{\lambda t}\), which is
multiplied to both sides to yield Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-4}.

\begin{equation}
e^{\lambda t}\frac{dn}{dt} + e^{\lambda t}\lambda n = e^{\lambda t}\Omega\
\label{eq:s6-4}
\end{equation}

Then both sides are integrated with respect to \emph{t:}

\[\int_{}^{}\left( e^{\lambda t}\frac{dn}{dt} + e^{\lambda t}\lambda n \right)dt = \int_{}^{}{e^{\lambda t}\Omega}dt\]

Because
\({\frac{d}{dt}(ne^{\lambda t}) = e}^{\lambda t}\frac{dn}{dt} + e^{\lambda t}\lambda n\)
by the product rule Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-4} reduces to:

\begin{equation}
n_{t}e^{\lambda t} = \frac{\Omega}{\lambda}e^{\lambda t} + C
\label{eq:s6-5}
\end{equation}

Where \emph{C} is an unknown constant introduced by indefinite
integration. This expression becomes more intuitive when both sides are
divided by the integration factor and by substituting the steady-state
concentration from Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-3}.

\begin{equation}
n_{t} = n_{ss} + Ce^{- \lambda t}
\label{eq:s6-6}
\end{equation}

The integration constant \emph{C} can be solved by enforcing the
condition that \(n_{t}(t = 0) = n_{0}\), leading to:

\begin{equation}
n_{0} = n_{ss} + C
\label{eq:s6-7}
\end{equation}

Solving Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-7} for \emph{C} and substituting into Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-6} yields
Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-8}. Now an inequality is assigned to the equation to reflect the
initial assumption that the isothermal helium outgassing rate
(\(\Omega\)) is constant \emph{or decreasing}.

\begin{equation}
n_{t} \leq \ n_{ss} + \left( n_{0} - n_{ss} \right)e^{- \lambda t}
\label{eq:s6-8}
\end{equation}

For long timescales (i.e. when \(e^{- \lambda t} \approx 0\)), Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-8}
reduces to \(n_{t} \leq \ n_{ss}\). This is effectively true for steps
long enough such that \(t \geq \frac{5}{\lambda}\), for which
\(e^{- \lambda t} \leq 0.007.\) 

To estimate the upper bound on the permeation lag (\(n_{t}^{UB}\)), one
must consider the circumstances that would produce the highest possible
value for \(n_{t}\). Because
\(n_{t}\) scales with $\omega$ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-3}, the upper bound estimate
\(n_{t}^{UB}\) must assume the upper bound estimate for $\omega$, which is
given in Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-1} as \({m + n}_{t}^{UB}\). For cases where $t \geq \frac{5}{\lambda}$, \(\omega^{UB} = {m + n}_{t}^{UB}\) can be substituted into Eq.~\eqref{eq:s6-3} to express
\(n_{t}^{UB}\) in terms of the measured value \emph{m}:

\[
n_{t}^{UB} = n_{ss}(\omega = m + n_{t}^{UB}) = \frac{m + n_{t}^{UB}}{\lambda t}
\]

Solving for \(n_{t}^{UB}\) yields main text Eq.~\MainEqSteadystate : 
% Remap this to in-text equation! eq:steady-state

\begin{equation}
n_{t}^{UB} = \frac{m}{\lambda t - 1}
\label{eq:6}
\end{equation}

\section{Analytical Methods for Hydrothermal Goethite Step Heat Experiment}\label{sec:si-analytical-methods}

\subsection{Helium-in-Goethite Diffusivity Inversions for Bounding Method}\label{helium-in-goethite-diffusivity-inversions}

Apparent values of D/a\textsuperscript{2} for \textsuperscript{3}He and \textsuperscript{4}He were
calculated from times and cumulative measured yields using the equations of \citet{fechtigkalbitzer1966}. 
Lower and upper bounds for diffusivity were calculated 
using the upper bound for the helium contents of the 
ampoules before and after each step, evaluated using \MainEqSteadystate. 
Diffusivity for step $i$ is a function of the cumulative fractional yield for step i 
$\Sigma ^{i}F$ and the cumulative fractional yield of the previous step $\Sigma ^{i-1}F$. The diffusivity
is maximized when the difference between the fractional yields is largest, and minimized when the difference is smallest. 
This difference is maximized for the extreme case where the previous step ended with no permeation lag, and the current step ended 
with the maximum amount of permeation lag $\left(_{SS}^{i}n_t\right)$, whereas the opposite is true for the minimum diffusivity. 


\begin{equation}
\label{eq:diffusivity-bounds}
\begin{gathered}
{}^{UB}D_\omega = D\!\left(\Sigma^{i-1}F_m ,~ \Sigma^{i}F_m + {}_{SS}^{i}n_t\right) \\
{}^{LB}D_\omega = D\!\left(\Sigma^{i-1}F_m + {}_{SS}^{i-1}n_t ,~ \Sigma^{i}F_m\right)
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Helium Isotope Ratio Uncertainty for Bounding Method}\label{helium-isotope-ratio-uncertainty}

The upper and lower bounds of the $\frac{^4He}{^3He}$ isotope ratios for each heating step are taken as the maximum and minimum $\frac{^4He}{^3He}$
isotope ratio calculated out of the 4 hypothetical end-member permeation scenarios, which would have been experienced by both helium isotope systems:

\begin{enumerate}
\item $n_t = 0,\ n_0 = 0$
\item $n_t = n_t^{\mathrm{UB}},\ n_0 = 0$
\item $n_t = n_t^{\mathrm{UB}},\ n_0 = n_0^{\mathrm{UB}}$
\item $n_t = 0,\ n_0 = n_0^{\mathrm{UB}}$
\end{enumerate}

Intuitively, higher differential permeability between the two helium isotopes ($\delta = \frac{K_{3He}}{K_{4He}}$ here) will result in greater isotope fractionation between the measured $\left(\frac{m_{4}}{m_{3}}\right)$ and true $\left(\frac{\omega_{4}}{\omega_{3}}\right)$ ratios.
Furthermore, lower absolute permeabilities will result in stronger permeation lag, additionally producing higher isotope fractionation in the measured value. The \textsuperscript{4}He permeability is thus conservatively taken to be identical to \citet{swets1961}, an anhydrous
literature value which this experiment's own isothermal \textsuperscript{3}He permeation measurements show to underestimate the ampoule's true permeability (Fig.~\MainFigAmpoulePermeability); \textsuperscript{4}He permeability itself could not be independently measured in this experiment. For the purposes of evaluating the measured isotope ratios, the relative \textsuperscript{3}He/\textsuperscript{4}He permeability ($\delta$) is taken to be 1.29, the 2$\sigma$ upper bound of the value measured independently for a helium-soaked water-filled ampoule:
1.10 $\pm$ 0.19 (2$\sigma$). 1.29 is in great excess of realistic values of the relative \textsuperscript{3}He/\textsuperscript{4}He permeability, which is expected to be between 1.06--1.11 for the temperature range relevant to this experiment \citep{jones1953}. These
conservative assumptions taken together guarantee that the calculated upper and lower bounds for the isotope ratios accurately constrain the true value. The upper bounds of the
ampoule contents are calculated using the steady-state approximation of Eq.~\MainEqSteadystate\ (main text \S\MainRefBounding): $n_{ss} = \frac{m}{\lambda t - 1}$. For the four extreme cases listed above, the following equations apply:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \scalebox{1.5}{$\frac{\omega_{4}}{\omega_{3}} = \frac{m_{4}}{m_{3}}$}
\item \scalebox{1.5}{$\frac{\omega_{4}}{\omega_{3}} = \frac{m_{4}+\frac{m_{4}}{\lambda t - 1}}{m_{3}+\frac{m_{3}}{\delta \lambda t - 1}}$}
\item \scalebox{1.5}{$\frac{\omega_{4}}{\omega_{3}} = \frac{m_{4}+\frac{m_{4}}{\lambda t - 1} - \frac{m_4^{i-1}}{\left(\lambda t\right)^{i-1} - 1}}{m_{3}+\frac{m_{3}}{\delta \lambda t - 1} - \frac{m_3^{i-1}}{\delta\left(\lambda t\right)^{i-1} - 1}}$}
\item \scalebox{1.5}{$\frac{\omega_{4}}{\omega_{3}} = \frac{m_{4} - \frac{m_4^{i-1}}{\left(\lambda t\right)^{i-1} - 1}}{m_{3} - \frac{m_3^{i-1}}{\delta\left(\lambda t\right)^{i-1} - 1}}$}
\end{enumerate}

Where $m_{x}^{i-1}$ and $\left(\lambda t \right)^{i-1}$ refer to the measured helium content and $\lambda t$ of the previous step, respectively. $\lambda$ here applies explicitly to \textsuperscript{4}He, whereas $\delta \lambda$ applies to \textsuperscript{3}He. The upper and lower bounds of the isotope ratios are then taken to be the maximum and minimum of these four extreme cases.

\section{Finite Difference Diffusion Model of Cooling Tube}\label{sec:finite-difference-diffusion-model-of-cooling-tube}

One potential concern about the results of the internally measured permeabilities for HT-Win-06-01b is that some additional helium was permeated
during the cooling period after each permeation step. In order to evaluate the significance of 
this effect, a finite difference model was built in python to simulate diffusion, influx, and
outflux of helium across a hollow glass cylinder. The glass
cylinder was given the same dimensions of the ampoule used in the
Win-06-01b hydrothermal experiment, and was subjected to the same
heating and cooling schedule as the hydrothermal experiment's permeation
steps. The tube was charged with 100 arbitrary units of helium atoms at
the end of each experimental step, and the concentration profile for
the new arbitrary charge was initialized at T = 24\textdegree C using the
numerical burn-in procedure described in \S\ref{sec:numerical-methods}. Permeability
values for \textsuperscript{3}He were assumed as the product of the \textsuperscript{4}He permeability
values given by \citet{swets1961} and the \textsuperscript{3}He/\textsuperscript{4}He relative
permeabilities given by \citet{jones1953}.

\subsection{A Parameterized Cooling Rate of the HT-Win-06-01b Ampoule}\label{sec:si-ampoule-cooling-rate}

To simulate cooling, a cooling rate had to be assumed. To most
accurately simulate the cooling of the experimental ampoule, Watlow temperature records
of the experimental ampoule during cooling steps were used to calculate the cooling rate dT/dt for discrete time intervals.
A quadratic function was fit to these data points to model the cooling rate as a function of temperature with a single root at 
T = 24\textdegree C such that the temperature would stabilize at T = 24\textdegree C by design.

\subsection{Numerical Methods}\label{sec:numerical-methods}

A numerical model of diffusive permeation in glass ampoules is invaluable, as it
provides a flexible way to estimate the permeation fraction
\(\left( \frac{m}{n_{0}} \right)\) even when the required assumptions
for the analytical solutions are not met. For example, a numerical
approach can calculate the expected permeation fraction for a
non-isothermal time-temperature history, and it needn't obey the
assumption that the helium concentration profile in the glass is in
diffusive equilibrium with the partial pressure of helium in the
encapsulated gas phase.

Assuming that the ampoule's concentration
profile is radially symmetrical and uniform with height (z), Eq. 5, page 17 of \citet{carslawjaeger1959}, second edition gives the equation for the change in
concentration by diffusion in a cylinder:

\begin{equation}
\frac{dC}{dt} = D\left\lbrack \frac{\partial^{2}C}{\partial r^{2}} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial C}{\partial r} \right\rbrack
\label{eq:s4-2}
\end{equation}

We numerically solve Eq.~\eqref{eq:s4-2} using the Forward-Time Central-Space (FTCS) Finite Difference
Method (FDM) along a radial transect of a model ampoule of arbitrary
geometry for small, discrete time intervals. The system is spatially
discretized into 100 cells along a radial transect, and numerical
stability was ensured by solving for the time interval of a single step,
$\Delta t$, such that \(\frac{D\Delta t}{\Delta r^{2}} = 0.5\), satisfying the
Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) condition.

Helium concentrations along the transect (except for the boundaries,
where r = a and r = b) are updated at each step using an FTCS
discretization of the radially symmetric diffusion equation for a
cylinder.

\begin{equation}
\Delta C_{r} \approx \Delta t\  \bullet D\left\lbrack \frac{C_{r - \Delta r} - 2C_{r} + C_{r + \Delta r}}{\Delta r^{2}} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{C_{r + \Delta r} - C_{r - \Delta r}}{2\Delta r}\  \right\rbrack
\label{eq:s5-0}
\end{equation}

Dirichlet boundary conditions were enforced at the inner and outer
boundaries. The concentration at the model ampoule's outer boundary
(where r = b) was held at 0, while the concentration at the inner
boundary (where r = a) was calculated at each discrete step by
multiplying the solubility (in units of
\(\frac{atoms}{atm \times cm^{3}}\)) by the helium partial pressure
within the ampoule, which can be related to the atomic abundance of helium in the gas
phase using the ideal gas law and the formula for the volume of a
cylinder:

\[C_{He}^{glass} = S(T) \bullet P_{He}^{gas}\]

\[P_{He}^{gas} = \frac{n_{gas}RT}{V_{gas}}\]
\[V_{gas} = \pi a^{2}h\]

\begin{equation}
C_{He}^{glass} = S(T) \bullet \frac{n_{gas}RT}{\pi a^{2}h}
\label{eq:s5-1a}
\end{equation}

For helium permeation out of glass ampoules, the internal pressure is not constant. \(n_{gas}\)
changes after every step and Eq.~\eqref{eq:s5-1a} is reapplied to the inner
boundary cell where r = a. While the helium concentrations in the glass
and in the encapsulated gas phase are both dependent on the length of
the ampoule (\emph{h}), they compensate for one another such that the
absolute atom flux into and out of the ampoule is not a function of
\emph{h}. 

In order to simulate permeation, the atomic abundances of helium within
the gas phases both outside the ampoule (at high vacuum) and inside the
ampoule must be tracked, as do the fluxes between the glass and these
two gas phases. These fluxes were calculated simply as the diffusive
flux in \(\frac{atoms}{s \bullet cm^{2}}\) as is given by Fick's law. 
For spatially discrete cells throughout the ampoule at any
radius except for the boundaries, the diffusive flux can be approximated
with the finite difference:

\begin{equation}
J_{r} = - D\frac{\partial C_{r}}{\partial r} \approx \  - D\frac{C_{r + \Delta r} - C_{r - \Delta r}}{2\Delta r}
\label{eq:s5-3}
\end{equation}

However, Eq.~\eqref{eq:s5-3} cannot be applied at the ampoule's inner wall when r
= a or outer wall when r = b because there is no remaining glass on one
side of the cell. In that case, second order terms from Taylor
expansions centered at \(a + \Delta r\ \)and \(b - \Delta r\) can be
used to better approximate the \(\frac{\partial C_{a}}{\partial r}\) and
\(\frac{\partial C_{b}}{\partial r}\) terms. Letting
\(f(r) = \ \frac{\partial C_{r}}{\partial r}\) and
\(f'(r) = \ \frac{\partial^{2}C_{r}}{\partial r^{2}}\):

\begin{equation}
\resizebox{0.98\linewidth}{!}{$\displaystyle
J_{a} = - D\frac{\partial C_{a}}{\partial r} \approx \  - D\left( \frac{\partial C_{a + \Delta r}}{\partial r} + \frac{\partial^{2}C_{a + \Delta r}}{\partial r^{2}}\Delta r \right) \approx - D\left( \ \frac{C_{a + 2\Delta r} - C_{a}}{2\Delta r} + \frac{C_{a} - {2C_{a + \Delta r} + C}_{a + 2\Delta r}}{2\Delta r^{2}}\Delta r \right)\
$}
\label{eq:s5-3a}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\resizebox{0.98\linewidth}{!}{$\displaystyle
J_{b} = - D\frac{\partial C_{b}}{\partial r} \approx \  - D\left( \frac{\partial C_{b - \Delta r}}{\partial r} - \frac{\partial^{2}C_{b - \Delta r}}{\partial r^{2}}\Delta r \right) \approx - D\left( \frac{C_{b} - C_{b - 2\Delta r}}{2\Delta r} - \frac{C_{b} - {2C_{b - \Delta r} + C}_{b - 2\Delta r}}{2\Delta r^{2}}\Delta r\  \right)\
$}
\label{eq:s5-3b}
\end{equation}

The helium contents in the measurable (external, high vacuum) reservoir
and the inner encapsulated gas phase are updated every step using fluxes
determined from Eq.~\eqref{eq:s5-3b} and \eqref{eq:s5-3a} respectively. To convert these
area-normalized fluxes into absolute atomic abundances, these fluxes are
multiplied by the time increment ($\Delta t$) and the surface area of the
cylinder at the outer and inner walls respectively.

Simulations require an initial helium concentration profile in the glass
appropriate for a fresh charge of helium at a given temperature. A closed-form analytical solution 
for this profile is relatively easy to derive, but such a derivation is of little significance to this work and is too lengthy to include here. 
Therefore, the steady state concentration profile is retrieved numerically: the inner and outer boundary conditions are held
fixed while the model iterates Eq.~\eqref{eq:s5-0} until the
concentration profile no longer changes. This produces the same result as solving Eq.~\eqref{eq:s4-2}
analytically for the steady-state profile.

\section{Goethite dehydroxylation: thermodynamic model}\label{sec:gtsi-thermo}

The following sections give the full formulation, data and sensitivity tests behind the
condensed analysis of goethite dehydroxylation in the main text (\S\MainRefMethodsKinetics\ and
\S\MainRefDiscussionConversion).

Goethite breaks down to hematite by the dehydroxylation reaction
\begin{equation}
  2\,\alpha\text{-FeOOH} \;\rightleftharpoons\; \alpha\text{-Fe}_2\text{O}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O}_{(\text{fluid})},
  \label{eq:gtsi_rxn}
\end{equation}
whose Gibbs energy change, written per mole of hematite formed, is
\begin{equation}
  \Delta G_r(T,P) = G_{\mathrm{hem}}(T,P) + G_{\mathrm{w}}(T,P) - 2\,G_{\mathrm{goe}}(T,P) + \Delta G_{\mathrm{surf}} .
  \label{eq:gtsi_dGr}
\end{equation}
Goethite is stable where $\Delta G_r>0$ and hematite plus water where $\Delta G_r<0$.

\textit{Gibbs energies of the solids.} The Gibbs energy of a phase at $(T,P)$ follows from
integrating $dG = -S\,dT + V\,dP$ from the reference state $(T_r,P_r) = (298.15~\mathrm{K}, 1~\mathrm{bar})$,
first in temperature at $P_r$ and then in pressure at $T$. At $P_r$,
$H(T) = H(T_r) + \int_{T_r}^{T}C_p\,dT$ and $S(T) = S(T_r) + \int_{T_r}^{T}(C_p/T)\,dT$, so
$G(T,P_r) = H(T) - T S(T)$. Adding $\int_{P_r}^{P}V\,dP$, and treating the solids as incompressible
with no thermal expansion, gives
\begin{equation}
  G_i(T,P) = \Delta_f H_i^{0} + \int_{T_r}^{T} C_{p,i}\,dT
  \;-\; T\left[S_i^{0} + \int_{T_r}^{T}\frac{C_{p,i}}{T}\,dT\right]
  \;+\; V_i\,(P-P_r),
  \label{eq:gtsi_Gsolid}
\end{equation}
where we have set $H(T_r) = \Delta_f H_i^{0}$, the enthalpy of formation from the elements, and
$S(T_r) = S_i^{0}$, the third-law entropy. This is the ``apparent'' Gibbs energy of formation
\citep{berman1988}; see also \citet{helgeson1978} and \citet{anderson2005}. It differs from
the true Gibbs energy of formation,
$\Delta_f G_i = \Delta_f H_i - T(S_i - \sum_e n_{e,i} S_e)$, by the term $T\sum_e n_{e,i}S_e$,
where $n_{e,i}$ is the number of atoms of element $e$ in phase $i$ and $S_e$ the entropy of the
element in its reference state. For any balanced reaction $\sum_i \nu_i n_{e,i} = 0$ for every element,
so these terms cancel in $\Delta G_r$. The incompressible approximation affects $\Delta G_r$
through $\Delta V_s (P-P_r)$ with $\Delta V_s = V_{\mathrm{hem}} - 2V_{\mathrm{goe}} = -11.5$~cm$^3$,
i.e.\ at most a few kJ below 1~GPa; compressibility and thermal expansion change $V_i$ by
$\lesssim1\%$ over the conditions considered and are neglected.

Goethite heat capacity uses the Berman--Brown form \citep{berman1985} refined by
\citet{majzlan2003},
\begin{equation}
  C_p = k_0 + k_1 T^{-1/2} + k_2 T^{-2} + k_3 T^{-3},
\end{equation}
which integrates to
\begin{align}
  \int_{T_r}^{T} C_p\,dT &= k_0 (T-T_r) + 2k_1\left(T^{1/2}-T_r^{1/2}\right) \notag\\
    &\quad - k_2\left(T^{-1}-T_r^{-1}\right) - \tfrac{1}{2}k_3\left(T^{-2}-T_r^{-2}\right), \\
  \int_{T_r}^{T} \frac{C_p}{T}\,dT &= k_0 \ln\frac{T}{T_r} - 2k_1\left(T^{-1/2}-T_r^{-1/2}\right) \notag\\
    &\quad - \tfrac{1}{2}k_2\left(T^{-2}-T_r^{-2}\right) - \tfrac{1}{3}k_3\left(T^{-3}-T_r^{-3}\right).
\end{align}
Hematite uses a Maier--Kelley form, $C_p = a + bT + cT^{-2}$, with
\begin{align}
  \int_{T_r}^{T} C_p\,dT &= a(T-T_r) + \tfrac{1}{2}b\left(T^2-T_r^2\right) - c\left(T^{-1}-T_r^{-1}\right), \\
  \int_{T_r}^{T} \frac{C_p}{T}\,dT &= a\ln\frac{T}{T_r} + b(T-T_r) - \tfrac{1}{2}c\left(T^{-2}-T_r^{-2}\right).
\end{align}
% TODO: cite the source of the hematite Maier-Kelley coefficients (Kelley-type fit,
% cf. Robie & Hemingway 1995, USGS Bull. 2131); Majzlan et al. used Berman (1988). Differences are < 1 J/K/mol at 298 K.

\textit{Water.} Water is evaluated with the IAPWS-95 equation of state \citep{wagner2002},
implemented in CoolProp \citep{bell2014}, which is valid for liquid, vapor and supercritical
water to 1~GPa. In the same apparent convention,
\begin{equation}
  G_{\mathrm{w}}(T,P) = \Delta_f H_{\mathrm{w}}^{0} + \left[h(T,P) - h(T_r,P_r)\right]
  - T\left[S_{\mathrm{w}}^{0} + s(T,P) - s(T_r,P_r)\right],
  \label{eq:gtsi_Gw}
\end{equation}

input data. We use the enthalpies and entropies of \citet{majzlan2003} refined by mathematical
programming against the phase-equilibrium brackets of \citet{voigt1981}.

As a check, at 298.15~K and 1~bar with liquid water, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_dGr}--\eqref{eq:gtsi_Gw}
give $\Delta G_r = -0.26$~kJ: hematite plus liquid water is favored by only 0.13~kJ per mole of
FeOOH. This is well inside the $\pm1.4$~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$ uncertainty that \citet{majzlan2003}
assign to the goethite--hematite free-energy difference; the two assemblages are essentially
balanced at ambient conditions. We carry this uncertainty ($\pm2.8$~kJ per reaction as written)
as bounding curves on the phase diagram.

\textit{Surface energy.} A crystal of finite size carries an excess free energy at its
surface in addition to the free energy of its bulk \citep{gibbs1878}. For a population of
particles of fixed size, this excess is $\gamma A$, where $\gamma$ is the surface energy
(J\,m$^{-2}$) and $A$ the total surface area. Both the bulk and surface terms scale with the
amount of material at fixed particle size, so the surface contribution can be written per mole,
$G = G_{\mathrm{bulk}} + \gamma A_m$, with $A_m$ the surface area per mole. $A_m$ grows as $1/d$,
so the term is negligible for coarse crystals and significant for nanoparticles
\citep{navrotsky2008}. For the reaction as written (one mole of hematite from two moles of
goethite),
\begin{equation}
  \Delta G_{\mathrm{surf}} = \gamma_{\mathrm{hem}} A_{m,\mathrm{hem}} - 2\,\gamma_{\mathrm{goe}} A_{m,\mathrm{goe}},
  \label{eq:gtsi_dGsurf}
\end{equation}
where $A_{m,\mathrm{hem}}$ is per mole of Fe$_2$O$_3$ and $A_{m,\mathrm{goe}}$ per mole of FeOOH.

A size must be chosen for each phase. We assume pseudomorphic dehydroxylation, in which hematite
inherits the external envelope of the parent goethite, so that both phases share the same
external surface area per mole of Fe. This is also the basis of the stability diagrams of
\citet{navrotsky2008}. For spherical goethite grains of diameter $d$ this shared area is
\begin{equation}
  A_{\mathrm{Fe}} = \frac{6\,V_{\mathrm{goe}}}{d}\quad\text{per mole of Fe}.
\end{equation}
Goethite has one Fe per formula unit, so $A_{m,\mathrm{goe}} = A_{\mathrm{Fe}}$. Hematite has two,
so $A_{m,\mathrm{hem}} = 2A_{\mathrm{Fe}}$. Substituting into Eq.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_dGsurf},
\begin{equation}
  \Delta G_{\mathrm{surf}} = \gamma_{\mathrm{hem}}\,(2A_{\mathrm{Fe}}) - 2\,\gamma_{\mathrm{goe}}\,A_{\mathrm{Fe}}
  = 2A_{\mathrm{Fe}}\left(\gamma_{\mathrm{hem}} - \gamma_{\mathrm{goe}}\right).
  \label{eq:gtsi_equalarea}
\end{equation}
The factor of two counts the two moles of Fe that pass through the reaction. It multiplies both
terms because both phases carry the same area per Fe. For $d=10$~nm,
$A_{\mathrm{Fe}} = 6\times20.881\times10^{-6}/10^{-8} = 1.253\times10^{4}$~m$^2$\,mol$^{-1}$, and
with hydrated surface energies
$\Delta G_{\mathrm{surf}} = 2\times1.253\times10^{4}\times(0.75-0.60) = +3.76$~kJ.
The corresponding values are $+0.38$~kJ at 100~nm and $+0.04$~kJ at 1~\textmu m. A positive
$\Delta G_{\mathrm{surf}}$ stabilizes goethite. The result depends on the choice of basis: at
equal diameter the same hydrated surface energies destabilize fine goethite
(\S\ref{sec:gtsi_supp_basis}).

We use hydrated surface energies. They are referenced to liquid water, so they apply wherever the
surfaces are in equilibrium with water at or near saturation, which includes all of the
hydrothermal experiments. Anhydrous surface energies apply only to surfaces that have lost their
chemisorbed water, i.e.\ under vacuum.

\textit{Phase diagram.} The phase diagram (Fig.~\ref{fig:gtsi_PT}) is computed by solving
$\Delta G_r(T,P) = 0$ (Eqs.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_dGr}--\eqref{eq:gtsi_equalarea}) numerically for
$P = P_{\mathrm{H_2O}}$ at each temperature, using Brent's method on $\log_{10}P$ between
$10^{-12}$ and $10^{4}$~bar, with the solids at the fluid pressure. Where no root exists below the
melting curve of ice, the curve is truncated.

\textit{Driving force and the role of water.} The quantity that matters for kinetics is the
driving force for hematite formation, $\Delta\mu \equiv -\Delta G_r^{0}$ (per mole of Fe$_2$O$_3$).
Water controls $\Delta\mu$ through its own chemical potential, because it is a product of
reaction~\eqref{eq:gtsi_rxn}. $G_{\mathrm{w}}$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_dGr} is the chemical potential of
H$_2$O at the prevailing water fugacity. It depends on the partial pressure of water, not on the
total pressure. An inert gas, or any other mechanical load, acts only through the solid-volume
term $\Delta V_s(P-P_r)$, which contributes $-1.15$~J\,bar$^{-1}$ and slightly favors the denser
hematite. At 40~bar that is 46~J, three orders of magnitude smaller than the effect of 40~bar of
water. The water-buffered step-heating technique therefore stabilizes goethite primarily by
mass action on the bulk reaction. Surface hydration is a second-order correction that matters
only for nanometer grains (Eq.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_equalarea}).

\subsection{Stability and driving force at the experimental conditions}

The thermodynamic model above places the goethite--hematite--water
boundary in $\log P_{\mathrm{H_2O}}$--$T$ space (Fig.~\ref{fig:gtsi_PT}). Above the
saturation pressure, water is liquid, the reaction volume
$\Delta V_r = V_{\mathrm{hem}} + V_{\mathrm{w}} - 2V_{\mathrm{goe}} \approx +6.6$~cm$^3$ is small,
and the boundary becomes nearly vertical in $\log P$--$T$ space. Bulk goethite meets the
liquid--vapor curve at 18~$^\circ$C, 100~nm goethite at 28~$^\circ$C and 10~nm goethite at
108~$^\circ$C. At 25~$^\circ$C, bulk goethite requires $P_{\mathrm{H_2O}}\approx10^{2.6}$~bar
(compressed liquid). By contrast, 100~nm goethite is stable above $10^{-1.52}$~bar (96\%
relative humidity) and 10~nm goethite above $10^{-2.11}$~bar (24\% relative humidity). Bulk
goethite is therefore metastable at the conditions of every experiment considered here, in
water or in vacuum.

\subsection{Sensitivity of the size effect to the surface-area basis}
\label{sec:gtsi_supp_basis}

The sign of the surface stabilization depends on how the surface areas of the two phases are
related. At equal area per mole of Fe (Eq.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_equalarea}), goethite is stabilized
whenever $\gamma_{\mathrm{hem}}>\gamma_{\mathrm{goe}}$. If both phases are instead spheres of the
same diameter $d$, then $A_i = 6V_i/d$ and
\begin{equation}
  \Delta G_{\mathrm{surf}} = \frac{6}{d}\left(\gamma_{\mathrm{hem}}V_{\mathrm{hem}} - 2\gamma_{\mathrm{goe}}V_{\mathrm{goe}}\right),
\end{equation}
which stabilizes goethite only if
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{hem}}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{goe}}} > \frac{2V_{\mathrm{goe}}}{V_{\mathrm{hem}}} = \frac{2\times20.881}{30.274} = 1.379 .
\end{equation}
Hematite is denser and carries less surface per Fe at equal size. For hydrated surfaces,
$\gamma_{\mathrm{hem}}/\gamma_{\mathrm{goe}} = 0.75/0.60 = 1.25 < 1.379$, so fine goethite is
destabilized: at 10~nm,
$\Delta G_{\mathrm{surf}} = (6/10^{-8})(0.75\times30.274 - 2\times0.60\times20.881)\times10^{-6} = -1.41$~kJ.
For anhydrous surfaces, $1.90/0.91 = 2.09 > 1.379$ and $\Delta G_{\mathrm{surf}} = +11.7$~kJ at
10~nm. Table~\ref{tab:gtsi_basis} compares the two bases. The equal-area basis corresponds to
pseudomorphic conversion and underlies the main-text calculations. The equal-diameter basis is
more appropriate where hematite grows as separate crystals, as in fluid-mediated conversion
(\S\ref{sec:gtsi-pigment}). There the surface term slightly favors hematite, but it is
negligible at micrometer sizes.

\section{Kinetic analysis of the vacuum step heats}\label{sec:gtsi-vacuum}

Two published vacuum step heats of irradiated Win-06-01b are reanalyzed here. The experiment of
\citet{farley2024} (hereafter KAF) used 1~h steps from 70 to 330~$^\circ$C, including isothermal
repeats at 210, 230 and 240~$^\circ$C. Its extraction line was open to a liquid-nitrogen-cooled
charcoal trap, so evolved water did not accumulate, and the water released in each step was
measured by capacitance manometry. The experiment of \citet{monteiro2017} (hereafter HDM) used
a different schedule: prograde to 238~$^\circ$C, retrograde to 180~$^\circ$C, then prograde
again, with 0.5~h steps above 238~$^\circ$C, to 498~$^\circ$C. Water could accumulate in the
HDM line. For each experiment, the cumulative fraction of $^3$He released after step $i$ is
\begin{equation}
  F_i = \frac{\sum_{j\le i} {}^3\mathrm{He}_j}{\sum_{\mathrm{steps}} {}^3\mathrm{He} + {}^3\mathrm{He}_{\mathrm{fusion}}} .
\end{equation}

We describe conversion by a first-order rate law,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\alpha}{dt} = k(T)\,(1-\alpha),
  \qquad
  k(T) = A\exp\!\left(-\frac{E_a}{RT}\right),
  \label{eq:gtsi_F1}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is the fraction of goethite converted. For a powder of many grains, first-order
kinetics is the limit in which each grain converts rapidly once a hematite nucleus forms, so
that the conversion rate is set by the nucleation rate per untransformed grain
\citep{mampel1940}. $k$ is then a per-grain nucleation rate. We assume $^3$He is released when
its host grain converts, except for a fraction $\phi$ that is inherited by the hematite
product:
\begin{equation}
  F = (1-\phi)\,\alpha .
  \label{eq:gtsi_Fphi}
\end{equation}
For a schedule of isothermal steps of duration $\Delta t_j$ at $T_j$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_F1}
integrates to
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_i = 1 - \exp\!\left[-A\sum_{j\le i} m_j\,\Delta t_j \exp\!\left(-\frac{E_a}{RT_j}\right)\right],
  \label{eq:gtsi_F1_cum}
\end{equation}
where $m_j = s$ for the two KAF steps run with the trap isolated (\S\ref{sec:gtsi-vacuum}) and $m_j=1$
otherwise. Inverting Eq.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_F1} step by step gives an apparent rate constant for every
step that is independent of any fitted parameter except $\phi$:
\begin{equation}
  k_i = -\frac{1}{\Delta t_i}\ln\frac{1-\alpha_i}{1-\alpha_{i-1}},
  \qquad \alpha_i = \frac{F_i}{1-\phi}.
  \label{eq:gtsi_stepk}
\end{equation}
For comparison, the diffusion parameter of each step is obtained with the same
\citet{fechtigkalbitzer1966} inversion for a sphere that is applied to the hydrothermal
experiment (\S\ref{helium-in-goethite-diffusivity-inversions}),
\begin{equation}
  \frac{Dt}{a^2} =
  \begin{cases}
    \dfrac{1}{\pi^2}\left[2\pi - \dfrac{\pi^2}{3}F - 2\pi\sqrt{1-\dfrac{\pi F}{3}}\,\right], & F \le 0.85,\\[2ex]
    -\dfrac{1}{\pi^2}\ln\!\left[\dfrac{\pi^2}{6}(1-F)\right], & F > 0.85,
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:gtsi_FK}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation*}
  \left(\frac{D}{a^2}\right)_i = \frac{(Dt/a^2)_i - (Dt/a^2)_{i-1}}{\Delta t_i}.
\end{equation*}
For the hydrothermal experiment, $F_i$ and $D/a^2$ are always taken from the bounding-method
reduction (main text \S\MainRefBounding): the cumulative release at the end of step $i$ is
bounded by the measured cumulative $^3$He and by that value plus $n_{t,\mathrm{UB}}$, and the
central value is their midpoint. As in the main text, steps without defined bounds and
steps with less than 0.1~pccSTP of measured $^3$He are excluded.

All steps are plotted, but fits use only KAF steps 5--23
(185--285~$^\circ$C) and HDM steps 8--28 (199--338~$^\circ$C). KAF steps 1--4 and HDM steps 2--7
(below $\sim$180~$^\circ$C) release a small ($<2\%$), separate low-temperature component
(\S\ref{sec:gtsi-vacuum}). Later steps sit on the post-conversion plateau, where
$1-\alpha\to0$ and Eq.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_stepk} becomes controlled by the choice of $\phi$. The KAF
steps run with the trap isolated are excluded from the Arrhenius regression.

\subsection{Results}

Fitting Eqs.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_Fphi}--\eqref{eq:gtsi_F1_cum} to the cumulative KAF $^3$He
release by least squares gives $E_a = 122.9$~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$, $\ln A = 18.67$ ($A$ in s$^{-1}$),
$\phi = 0.112$ and $s = 0.46$, with an rms misfit in $F$ of 0.0085. The step rate constants of
both experiments (Eq.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_stepk}) fall on a single Arrhenius line
(main text Fig.~\MainFigGtArrhenius{}a). This includes the HDM retrograde steps, which a first-order
process requires: its rate depends only on $T$ and on the amount of goethite remaining. An
unweighted least-squares fit of $\ln k_i$ against $1/T_i$ for the 38 steps in the fitting window
gives
\begin{equation}
  E_a = 121.4 \pm 4.4~\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}},
  \qquad
  \ln A = 18.08 \pm 1.05
  \label{eq:gtsi_line}
\end{equation}
(KAF alone $134.9\pm6.1$; HDM alone $116.3\pm5.6$~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$), in agreement with the
cumulative fit. At 250~$^\circ$C this gives $k = 5.4\times10^{-5}$~s$^{-1}$, or 18\% conversion
per hour. The result does not depend on the fitting window: fitting all steps instead gives
$E_a = 122.7$~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$ and the same ranking of models (\S\ref{sec:gtsi_supp_mech}).
Thermogravimetric studies of goethite dehydroxylation report somewhat higher apparent activation
energies, $154\pm15$ to $169\pm8$~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$, for a reaction controlled by a two-dimensional
phase boundary, with a different mechanism at early stages and low temperature \citep{goss1987}.
% TODO: compare conditions (heating in air/N2 vs vacuum; sample grain size) before finalizing.

In the diffusion representation (main text Fig.~\MainFigGtArrhenius{}b) the two experiments do not
collapse. HDM's retrograde steps between 180 and 220~$^\circ$C plot about three natural-log units
above HDM's own prograde steps at the same temperatures. Volume diffusion from grains that are
being progressively depleted cannot produce this, whether from a single domain or multiple
domains: retrograde steps must fall on or below the prograde trend. $D/a^2$ also turns over
above $\sim$275~$^\circ$C, where release stalls at the plateau.

The parameters fitted to KAF alone predict the independent HDM schedule with an rms misfit in
$F$ of 0.017 over the fitting window (Fig.~\ref{fig:gtsi_vac_fit}c,d). The prediction reproduces
the main release and follows the retrograde--prograde cycle within about a factor of two on
individual steps. A single-domain spherical diffusion model fits KAF worse (rms 0.046) and
predicts HDM worse (rms 0.053). It also predicts $F\to1$ by $\sim$300~$^\circ$C, whereas both
experiments stall at $F\approx0.89$. Within a single schedule, fits alone cannot discriminate
between kinetic mechanisms (\S\ref{sec:gtsi_supp_mech}). The second schedule is what gives the
comparison its power.

During the main vacuum release (KAF, $0.04<F<0.88$), the step $^4$He/$^3$He ratio stays within
0.95--1.10 of the bulk ratio. Grains therefore release their integrated inventory as they
convert, rather than releasing a diffusive, rim-weighted component.

In both experiments release stalls at $F\approx0.89$. The fitted $\phi=0.112$ is the same for KAF
and HDM. In HDM, the remaining $\sim$11\% is released slowly between 340 and 500~$^\circ$C and on
fusion. We interpret this fraction as $^3$He inherited by the hematite product and subsequently
released by the much slower diffusion out of hematite. Single-domain goethite diffusion cannot
produce a plateau. The conversion and the plateau correspond to the ``Stage 2'' and
``Stage 3'' release of \citet{farley2024}, whose transition near $F\approx0.88$ is independent of
heating schedule.

The water evolved in each KAF step tracks the $^3$He release closely
(Fig.~\ref{fig:gtsi_vac_fit}b). Water leads $^3$He slightly at 230~$^\circ$C and lags it by
$\sim$0.1 between 250 and 280~$^\circ$C. Fitting Eq.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_F1_cum} to the water data alone
gives $E_a = 91.5$~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$ (rms 0.033). The larger misfit is consistent with
water adsorbed before dehydroxylation ($\sim$1\% of the total, released below 200~$^\circ$C). It
is also consistent with systematically low manometer readings on the first step of each
measurement day, and with normalizing the water to the 330~$^\circ$C total. % TODO: confirm the day-boundary artifact with the line operator
Water release thus provides an independent measure of conversion that is consistent with the
$^3$He record.

In two KAF steps the trap was isolated and evolved water accumulated in the line. At
230~$^\circ$C the step rate constant fell to $0.33$ times the mean of its neighbors at the same
temperature, and at 240~$^\circ$C to $0.22$ times. The rate recovered fully when the trap was
restored. Water and $^3$He were suppressed together. A diffusion process would not respond to
the partial pressure of water. We use these two steps in \S\ref{sec:gtsi-nucleation} to calibrate
the nucleation model.

Below $\sim$180~$^\circ$C both vacuum experiments release $^3$He 0.5--3 orders of magnitude
faster than the extrapolated conversion rate (Fig.~\ref{fig:gtsi_delta}). This component amounts
to less than 2\% of the $^3$He and is not conversion. We attribute it to diffusion from the least
retentive domains (main text \S\MainRefDiscussionMDD).

\subsection{Kinetic mechanisms and the limits of a single heating schedule}
\label{sec:gtsi_supp_mech}

Solid-state rate laws take the form $d\alpha/dt = A\exp(-E_a/RT)\,h(\alpha)$. For any heating
schedule this integrates to
\begin{equation}
  g(\alpha) \equiv \int_0^{\alpha}\frac{d\alpha'}{h(\alpha')} = x,
  \qquad
  x = A\sum_j \Delta t_j\exp\!\left(-\frac{E_a}{RT_j}\right).
\end{equation}
We tested the mechanisms in Table~\ref{tab:gtsi_mech_forms}, including spherical diffusion on the
same footing. Under vacuum, the driving-force factor $1-\exp(-\Delta\mu/RT)$ is unity to better
than $10^{-9}$, so $x$ is simply an Arrhenius-weighted time. Diffusion and conversion then
differ only in the shape $\alpha(x)$ and in the fitted $E_a$.

\section{Hydrothermal tests of diffusion versus conversion}\label{sec:gtsi-hydro}

The hydrothermal experiment heated an aliquot of the same goethite in distilled water, with
much of the sample submerged in the liquid. The schedule rose from 170 to 230~$^\circ$C, cycled
down to 175~$^\circ$C, rose to 250~$^\circ$C, cycled down to 225~$^\circ$C and back to 250~$^\circ$C,
where three 5~h steps preceded a 170.8~h step, and ended with a step at 240~$^\circ$C (442.8~h in
total). Only 14.7\% of the $^3$He was released. Under the vacuum kinetics of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_line}, the same schedule would have converted the sample completely. Two tests,
both applied to the bounding-method reduction (main text \S\MainRefBounding), show that the
release follows diffusion rather than conversion.

\textit{Test 1: history dependence through temperature cycles.} A line through the first
prograde sweep (200--230~$^\circ$C) serves as the reference. The two retrograde steps of the
first cycle with adequate signal, at 225 and 215~$^\circ$C, fall 0.45 and 0.86 natural-log units
below it in $\ln(D/a^2)$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_FK}; central values). The upper bound of each step
still falls 0.18 and 0.47 log units below a line fitted to the lower bounds of the first sweep.
On the next prograde sweep, the 220~$^\circ$C step sits 0.58 log units (at least 0.32) below its
first-sweep value (main text Fig.~\MainFigGtArrhenius{}b). The low-signal steps of the first
retrograde sweep (205--175~$^\circ$C, and 200--210~$^\circ$C on the following prograde sweep;
excluded from all plots) sit lower still, by 1.3--2.1 log units. In the last cycle, retrograde and
prograde values at 230--245~$^\circ$C agree within 0.07--0.45 log units, and their bounds
overlap at every temperature. The Stage 1 regression over this interval (cumulative yield
$\geq$7\%) gives $E_a = \ChTwoEa\pm\ChTwoEaErr$~kJ\,mol$^{-1}$
(\S\ref{helium-in-goethite-diffusivity-inversions}). This is the signature of depleting fast,
low-retentivity domains in the first cycle, with diffusion dominated by more retentive domains
thereafter. A first-order conversion rate depends only on temperature and on the fraction
converted, which is almost unchanged at $F\approx0.04$. It would return retrograde steps to the
prograde line, as the vacuum HDM experiment does in the reaction representation
(main text Fig.~\MainFigGtArrhenius{}a).

\textit{Test 2: the 250~$^\circ$C holds.} The two processes project differently through a long
isothermal step. Diffusion slows as the diffusion front moves inward, whereas first-order
conversion proceeds at a nearly constant rate while $\alpha\ll1$. We calibrate both on the three
5~h steps at 250~$^\circ$C and project through the 170.8~h step. For first-order conversion,
$-\ln(1-\alpha)$ grows linearly in time at fixed $T$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_F1}), so
\begin{equation}
  F(t) = (1-\phi)\left[1-(1-\alpha_0)\,e^{-kt}\right],
  \qquad \alpha_0 = \frac{F_0}{1-\phi},
\end{equation}
with $k$ fitted to the three calibration steps and $F_0 = 0.0795$ (bounds 0.0793--0.0798) the
release before the first of them. For diffusion from a sphere, the Fourier number $Dt/a^2$ grows
linearly in time at fixed $T$:
\begin{equation}
  F(t) = 1 - \frac{6}{\pi^2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^2}
  \exp\!\left[-n^2\pi^2\left(\left(\frac{Dt}{a^2}\right)_0 + \frac{D}{a^2}\,t\right)\right],
\end{equation}
where $(Dt/a^2)_0$ follows from $F_0$ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_FK} and $D/a^2$ is fitted to the
calibration steps. Both models reproduce the three calibration steps (main text Fig.~\MainFigGtHold{}).
Over the long step they diverge. Diffusion projects $F = 0.149$ against an observed 0.146
(bounds 0.1460--0.1463). First-order conversion projects 0.173, overpredicting the release in
that step by 46\%: $(0.173-0.087)/(0.146-0.087) = 1.46$. Calibrating instead on the lower- and
upper-bound series gives 0.148--0.150 for diffusion and 0.172--0.174 (a 44--47\%
overprediction) for first-order conversion. We also tested the contracting-geometry and Avrami
models of \S\ref{sec:gtsi_supp_mech}, which fit the vacuum data less well. Calibrated the same
way, they project 0.174--0.212, and none changes the result.

Plotted as first-order rate constants (main text Fig.~\MainFigGtArrhenius{}a), the hydrothermal steps sit
1.3--2.8 orders of magnitude (median 2.4) below the vacuum line. They show what a first-order
process forbids: retrograde steps 0.7--1.5 natural-log units below prograde steps at the same
temperature, and a falling $k$ during the 250~$^\circ$C holds. In the diffusion representation
(main text Fig.~\MainFigGtArrhenius{}b) the same steps form a coherent, history-dependent array. The
vacuum experiments behave like conversion and the hydrothermal experiment behaves like
diffusion. The conversion rate expected at water saturation is too small, by tens of orders
of magnitude, to contribute to the observed release (\S\ref{sec:gtsi-nucleation}).

\section{Nucleation model and calibration of the effective interfacial energy}\label{sec:gtsi-nucleation}

\textit{Nucleation barrier.} In classical nucleation theory \citep{gibbs1878,volmer1926,becker1935},
reviewed by \citet{christian2002}, \citet{kashchiev2000} and, for mineral systems,
\citet{deyoreo2003} and \citet{lasaga1998}, a spherical hematite nucleus of radius $r$ has free
energy
\begin{equation}
  \Delta G(r) = -\frac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}\,\frac{\Delta\mu}{V_{\mathrm{hem}}} + 4\pi r^{2}\gamma,
\end{equation}
where $\Delta\mu$ is the driving force per mole of Fe$_2$O$_3$ (Table~\ref{tab:gtsi_dmu}),
$V_{\mathrm{hem}}$ the molar volume and $\gamma$ the energy of the interface surrounding the
nucleus. The first term is the free energy released by transforming the nucleus volume. The second
is the cost of creating its interface. Setting $d\Delta G/dr=0$ gives the barrier height
\begin{equation}
  \Delta G^{*} = \frac{16\pi\,\gamma^{3}\,V_{\mathrm{hem}}^{2}}{3\,\Delta\mu^{2}} .
  \label{eq:gtsi_barrier}
\end{equation}
Written per formula unit, $V_{\mathrm{hem}}\to V_{\mathrm{hem}}/N_A$ and $\Delta\mu\to\Delta\mu/N_A$, so
Avogadro's number cancels and Eq.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_barrier} is directly the barrier per nucleus in
joules. In the first-order limit of \S\ref{sec:gtsi-vacuum}, the rate constant is a nucleation
rate,
\begin{gather}
  k = A\exp\!\left(-\frac{E_a}{RT}\right)\exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta G^{*}}{k_B T}\right), \notag\\
  \frac{\Delta G^{*}}{k_B T} = \frac{C\gamma^{3}}{T\,\Delta\mu^{2}},
  \qquad
  C \equiv \frac{16\pi V_{\mathrm{hem}}^{2}}{3k_B} = 1.112\times10^{15}~\mathrm{m^6\,K\,J^{-1}\,mol^{-2}} ,
  \label{eq:gtsi_knuc}
\end{gather}
where $A\exp(-E_a/RT)$ describes the atomic mobility and $\exp(-\Delta G^{*}/k_BT)$ the probability of
forming a critical nucleus. The barrier scales as $\gamma^3/\Delta\mu^2$. Between vacuum and water
saturation, $\Delta\mu$ falls tenfold and the barrier rises by the factor in the last column of
Table~\ref{tab:gtsi_dmu}: about 100 at 250~$^\circ$C and nearly 180 at 150~$^\circ$C.

\textit{An upper bound on the vacuum barrier.} The vacuum conversion rate is observed, so the
barrier that controls it cannot exceed what the fastest conceivable prefactor allows. In
transition-state theory \citep{eyring1935} no elementary step is faster than $k_BT/h$, which is
$1.09\times10^{13}$~s$^{-1}$ at 250~$^\circ$C. If every one of the $N$ formula units in a grain were a
nucleation site attempting at this frequency, the per-grain rate could not exceed
$N(k_BT/h)\exp(-\Delta G^{*}/k_BT)$. A 1~\textmu m goethite sphere contains
$N = (\pi d^3/6)/(V_{\mathrm{goe}}/N_A) = 1.5\times10^{10}$ formula units. Requiring this ceiling to
reach the observed $k_{\mathrm{vac}} = 5.4\times10^{-5}$~s$^{-1}$ at 250~$^\circ$C (Eq.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_line})
gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\Delta G^{*}_{\mathrm{vac}}}{k_BT} \le \ln\frac{N\,k_BT/h}{k_{\mathrm{vac}}}
  = \ln\frac{1.5\times10^{10}\times1.09\times10^{13}}{5.4\times10^{-5}} = 63 .
  \label{eq:gtsi_ceiling}
\end{equation}
A tenfold larger grain raises the bound by only $\ln 1000 = 7$. Real prefactors are smaller, so the
true barrier is lower still.

\textit{Nucleation must be internal.} The surface energies of \citet{navrotsky2008} fail this
bound by a wide margin. If the nucleus were bounded by a free, anhydrous hematite surface
($\gamma = 1.90$~J\,m$^{-2}$, $\Delta\mu = 106.0$~kJ at 250~$^\circ$C), Eq.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_knuc} would give
$\Delta G^{*}/k_BT = 1.3\times10^{3}$. Nucleation on a goethite surface lowers this. For a nucleus
shaped as a spherical cap on a flat substrate, the barrier is reduced by the purely geometric
factor
$f(\theta) = (2+\cos\theta)(1-\cos\theta)^2/4 \le 1$, where the contact angle $\theta$ is set by the
balance of interfacial tensions at the triple junction (Young's equation),
$\cos\theta = (\gamma_{\mathrm{goe}} - \gamma_{\mathrm{hem|goe}})/\gamma_{\mathrm{hem}}$
\citep{volmer1926,turnbull1950}. Even with a hematite--goethite interfacial energy of zero,
$\cos\theta \le 0.91/1.90 = 0.48$, $f\ge0.17$ and $\Delta G^{*}/k_BT\ge2.2\times10^{2}$. No
non-negative interfacial energy brings nucleation on free surfaces within the bound of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_ceiling}. Hematite must therefore nucleate internally, where the only new
interface is between hematite and goethite.

This agrees with the known mechanism. Goethite dehydroxylates topotactically: the hematite
inherits the oxygen framework and orientation of the parent, and the pseudomorph develops pores
and twinned hematite domains \citep{limadefaria1963,watari1979,watari1983,cudennec2005}. Interfaces
of this kind are coherent or semicoherent. In the general classification of \citet{porter2009},
developed for metals, coherent interfaces have energies up to $\sim$0.2~J\,m$^{-2}$ and semicoherent
interfaces 0.2--0.5~J\,m$^{-2}$, compared with 0.5--1~J\,m$^{-2}$ for incoherent ones. For this system
specifically, density-functional calculations give 0.24~J\,m$^{-2}$ for the prismatic twin boundaries
between hematite domains formed by dehydrating goethite \citep{saito2016}. We are not aware of a
measured or calculated energy for the goethite--hematite interface itself. In what follows,
$\gamma$ is replaced by an effective interfacial energy $\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}$, calibrated from the
data. It absorbs the geometry of internal nucleation and is a property of the sample.

\textit{Calibrating $\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}$ from the trap-isolated steps.} Isolating the trap
raised $P_{\mathrm{H_2O}}$ in the line and lowered $\Delta\mu$, and the isothermal rate fell by a factor
$s$ (\S\ref{sec:gtsi-vacuum}). With $A$ and $E_a$ unchanged, Eq.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_knuc} gives
\begin{equation}
  \ln\frac{1}{s} = \frac{C\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}^{3}}{T}\left(\frac{1}{\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{off}}^{2}} - \frac{1}{\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{on}}^{2}}\right)
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \gamma_{\mathrm{eff}} = \left[\frac{T\ln(1/s)}{C\left(\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{off}}^{-2} - \Delta\mu_{\mathrm{on}}^{-2}\right)}\right]^{1/3}.
  \label{eq:gtsi_gamma_cal}
\end{equation}
The driving forces are evaluated as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{on}}$ and $\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{off}}$ are bulk driving forces,
  $-\Delta G_r$ from Eqs.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_dGr}--\eqref{eq:gtsi_Gw} with $\Delta G_{\mathrm{surf}} = 0$, at the
  step temperature and with the solids at vacuum.
  \item They differ only through the chemical potential of water: $\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{on}}$ is
  evaluated at $P_{\mathrm{H_2O}} = 10^{-8}$~atm and $\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{off}}$ at the water pressure
  reached with the trap isolated (below). At these pressures (at most 20~Pa), IAPWS-95 and the
  ideal gas agree, and
  $\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{on}} - \Delta\mu_{\mathrm{off}} = RT\ln(P_{\mathrm{off}}/P_{\mathrm{on}})$, which is
  32--41~kJ for the pressures in Table~\ref{tab:gtsi_gamma}.
  \item Surface terms are omitted in both. The barrier of an internal nucleus depends on the free
  energy per volume of material transformed, not on the external surface of the host grain. The
  grain or domain size of the natural sample is also not characterized. The external surface
  energy of the grain enters the overall thermodynamic stability (\S\ref{sec:gtsi-thermo}),
  but we do not include it in the barrier. The sensitivity to this choice is small
  (\S\ref{sec:gtsi_supp_barrier}).
\end{enumerate}
We bound the water pressure with the trap isolated by assuming that all evolved water was in the
vapor, distributed uniformly through an apparatus of volume $V_{\mathrm{app}}$ at 298~K:
\begin{equation}
  P_{\mathrm{H_2O}} = \frac{(m_{\mathrm{w}}/M_{\mathrm{w}})\,RT}{V_{\mathrm{app}}} .
\end{equation}
With $m_{\mathrm{w}} = 0.027$~mg at 230~$^\circ$C and 0.016~mg at 240~$^\circ$C ($1.50$ and
$0.89~\mu$mol), and $190\le V_{\mathrm{app}}\le600$~mL, Table~\ref{tab:gtsi_gamma} gives the pressures
and the resulting $\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}$. The pressures are end-of-step values; we also evaluate half
of each as a time average for steady accumulation. Pressure enters only through $\ln P$ in
$\Delta\mu$ and then through a cube root, so a threefold uncertainty in volume changes
$\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}$ by only about 10\%. The two steps agree within 15\%, and we adopt
\begin{equation}
  \gamma_{\mathrm{eff}} = 0.16\pm0.03~\mathrm{J\,m^{-2}} ,
\end{equation}
within the range expected for coherent and semicoherent interfaces. The corresponding barriers at
250~$^\circ$C are 0.5--1.3~$k_BT$ under vacuum and 52--129~$k_BT$ at saturation. Under vacuum the
barrier is negligible and the Arrhenius term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_line} sets the rate; at saturation
the barrier dominates.

\textit{Translation to water saturation.} For the same sample, with the same internal interface
and the same mobility term, only $\Delta\mu$ changes between vacuum and saturation:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{k_{\mathrm{sat}}}{k_{\mathrm{vac}}} = \exp\!\left[-\frac{C\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}^{3}}{T}\left(\frac{1}{\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{sat}}^{2}} - \frac{1}{\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{vac}}^{2}}\right)\right],
  \label{eq:gtsi_translate}
\end{equation}
with both driving forces evaluated as in the calibration (bulk, IAPWS-95 water at $P_{\mathrm{sat}}$ and
at $10^{-8}$~atm). For $\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}} = 0.14$, 0.16 and 0.19~J\,m$^{-2}$, this ratio is
$7\times10^{-23}$, $9\times10^{-34}$ and $5\times10^{-56}$ at 250~$^\circ$C, and $5\times10^{-43}$,
$7\times10^{-64}$ and $2\times10^{-106}$ at 200~$^\circ$C.

How large a barrier would still allow detectable conversion follows from the same kinetics. Over
the 442.8~h ($1.59\times10^{6}$~s) hydrothermal experiment, converting even 1\% of the sample at
250~$^\circ$C requires $k_{\mathrm{sat}}t\gtrsim0.01$, i.e.
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\Delta G^{*}_{\mathrm{sat}}}{k_BT} \lesssim \ln\frac{k_{\mathrm{vac}}\,t}{0.01}
  = \ln\frac{5.4\times10^{-5}\times1.59\times10^{6}}{0.01} = 9 ,
\end{equation}
far below the calibrated 52--129~$k_BT$. Applying Eq.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_translate} step by step to the
hydrothermal schedule, with $k_{\mathrm{vac}}$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_line}, the predicted conversion is
\begin{equation}
  \alpha \approx \sum_i k_{\mathrm{vac}}(T_i)\,\frac{k_{\mathrm{sat}}}{k_{\mathrm{vac}}}(T_i)\,\Delta t_i
  = 10^{-20.6},\ 10^{-31.5},\ 10^{-53.7}
\end{equation}
for the three values of $\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}$. Even the most permissive calibration,
$\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}} = 0.084$~J\,m$^{-2}$, gives $\alpha = 6\times10^{-4}$. That value would require
0.1~bar of water in the line, far above the volume constraint. If the observed release came from
conversion, it would imply $\alpha = F/(1-\phi) = 0.147/0.888 = 0.17$.

\textit{Residuals.} Figure~\ref{fig:gtsi_delta} shows the difference between each observed step
rate constant (Eq.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_stepk}) and the rate predicted for its setting:
\begin{gather}
  \Delta\log_{10}k_i = \log_{10}k_i^{\mathrm{obs}} - \log_{10}k^{\mathrm{pred}}(T_i), \notag\\
  k^{\mathrm{pred}} =
  \begin{cases}
    k_{\mathrm{vac}}(T) & \text{vacuum},\\
    k_{\mathrm{vac}}(T)\,\dfrac{k_{\mathrm{sat}}}{k_{\mathrm{vac}}}(T;\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}) & \text{water-saturated}.
  \end{cases}
\end{gather}
The vacuum steps in the fitting window scatter about zero with an rms of 0.15 (KAF) and 0.21
(HDM) orders of magnitude. The trap-isolated steps sit 0.30 and 0.55 orders below. The hydrothermal
steps sit 30--62 orders of magnitude above the saturated prediction for
$\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}=0.16$~J\,m$^{-2}$, and 20--41 and 53--105 orders above for 0.14 and
0.19~J\,m$^{-2}$. Far more $^3$He is released in water than decomposition, as constrained by the vacuum
experiments, could supply. The hydrothermal release must be controlled by diffusion.

A rate law linear in the driving force cannot produce this contrast. Such a law has
$k\propto1-\exp(-\Delta\mu/RT)$, which at saturation is 0.80, 0.87 and 0.91 of the vacuum value at
150, 200 and 250~$^\circ$C. That predicts only a 10--20\% slowdown, far less than the trap-isolated
steps and hydrothermal data show. Suppression requires a barrier that grows as $\Delta\mu^{-2}$.

\textit{Assumptions.} This treatment is a consistent physical explanation of the data, not a
proof. It rests on four assumptions.
(i) The slowdown during the trap-isolated steps acts through $\Delta\mu$. If adsorbed water instead
slowed dehydroxylation by blocking reactive sites, these steps would not calibrate
$\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}$.
(ii) The effective interface energy, $A$ and $E_a$ are the same in vacuum and in water. The
mechanism is internal nucleation in both settings, and only the chemical potential of water
changes.
(iii) The barrier depends on the bulk driving force. Including the surface terms of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_equalarea} for grain sizes down to 10~nm raises the predicted hydrothermal
conversion by about eight orders of magnitude, to $\alpha\approx10^{-23}$
(\S\ref{sec:gtsi_supp_barrier}).
(iv) Only solid-state conversion is modeled. Conversion by dissolution of goethite and
precipitation of hematite from the fluid is not described by Eq.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_translate}
(\S\ref{sec:gtsi-pigment}).
The comparison is valid only between experimental conditions on the same sample. The calibrated
$\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}$ absorbs the sample's microstructure and should not be transferred to other
goethites.

\subsection{Nucleation barrier ratio and the calibration of $\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}$}
\label{sec:gtsi_supp_barrier}

From Eq.~\eqref{eq:gtsi_barrier}, the ratio of barriers at water saturation and under vacuum at
the same temperature is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\Delta G^{*}_{\mathrm{sat}}}{\Delta G^{*}_{\mathrm{vac}}}
  = \left(\frac{\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{vac}}}{\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{sat}}}\right)^{2}
    \left(\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{sat}}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{vac}}}\right)^{3}.
\end{equation}
The prefactors, the temperature and the molar volume cancel. The first factor follows from the
thermodynamic data alone (Table~\ref{tab:gtsi_dmu}; 99 at 250~$^\circ$C for bulk goethite). The
second is 1 if nucleation occurs at the same internal interface in both settings. It is
$(0.75/1.90)^3 = 1/16$ in the extreme case that the relevant interface is a free hematite
surface, hydrated at saturation and anhydrous under vacuum. Figure~\ref{fig:gtsi_barrier_ratio}
shows the ratio between these bounds for bulk and 10~nm goethite. Even at the lower bound, the
saturated barrier is about six times the vacuum barrier at 250~$^\circ$C. Because the barrier
appears in an exponent, that is still a difference of many orders of magnitude in rate.

\section{Hydrothermal treatment of synthetic goethite pigment}\label{sec:gtsi-pigment}

A qualitative test of fluid-mediated conversion was carried out on a commercial yellow iron-oxide
pigment composed of acicular goethite crystals $\sim$1~\textmu m long.
% TODO: add the pigment product name/grade and supplier.
These experiments were intended to give a rapid, qualitative answer, and many experimental
details (exact temperature histories, run durations, and water-to-solid ratios for individual
ampoules) were not recorded. At least five large silica glass ampoules were each loaded with
pigment and an excess of water and fused shut at ambient pressure. They were prepared far
less carefully than the step-heat ampoule of main text \S\MainRefMethodsGoethite; their larger
size made them much easier to handle. The ampoules were heated in an oven without temperature
control: the temperature was monitored and the heater voltage adjusted by hand to keep it
between 200 and 250~$^\circ$C, and the ampoules were held for hours, typically about a day.
Reaction was assessed by color (yellow goethite, red hematite), and representative unreacted and
reacted products were imaged in secondary electron mode (as in main text \S\MainRefMethodsSEM).
% TODO: confirm SEM instrument/sample preparation for the pigment images (SE2, 10 kV, Feb 2024).

Fluid-mediated conversion does occur in some materials. In the qualitative experiments on
acicular synthetic goethite pigment described above, heated with excess water at
200--250~$^\circ$C for about a day, the outcome was all or nothing. Roughly equal proportions of
ampoules were completely reacted (red), completely unreacted (yellow), or contained both
reacted and unreacted material. In the mixed ampoules, the red and yellow portions were entirely
isolated from one another, separated by gas bubbles. Unreacted pigment retains its acicular habit
(Fig.~\ref{fig:gtsi_pigment}b). The reacted product is instead composed of equant to platy crystals
$\sim$0.5--1~\textmu m across (Fig.~\ref{fig:gtsi_pigment}a), interpreted as hematite from its color
and habit. The change of shape rules out pseudomorphic dehydroxylation. It indicates dissolution of
goethite and nucleation and growth of hematite from the fluid, a pathway outside the solid-state
model of \S\ref{sec:gtsi-nucleation}.

The distribution of outcomes is itself diagnostic. Conversion either went to completion within
a fluid-connected volume or did not occur at all, even where reacted and unreacted pigment shared
the same ampoule and thermal history. This is the behavior of a nucleation-limited process:
once hematite nucleates, the reaction proceeds rapidly to completion, and where it does not
nucleate, goethite persists for the full run. The fluid-mediated pathway is therefore governed,
like the solid-state pathway, by the difficulty of forming a hematite nucleus rather than by the
rate at which hematite grows.

Grain size and bulk thermodynamics do not explain why these blades reacted when the natural
goethite did not. At 1~\textmu m, the surface contribution to $\Delta G_r$ is only
$\sim$0.04~kJ, and the bulk driving force at saturation is the same 6--11~kJ for both materials
(Table~\ref{tab:gtsi_dmu}). The most likely difference is the fluid itself. Commercial iron-oxide
pigments are commonly produced by precipitation from iron-salt solutions. They can carry
residual soluble salts, such as sulfate, and may be surface-treated. Such contaminants would
change the pH and ionic strength of the water they are heated in, and with them the solubility
of Fe(III). The dissolution--precipitation pathway is limited by how much iron the fluid can
carry, so a modest increase in Fe(III) solubility could turn a negligible reaction into a
visible one. Differences in residue loading or in the handling of individual ampoules would also
explain why the outcome was not reproducible. The natural goethite, by contrast, was heated in
distilled water, where near-neutral Fe(III) solubility is very low.
% TODO: check the pigment product data sheet (grade, surface treatment, soluble-salt content, aqueous pH).

We conclude that water-saturated conditions suppress solid-state conversion of goethite, but
they do not by themselves exclude fluid-mediated conversion. Whether that pathway operates
depends on the solution chemistry. For the natural sample in distilled water,
\S\ref{sec:gtsi-hydro} shows that the release is diffusive; a nucleation-limited, all-or-nothing
reaction would also be expected to release helium abruptly rather than in the smooth,
history-dependent manner observed. For samples whose chemistry is less well characterized, the
absence of conversion should be verified directly, for example by post-run X-ray diffraction or
electron microscopy.
