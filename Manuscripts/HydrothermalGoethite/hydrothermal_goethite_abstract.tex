U-Th/He geochronology of goethite (FeOOH) dates weathering and ferruginization directly, 
but interpreting a goethite date requires knowing how the mineral retains radiogenic helium, 
which has proven difficult to measure. Recent work revealed that goethite decomposes to hematite 
and water during vacuum step heating, and helium release records dehydration kinetics rather than 
helium migration through stable goethite. Goethite heated in vacuum develops pervasive contraction cracks visible with SEM, 
but this evidence of dehydration is absent in goethite heated at the same temperature under hydrothermal conditions. 
Thus, goethite step heats under hydrothermal conditions are expected to be less influenced by experimentally induced dehydration, 
and more likely to record helium loss in nature. We report the first helium step-heat experiment on goethite 
under hydrothermal conditions, made possible by sealing irradiated goethite and liquid water inside a pure 
silica glass ampoule, which retains water at up to 85\,bar while transmitting helium to the vacuum system. Permeation through the ampoule wall delays measurement of helium released by the sample. We quantify this lag with a 
first-order permeation model and remove it either by measuring the ampoule's residual helium with low-temperature 
permeation steps or, for long steps where the effect is comparatively minor, by bounding it by assuming the helium flux through the ampoule and
sample are at steady state. Under this correction, encapsulated Durango apatite reproduces its published Arrhenius array, establishing that the ampoule's contribution to the signal can be separated from the sample's. 

Proton-irradiated natural goethite sample Win-06-01, previously step heated in vacuum, was
held in water vapor saturated conditions over 170--250\textdegree C for 40 days through multiple prograde and retrograde step heat cycles. 
Apparent helium diffusivities are approximately seven natural log units below those measured on the same sample 
in vacuum and its delta plot follows 
multiple diffusion domain systematics. Above 7\% cumulative \textsuperscript{3}He yield the array is linear, 
with an activation energy of \ChTwoEa\ $\pm$ \ChTwoEaErr\,kJ/mol. A domain with these parameters is nearly quantitatively retentive of helium at 25\textdegree C over billion-year timescales. 
Reanalysis of the vacuum step heats shows that they track first-order conversion of goethite to hematite. A nucleation
model calibrated on them predicts that solid-state conversion at water saturation is slower by tens of orders of magnitude,
and the hydrothermal release instead shows the history and time dependence of diffusion.
We consider hydrothermal step heats to be a more faithful measure of helium migration in goethite than vacuum step heats,
which are known to be compromised by dehydration. Whether these results are free of subtler alteration, and whether laboratory hydrothermal helium loss shares 
the same geometry as loss in nature, remain open questions a single experiment cannot resolve. The method developed herein enables systematic tests of crystallite size, 
chemical composition, and radiation damage that could address them.
